# Silver slag — Skoria Argurou, "helcysma" or "encauma"; Dioscorides 5-101

## *The slag (scoria) left from smelting silver, which Dioscorides says is called helcysma or encauma and has the strength of plumbago, used in black plasters and plasters that form new skin because it is extractive and astringent; the translator's identification is "Silver Slag", and we adopt it with high confidence for the kind of material, none for its composition* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** Metal-smelting slags can carry lead and other toxic metals, and the plasters this slag goes into are litharge-type plasters. This entry is a historical record only. Nobody should make or apply it.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-101, *Skoria Argurou* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10336-10338; the Codex's name-index row in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` gives locus "5-101", class mineral; list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 827; the book index sends the reader to page 794). The whole chapter was read. It is a single sentence (line 10338):

"Scoria [slag] of silver is called helcysma or encauma. It has the same strength as plumbago [5-100]; as a result it is mixed with black plasters, and those for forming new skin, as it is extractive [draws out] and astringent."

The translator's line under the heading reads "Silver Slag" (line 10337). The chapter gives two other names (*helcysma*, *encauma*, as printed), a comparison (the strength of *molubdaina*, 5-100, which the translator renders "plumbago") and an explanation of use: the slag is mixed into "black plasters" and into "those for forming new skin", "as it is extractive [draws out] and astringent". The text names the two qualities as the reason for the use.

**The comparison chapter.** Chapter 5-100 (*Molubdaina*, line 10327) is headed by the translator with several suggestions ("Silver Salts and Gold Salts Plumbago — Molybdos [Greek] — Lead — in this manuscript; Molybdomenite — Selenite of Lead") and marked "TOXIC". It says the best kind "looks like frothy silver", is "yellow, somewhat glittering", that it "is made from silver and gold" and also dug at "Sebastia and Corycum", that it "has properties like lithargyrum [5-102] and [slag] of lead, and is washed and burnt the same way", and that it suits "gentle plasters called lipara" and promotes "the growth of flesh in a wound or sore, and forming new skin". The strength of silver slag is stated to be the same. Chapter 5-102 (*Litharguros*, line 10339) is the litharge chapter; it too is marked "TOXIC" by the translator. The slag's own chapter says nothing about washing or burning.

**Identity.** The translator prints "Silver Slag", and the Greek word *skoria* means slag. We take the material to be the residue of silver smelting. The chapter gives no composition, and which ore or flux was used is not stated. The Codex marks the identification as clear for the kind of material; any statement about its metals is general knowledge and not read in the chapter. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None is stated in the chapter. In general terms, a slag from smelting silver-bearing ore is a glassy or stony residue of silicates and metal oxides, and where silver was won from lead ores it can contain lead oxides and other metals in unknown proportion. The strength the text ascribes ("extractive and astringent") is in line with what is known of metal-oxide plasters in general; no composition is asserted.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Toxic by association.**

- **What the text says it is for.** An ingredient in black plasters and in plasters for forming new skin, because it draws out and is astringent.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no quantity, no formula for the plasters, no way of preparing the slag, no duration.

The Codex does not translate the text's terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend slag of any metal as a wound dressing. Lead-bearing materials applied to broken skin can be absorbed.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives no preparation of the slag itself. The only use named is as an ingredient of "black plasters" and "those for forming new skin" (line 10338). The comparison chapters (5-100, 5-102) give washing and burning methods for plumbago and litharge, but the text does not say that the slag is treated in the same way; a line at the end of the preceding chapters (line 10320) says of lead stone that it "has the same properties as scoria [slag] and is washed in the same way", which is said of another stone and is not applied here. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] As general facts: lead compounds are cumulative poisons that can be absorbed through broken skin and, through the gut, from hand-to-mouth transfer; metal-oxide plasters were a standard dressing in ancient medicine because oxides of lead and zinc are astringent and mildly antiseptic. No modern practice uses smelter slag on wounds.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM).** The chapter prints two qualities: "extractive [draws out] and astringent" (line 10338). No degree is given, and no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality is printed. The qualities of litharge (astringent and softening, cooling, "closing the pores", line 10342) belong to 5-102 and are not carried across.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. The chapter is one sentence of cross-reference: it gives the material's trade names and the reason for its use, which is a small example of the herbal's discipline of attention to what a material is called and what it does.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to take from it for cultivation. It is a waste product of metal-working that the herbal lists as a plaster ingredient, and it is not a recommended practice. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **TOXIC by association.** Slag from smelting may contain lead and other toxic metals. Do not ingest, handle without gloves, or apply to broken skin.
- **Wounds and skin ulcers.** These need medical care.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** Lead exposure is especially harmful; no statement in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Identity of the metals.** Not stated by the text. [USER: verify]
