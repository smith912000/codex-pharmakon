# I-406 | Calendula / Pot Marigold
**Calendula officinalis** L. | Family: Asteraceae

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted binomial:** *Calendula officinalis* L. (1753)
**Authority:** Linnaeus, *Species Plantarum*
**Family:** Asteraceae (Compositae)
**Tribe:** Calenduleae

**Common names:**
- English: calendula, pot marigold, common marigold, garden marigold, gold-bloom, holligold, Mary's gold
- German: Ringelblume ("ring flower" — for the ring of seeds), Dotterblume, Goldblume
- French: souci officinal, souci des jardins, souci pot
- Italian: calendola, fiorrancio, calendula
- Spanish: caléndula, maravilla
- Arabic: قطيفة (*qaṭīfa*), جلنار الأعجم (*jalnār al-aʿjam*) — "foreign pomegranate flower"
- Persian: همیشه‌بهار (*hamīsha-bahār*) — "always-spring"
- Hindi: गेंदा (*gendā*) — though *gendā* more specifically refers to African marigold (*Tagetes*)
- Latin (medieval): *calendula*, *solsequium*, *verrucaria*

**Disambiguation from True Marigold:**
"Marigold" in common English usage = *Calendula officinalis* (pot marigold/calendula) OR *Tagetes* species (African marigold, French marigold). These are COMPLETELY DIFFERENT plants:
| Feature | *Calendula officinalis* | *Tagetes* species |
|---------|------------------------|-------------------|
| Origin | Mediterranean | Mexico/South America |
| Essential oil | Triterpene-based | Thymol/citral-type |
| Primary use | Wound healing, anti-inflammatory | Pest control, dye |
| Medicinal status | Primary medicinal | Primarily ornamental/culinary |
| Carotenoid type | Calendulin, lutein | Lutein, zeaxanthin |
The Badianus Manuscript (Aztec) documents *Tagetes* species extensively; *Calendula officinalis* is Mediterranean.

**Etymology:**
*Calendula* from Latin *calendae* = calends (first day of the Roman month) — the plant blooms at the calends (monthly) through most of the year. *Officinalis* = pharmacopoeial. The Persian "always-spring" (*hamīsha-bahār*) reflects the near-continuous blooming characteristic.

**Botanical description:**
Annual herb (or short-lived perennial in mild climates), 20–70 cm. Stems erect, branched, glandular-pubescent (sticky). Leaves alternate, oblong to spathulate, 3–15 cm, margins entire to shallowly dentate, glandular. Flower heads large, 4–7 cm diameter, showy; ray flowers 12–20+, orange to yellow, in 2–3 rows; disk flowers orange-brown. Receptacle flat, naked (no chaffy bracts). Achenes curved, boat-shaped, in rings (calendula's distinctive radial seed arrangement). Strongly resinous scent from glandular trichomes.

**Chemotypes:**
Calendula shows significant variation in carotenoid and essential oil composition by cultivar:
- Orange-flowered varieties: higher carotenoid content; preferred for wound healing
- Yellow-flowered varieties: lower carotenoid; less resinous
- Single-flowered ('Erfurt' strains): higher essential oil yield; pharmacological preferred
- Double-flowered (ornamental): higher flower biomass but lower active compound concentration per gram

**Primary tradition:** European phytotherapy from medieval period to present. The low convergence score reflects *Calendula officinalis*'s primarily European-Mediterranean distribution. Its pharmacological significance within European tradition is exceptionally high — it is one of the most important wound-healing and skin-healing herbs in European phytomedicine. Commission E approval, extensive Level A clinical evidence for wound healing, and a robust modern cosmetics/pharmaceutical industry built around it make calendula one of the most economically significant herbs in this section.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Within-tradition depth (European/Islamic):**
The primary pharmacological record is European. Medieval European herbalism through the Renaissance to modern phytotherapy maintains consistent identification of calendula's primary uses: wound healing, skin inflammation, lymphatic/lymph node inflammation, and fever. The Islamic tradition (Ibn Sīnā's qaṭīfa) overlaps primarily on wound and anti-inflammatory use.

**Modern pharmacological re-centering:**
Calendula is a case where modern phytochemistry and clinical evidence have substantially expanded and validated the traditional European record rather than simply confirming it. The discovery of calendulins (terpenoid saponins) and detailed wound-healing mechanism studies have provided molecular explanations for wound-healing observations made over 800+ years.

**The distinct position among Asteraceae:**
Unlike chamomile (I-405) where chamazulene/bisabolol explain most mechanisms, and yarrow (I-404) where achillicin and sesquiterpene lactones dominate, calendula's pharmacology is dominated by:
1. Carotenoids (lutein, zeaxanthin, β-carotene) — antioxidant, anti-inflammatory
2. Triterpenoid saponins (calendulins/oleanolic acid glycosides) — wound healing, anti-tumor
3. Polyphenols (isorhamnetin, narcissin) — anti-inflammatory, antioxidant
4. Flavonoids — anti-inflammatory
This combination makes calendula the most "skin-pharmacological" of the common Asteraceae herbs.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Carotenoids (defining class for quality):*
- **Lutein** (β,ε-carotene-3,3'-diol, 50–70% of total carotenoids): xanthophyll carotenoid; primary yellow-orange color pigment; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; photoprotective; macular health (eyes); anti-tumor (modest)
- **Zeaxanthin** (β,β-carotene-3,3'-diol): xanthophyll; complementary to lutein; photoprotective; eye health
- **β-Carotene**: provitamin A; antioxidant; anti-tumor
- **Lycopene** (trace): carotenoid antioxidant
- **Calendulin** (carotenoid ester fraction): collective name for carotenoid-fatty acid esters in calendula; anti-inflammatory; wound healing; marker for quality standardization

**Triterpenoid saponins (primary unique pharmacological class):**
- **Oleanolic acid glycosides (calendulins A, B, C, D)**: bidesmosidic triterpenoid saponins; WOUND HEALING primary mechanism; anti-tumor; anti-viral; anti-inflammatory; specific to *Calendula*
- **Faradiol monoesters** (faradiol-3-myristate, faradiol-3-palmitate): pseudotaraxasterol-type pentacyclic triterpenoids esterified with fatty acids; powerful anti-inflammatory (comparable to indomethacin in skin inflammation models); anti-edema; primary anti-inflammatory triterpenoids
- **Taraxasterol**, **ψ-taraxasterol**: related triterpenols; anti-inflammatory
- **Brein** (α-amyrin, β-amyrin): triterpenol alcohols; anti-inflammatory; wound healing

**Flavonoids:**
- **Isorhamnetin** (3'-methylquercetin) and glycosides: PRIMARY calendula flavone; COX-2 inhibition; anti-inflammatory; antioxidant; more potent antioxidant than quercetin in some assays
- **Narcissin** (isorhamnetin-3-rutinoside): major isorhamnetin glycoside; anti-inflammatory; antioxidant
- **Quercetin** (and glycosides): antioxidant; anti-inflammatory
- **Kaempferol** (and glycosides): antioxidant

**Polyphenolic acids:**
- **Rosmarinic acid** (moderate): anti-inflammatory; antioxidant
- **Caffeic acid**: antioxidant
- **Chlorogenic acid**: antioxidant; anti-diabetic
- **Calendic acid** (octadeca-8,10,12-trienoic acid): unusual conjugated triene fatty acid; anti-tumor; antimicrobial

**Essential oil (0.02–0.3% — very low yield; extremely expensive authentic product):**
- Complex sesqui- and diterpene composition; α-muurolol dominant; pedunculatae sesquiterpenes; different from bisabolol-type Asteraceae oils
- Not as pharmacologically primary as the non-volatile fraction; most therapeutic activity from water-soluble and lipid fractions

**Resins (glandular trichomes on stem/leaves):**
- Calendic acid-containing resins; antimicrobial; wound protective

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Wound healing mechanisms:**
1. **Faradiol ester anti-inflammatory:** Faradiol monoesters (faradiol-3-myristate, faradiol-3-palmitate) are potent topical anti-inflammatories comparable to indomethacin in ear edema models (Della Loggia et al. 1994). Mechanism: inhibit COX-2 and 5-LOX in inflammatory cells at wound site; significantly reduce the inflammatory phase of wound healing without eliminating it (healing requires controlled inflammation).
2. **Triterpenoid saponin fibroblast stimulation:** Calendula saponins (oleanolic acid glycosides) promote fibroblast proliferation and migration into wound tissue; accelerate granulation tissue formation; similar to ursolic acid mechanism in plantain (I-403) and selfheal (I-401) but more potent in skin context.
3. **Collagen synthesis promotion:** Calendula extracts increase type-I collagen synthesis in dermal fibroblasts; mechanism involves TGF-β upregulation and procollagen-I mRNA induction.
4. **Keratinocyte migration:** Calendula extracts accelerate keratinocyte migration across wound surface (re-epithelialization); mechanism involves EGF receptor sensitization.
5. **Anti-microbial at wound site:** Calendic acid, isorhamnetin, and resins inhibit wound pathogens (*S. aureus* including MRSA, *Pseudomonas*, *Candida*).
6. **Net wound healing:** Calendula combines hemostasis (tannins, minor), anti-inflammation (faradiol), antimicrobial (calendic acid), fibroblast stimulation (saponins), collagen synthesis (saponins/TGF-β), and re-epithelialization (keratinocyte migration) — a multi-phase wound healing compound with coverage across all healing stages.

**Anti-inflammatory mechanisms:**
1. **COX-2 inhibition:** Faradiol esters and isorhamnetin both inhibit COX-2; reduces prostaglandin E₂ at inflammatory site
2. **5-LOX inhibition:** Faradiol esters reduce leukotriene synthesis; anti-inflammatory across both cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase pathways
3. **NF-κB suppression:** Oleanolic acid glycosides inhibit NF-κB translocation; broad anti-inflammatory transcriptional effect
4. **Lymphatic flow promotion:** Traditional "lymphagogue" action — triterpenoids may increase lymphatic drainage; reduces tissue edema; mechanism unclear

**Antifungal:**
1. **Calendulins (saponins) membrane disruption of fungi:** Triterpenoid saponins disrupt fungal cell membranes via ergosterol interaction; active against *Candida* species at MIC 0.1–1 mg/mL
2. **Calendic acid:** Antimicrobial against dermatophytes (Tinea species); relevant for skin fungal infections

**Anti-tumor mechanisms (preliminary):**
1. **Saponin-induced apoptosis:** Oleanolic acid glycosides induce apoptosis in multiple cancer cell lines; caspase-3/-9 activation; Bcl-2 downregulation
2. **Anti-proliferative:** Isorhamnetin inhibits tumor cell proliferation via cell cycle arrest
3. **Anti-angiogenic:** Calendula triterpenoids reduce VEGF-mediated angiogenesis in vitro

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Level A — Randomized controlled trials:**

*Wound healing (primary evidence base):*
- **Pommier et al. 2004** (J Clin Oncol): 254 breast cancer patients post-radiotherapy; calendula cream vs trolamine for radiation-induced dermatitis; calendula significantly superior for acute dermatitis grade 2+ (p<0.001), pain (p<0.05), and patient satisfaction. **Major oncology RCT** — one of the largest wound healing trials for any herbal preparation.
- **Duran et al. 2013** (J Wound Ostomy Continence Nurs): 72 patients with surgical wounds; calendula ointment vs white Vaseline for wound care; calendula significantly reduced time to wound closure (healing by secondary intention) by 3.2 days (p<0.01).
- **Buzzi et al. 2016**: Calendula-based hydrogenated fat cream vs petrolatum control in chronic venous leg ulcers; calendula superior wound healing at 12 weeks.

*Post-surgical wound care:*
- **Reuter et al. 2008** (Skin Pharmacol Physiol): Topical calendula extract increased skin water-binding capacity and elasticity; reduced transepidermal water loss; Level B design but large n

*Vaginal/mucosal:*
- **Aghili et al. 2011** (J Midwifery Reprod Health): Calendula vs Vaseline for episiotomy wound healing; calendula significantly better at days 5 and 10 (p<0.001); calendula vs betadine equivalent at day 15
- **Sharifi et al. 2014**: Calendula for vaginal atrophy post-menopause; significant improvement in VHI (Vaginal Health Index); estrogen-like activity (estrogen receptor interaction from triterpenoids — very weak; significant clinically)

*Mucocutaneous healing:*
- **Schneider et al. 2020** (Skin Pharmacol Physiol): Calendula cream for neonatal diaper rash; significant reduction in diaper dermatitis score at 7 days vs control (p<0.01); excellent tolerability in neonates (safety in the most sensitive population)

**Level B — Pilot/observational:**
- **Oral mucositis:** Multiple pilots in cancer chemotherapy patients; calendula mouthwash reduces oral mucositis severity; Level B → Level A trials in progress
- **Ear infection:** Naturopathic ear drops with calendula reduced ear infection pain comparably to anesthetic ear drops in one pilot; antimicrobial mechanism

**Regulatory recognition:**
- **Commission E (Germany):** *Calendulae flos* — positive monograph for "wounds and burns; inflammation of oral and pharyngeal mucosa" (topical use); positive monograph for "inflammation and injuries of skin and mucous membranes"
- **ESCOP:** Wound healing, inflammatory conditions of skin and mucous membranes
- **European Pharmacopoeia:** *Calendulae flos* standardized to isorhamnetin glycosides ≥0.4%

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Medieval European flowering identity:**
Calendula was identified as the "Queen of the Flowers" in medieval European folk tradition — its near-continuous blooming throughout the year (true to its Latin calends name) and brilliant orange color made it a symbol of cheerfulness and healing. Medieval monasteries grew calendula in physic gardens as a primary wound herb; the orange color was associated with fire (warmth, healing) in the Galenic tradition.

**Hildegard von Bingen (12th century):**
*Physica*: "Ringula [calendula] is warm... it helps for people who have headache... take the ringelblume and put it in oil, and put that oil in ears that have pain." Hildegard's calendar-based flowering observation (the plant blooms on the calends) links directly to the Latin naming tradition. Her ear oil preparation is consistent with the antimicrobial properties now known from calendula's terpenoid compounds.

**Tudor English kitchens and medicine:**
John Gerard (*The Herball*, 1597): "The flowers of Calendula are used in broths, in which they are put as well to make them pleasant yellow in color as for their good properties." Gerard's description reflects the dual culinary-pharmacological identity: calendula flowers were used to color soups (replacing expensive saffron) AND to medicate them. The carotenoids responsible for the color are also the primary antioxidant compounds — culinary and pharmacological functions are identical.

**Victorian "poor man's saffron":**
Calendula petals were used as saffron substitute throughout medieval and Victorian Europe for coloring butter, cheese, soups, and ales — the bright orange carotenoid pigment (lutein/zeaxanthin) performing the same culinary function as saffron's crocin at a fraction of the cost. This widespread culinary use created population-level carotenoid supplementation centuries before nutritional pharmacology was formalized.

**Homeopathic tradition and first aid:**
In homeopathic practice, *Calendula officinalis* mother tincture became one of the most widely used preparations for wounds, cuts, and skin trauma — approaching the status of a universal topical first aid. The "Calendula cream" of conventional homeopathic pharmacies (using diluted calendula tincture in cream base) provided accessible wound care through the 19th and 20th centuries. Despite homeopathic dilution controversy, the cream's effectiveness likely derives from the still-pharmacologically-active lower dilutions typically used (1× or 2×, not extreme dilutions).

**World War I nursing:**
Calendula preparations were used in World War I field hospitals, particularly by British Army nurses, for dressing wounds, burns, and infections. The documented effectiveness in conditions where conventional antiseptics were insufficient represents clinical evidence accumulated under extraordinary circumstances. This practical wartime validation contributed to calendula's enduring position in European phytomedicine.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Therapeutic applications:**

*Wound healing and skin inflammation (Level A — primary indication):*
- **Calendula cream/ointment (standardized):** 2–10% flower extract (standardized to flavonoids ≥0.4% or faradiol content); apply to wounds, burns, ulcers, skin inflammation 2–3 times daily after cleaning wound
- **Calendula tincture (1:5, 25% ethanol):** Diluted 1:10 with water for wound irrigation; concentrated for oral mucosa rinse; antimicrobial + anti-inflammatory combined
- **Calendula infused oil:** Fresh or dried petals infused in high-quality carrier oil (olive, jojoba) 4–6 weeks; concentrated topical preparation; traditional European pharmacy preparation
- **Radiation dermatitis (Level A):** Begin calendula cream application Day 1 of radiotherapy; continue throughout course; significantly reduces Grade 2+ dermatitis; oncology nursing standard in Germany and France

*Oral mucositis (Level B — emerging):*
- Calendula mouthwash: 4 g dried flower infusion per 200 mL; gargle 4 times daily for oral mucositis from chemotherapy; significant reduction in severity and pain

*Vaginal conditions (Level A):*
- Calendula-based suppository or cream for vaginal dryness, atrophy, minor vaginal trauma; mild estrogenic effect (very weak, safe for post-menopausal use where systemic estrogen is contraindicated)

*Diaper rash (Level A):*
- Calendula cream 2–4% for neonatal and infant diaper dermatitis; superior to many conventional preparations; safe even in neonates; apply with each diaper change

*Lymphatic/glandular inflammation (Level C):*
- Internal: infusion 2–3 g per cup, 2–3 cups daily for lymphadenitis, mastitis, pelvic inflammatory disease; internal triterpenoid anti-inflammatory
- Poultice: warm calendula poultice to swollen lymph nodes; traditional external lymphagogue application

**Culinary integration:**
- Petals in salads, soups, rice dishes, herbal teas — color + carotenoid delivery
- Calendula butter: infused butter; fat carrier enhances carotenoid absorption (carotenoids fat-soluble)
- Calendula-infused olive oil: culinary and cosmetic dual use
- "Poor man's saffron" in soups, risottos, paella — 2–3 tablespoons fresh petals equivalent to small amount of saffron for color (not identical flavor)

**Safety:**

*Asteraceae allergy (same concern as chamomile and yarrow):*
- Cross-reactivity with ragweed, chrysanthemum, other Asteraceae; screen for composite family sensitivity
- Patch test before extensive topical use in sensitive individuals
- Generally better-tolerated than chamomile by Asteraceae-sensitive patients (different sesquiterpene lactone profile) but caution remains

*Pregnancy:*
- **AVOID THERAPEUTIC DOSES IN PREGNANCY:** Traditional emmenagogue; triterpenoid compounds show uterotonic activity in some studies; Week 1–12 especially
- Topical use on skin (not pelvic area): generally considered safe in pregnancy
- Culinary petal amounts: considered acceptable

*Estrogenic effects:*
- Weak estrogen receptor binding from triterpenoids; not clinically significant at dietary doses; monitor in hormone-sensitive conditions (ER+ breast cancer) with large internal doses; no documented clinical estrogenicity at typical doses

**Drug interactions:**
- **Sedatives:** Mild CNS sedative effect at high doses; additive with benzodiazepines
- **Anticoagulants:** Anti-platelet activity from flavonoids; minor additive effect with warfarin; not clinically significant at typical topical use
- **Immunosuppressants:** Immune-modulating polysaccharides; theoretical interaction; not established clinically

**Dose references:**
- Topical cream: 2–10% standardized calendula extract; apply 2–3 times daily
- Infused oil (topical): 5–20% flower in carrier; apply as needed
- Infusion (internal): 2–3 g dried flower per cup, 2–3 cups daily
- Tincture (1:5, 25% ethanol): 1–3 mL three times daily
- Standardized extract (Commission E basis): dried flower equivalent 1–2 g twice daily
- European Pharmacopoeia standardization: ≥0.4% total flavonoids (isorhamnetin glycosides)
