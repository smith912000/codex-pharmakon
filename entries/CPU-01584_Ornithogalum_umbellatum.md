# Ornithogalum umbellatum — Ornithogalon (Dioscorides 2-174)

## *Ornithogalum umbellatum, the first of the names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 2-174 (which also prints Scilla campestris and Bulbus leucanthemus — "Eleven o' Clock Lady, Star of Bethlehem")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 6046) prints three names with two English names. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *2-174. ORNITHOGALON*, heading at line 6044, text at line 6047, in the section of Book Two headed *Lachana: Vegetables* (line 6042). The whole chapter reads: "Ornithogalon has a tender stalk — thin, whitish, about two feet high — with three or four tender slips growing together on the top from which come the flowers, which outwardly seem the colour of herbs but opened they are similar to milk. Between them is a little head (cut-in like cachrys [3-88]) that is baked together with bread (like melanthium [3-93]). The root is bulbose and is eaten both raw and boiled [vegetable]."

The chapter is a plant of food. It describes a thin, whitish stalk about two feet high, three or four "tender slips" at the top, flowers that outside "seem the colour of herbs" and, opened, are "similar to milk", and a "little head" between them which is "cut-in like cachrys" (chapter 3-88 *KAGCHRU*, heading at line 7325) and is baked in bread "like melanthium" (chapter 3-93 *MELANTHION*, heading at line 7364). The root is "bulbose" and "eaten both raw and boiled". The chapter prints no medicinal use, no habitat, no season and no other name.

**The chapters around it.** The chapters on either side are also vegetables. 2-173 *TRAGOPOGON* (line 6032) is the translators' salsify chapter; 2-175 *HUDNON* (line 6048), after it, is the translators' white truffle, whose text (line 6051) says it is "edible eaten either raw or boiled [vegetable]". 2-176 *SMILAX* (line 6052) follows. The Codex carries no statement of those chapters over to 2-174. The same Greek root name returns on the SUGGESTED lines of the squill chapters 5-25 and 5-26, where the translators print *Ornithogalum maritima* among the names of the medicinal squill (names list, lines 798 and 799); those chapters are about a different plant on the translators' lines and are not read into this row.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 2-174 and for the name *ornithogalon* found no existing entry on this chapter. I_871 (*Truffle*) names 2-174 as a neighbouring vegetable chapter in a paragraph on the chapters around the truffle, and quotes the sentence that its root "is eaten both raw and boiled [vegetable]", without treating it as a subject. The separate chapter 2-200, with the name *Ornithogalum luteum*, is treated in I_1147. No entry is duplicated.

### Active Components

The composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, bulbs of the lily family (Liliaceae in the older sense) contain starch and other storage carbohydrates, and some bulbs of related genera are known to contain toxic compounds. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a plant whose bulb and flower-head were eaten. The Codex gives no protocol for it. The identification is the translators', and a wild bulb should not be dug and eaten on the strength of this chapter, since many bulbs are poisonous.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives two preparations, with locus (line 6047): the root "eaten both raw and boiled", and the little head "baked together with bread". It gives no quantity for either. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that a bulb of unknown species should not be eaten raw. The identification is a translators' suggestion, so the chapter does not show that the plant it describes is *Ornithogalum umbellatum*.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to tell the plant (a thin whitish stalk of two feet, three or four slips on the top, flowers green outside and milk-white inside, a little head cut in like *cachrys*). The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a vegetable. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a vegetable, described so that it may be known. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no wild bulb should be eaten on the strength of this entry; many wild bulbs are poisonous. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
