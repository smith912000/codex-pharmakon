# Onosma — Golden Drop, Ratanjot (in trade), Zahr al-lisān (tongue-flower), red dyer's root

## *Onosma alboroseum* Fisch. & C.A.Mey. | Family: Boraginaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM, EP
**Energetic Classification:** Cool and dry, drawing and drying to open sores; a "sealing" astringent-vulnerary of the reddening class
**Training Phase:** Sealing Phase — the outer, wound-closing and skin-restoring work applied topically at the surface

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP):** The Ebers Papyrus records red plant-root pigments and resins among its topical remedies for wounds, burns and skin eruptions, applied in salves and dressings. The red naphthoquinone dyer's-roots of the Boraginaceae — of which *Onosma*, *Alkanna* and *Lithospermum* are the classic Old-World examples — belong to this ancient class of coloured wound-drugs whose visible redness marked them for use on broken and inflamed flesh.

**Assyrian (AT):** The Mesopotamian tablets list red root-dyes used both to colour and to dress the skin; the *Onosma*/alkanet group, gathered on the dry stony hillsides of the Near East, sits within this dual dye-and-medicine tradition where the pigment and the remedy were the same substance.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** Dioscorides describes *onosma* directly — a rough, hairy, low plant whose leaves were reputed, in his characteristically cautious note, to affect a pregnant woman who stepped over or ate them (an early abortifacient-caution rather than an endorsement). He also documents *anchousa* (alkanet), the closely allied red root used to colour and to treat ulcers and burns — the pharmacological heart of the group's topical reputation.

**Avicenna's Successors (SS):** The Unani physicians carried the red Boraginaceous roots as cooling, drying vulneraries for *quruh* (ulcers), burns and skin heat, and used the flowers as a mild cardiotonic and stimulant (*muqawwi*, *mufarrih*) — the "gladdening" flower-drug tradition. The root's colour fixed its role as both cosmetic dye and drawing salve for the surface.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** *Onosma* is the doctrine of the *red seal* — a root whose blood-colour signed it, by the doctrine of signatures, for the treatment of blood and broken skin. Here the symbolism is unusually well-earned: the red is the naphthoquinones **alkannin and shikonin**, enantiomeric pigments with genuine, documented wound-healing, anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial activity. The colour is the medicine. But the same family bond that gives the red gives the danger — the Boraginaceae also carry hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids, so the "gladdening flower" and the red seal are for the surface, not the inside.

### Active Components

- **Naphthoquinone pigments:** alkannin, shikonin and their acyl derivatives (A/S) — the red root pigments responsible for the wound-healing, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial and antitumoral activity of the group.
- **Naphthazarins and related quinones**; onosmins (characteristic glycosidic markers of the genus).
- **Phenolic compounds, flavones, aliphatic ketones and lipids.**
- **⚠️ Pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs)** — the hepatotoxic Boraginaceae signature, the reason internal use is hazardous.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Onosma is the Sealing-Phase topical vulnerary — a red drawing-and-closing agent for the outer surface: wounds, burns, ulcers, and inflamed skin eruptions. In the Kenetica frame it is the final, external gesture that follows clearing and consolidating: the naphthoquinone-rich root oil or salve is laid on the broken surface to reduce inflammation, resist microbes, and support closure. It is used **topically only**. Its historical internal uses (laxative, "blood," cardiotonic-flower) are recorded but are displaced in the Kenetica frame by the pyrrolizidine-alkaloid hazard — the plant earns its place as a surface-sealer, not an internal remedy.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the red-root wound tradition is the best-substantiated of this trio at the level of the active pigments, though the specific species *Onosma alboroseum* is data-sparse and most mechanistic work is on the shared naphthoquinones alkannin/shikonin (also from *Alkanna tinctoria* and *Lithospermum*).

- **Genus review — ethnopharmacology and chemistry.** Kumar, Kumar & Kishore (*Pharmacognosy Reviews*, 2013) review *Onosma* L. (Boraginaceae, ~150 species), documenting naphthoquinones (shikonins, onosmins), naphthazarins, alkaloids, phenolics and flavones, and traditional use for wounds, burns, piles, skin eruptions, and (flowers) as stimulant/cardiotonic — the anchor linking the genus to the wound-and-skin tradition. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4103/0973-7847.120513)
- **Antimicrobial and wound-healing pigments.** Rat et al. (*Frontiers in Pharmacology*, 2023) study alkannin, shikonin and derivatives as Boraginaceae secondary metabolites that "express anti-cancer, wound healing, and antimicrobial activities," characterising their metabolism by bacteria — direct confirmation of the pigments' bioactivity underpinning the red-root tradition. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2023.1244270)
- **Antimicrobial spectrum of A/S.** Rat et al. (*Frontiers in Microbiology*, 2021) confirm alkannin/shikonin possess antimicrobial, anti-tumoral and wound-healing properties, showing Gram-positive bacteria are sensitive to A/S while Gram-negatives resist — a mechanistic basis for the topical antiseptic-wound use. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2021.633488)

**Evidence grade: Traditional, with strong constituent-level support but species-sparse.** The alkannin/shikonin naphthoquinones have robust in-vitro wound-healing and antimicrobial data, and alkannin/shikonin ointments are an established topical class; however, controlled clinical data specific to *Onosma alboroseum* are lacking, and the cited pigment studies largely use *Alkanna*/*Lithospermum* material. Claims should stay at "traditional topical vulnerary supported by naphthoquinone pharmacology."

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Cool and dry; drying, drawing vulnerary for ulcers and burns; flowers *mufarrih*/*muqawwi al-qalb* (gladdening/heart-strengthening) in the flower tradition.
- **TCM analogue (by shared species):** *Lithospermum/Arnebia* red root — **Zi Cao** — is a genuine Chinese materia medica of the same naphthoquinone class, "cool the blood, clear heat, resolve toxin," used topically for macular eruptions, burns and sores. *Onosma* itself is an Old-World cross-reference to this well-attested function.
- **Ayurvedic analogue (trade name):** sold in the *Ratanjot* red-dye complex; provisional *rasa* tikta-kashaya (bitter–astringent), *vīrya* shīta (cooling), used for *rakta* (blood) and skin — a trade/functional cross-reference, not a single attested classical dravya.

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ Pyrrolizidine-alkaloid hepatotoxicity — do NOT take internally.** *Onosma*, like other Boraginaceae, contains hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids that can cause hepatic veno-occlusive disease (sinusoidal obstruction) with cumulative or high exposure. Internal use of the root or herb is not safe; reserve strictly for external/topical application, and prefer purified alkannin/shikonin preparations where possible.
- **Pregnancy and lactation — avoid entirely.** PAs cross the placenta and enter milk and are developmentally/hepatically toxic; Dioscorides' own abortifacient caution echoes this. Avoid even topically in pregnancy and lactation.
- **Apply only to appropriate wounds** and discontinue on any sign of sensitisation; seek medical care for deep, infected, or non-healing wounds.
- **Do not use on large denuded areas** where transdermal absorption of PAs could become systemic.
- Not a substitute for cardiac care despite the traditional "cardiotonic flower" claim.
