# White Lupine — Termis / Lupini / Lupinus / Egyptian Lupin

## *Lupinus albus L.* | Family: Fabaceae (Leguminosae)

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ, AT, DMM, EP, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Hot and dry (1st–2nd degree) — a bitter, "cleansing," incisive pulse in the Galenic-Unani scheme.
**Training Phase:** Clearing / Building bridge — a debittered protein pulse used to nourish, with a traditional cleansing (vermifuge / skin-clearing) edge.

---

### Origin & Tradition

White lupine is one of the oldest cultivated legumes of the Mediterranean and Near East — a large, flat, cream-coloured seed eaten as the pickled snack *lupini* (Italy), *termis/tirmis* (Egypt and the Levant), and prized both as a protein pulse and, in the raw bitter state, as a frankly medicinal drug. Its defining trait is bitterness: the wild and unprocessed seed is loaded with bitter alkaloids that must be leached out by prolonged soaking before the seed becomes food. That single fact — *bitter poison made into food by washing* — organises its entire traditional use.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (SBJ):** Lupin appears among the pulses and bitter drugs of the cuneiform Mesopotamian materia medica, used both as food and in the bitter-drug pharmacopoeia of the region.

**Ebers Papyrus / Egyptian (EP):** As *termis*, lupine is an ancient Egyptian staple and remedy; the debittered seed was food, while the bitter seed and its water were applied to the skin and taken to expel intestinal worms — a use that persisted in Egyptian folk medicine for millennia.

**Dioscorides / *De Materia Medica* (DMM):** Dioscorides describes *thermos* (lupine) explicitly: the bitter meal, taken with honey or applied as a poultice, expels worms and "brings down" parasites; the decoction cleanses the skin, treats eruptions, ulcers, and scabby conditions, and the bitter seed reduces swellings. This is the canonical classical source for lupine as vermifuge and skin-cleanser.

**Avicenna / *Qanun* (AT-adjacent Unani):** In the Unani tradition lupine (*turmus*) is classed as hot and dry, deobstruent and detergent — used to open obstructions of the liver and spleen, kill worms, and clear the skin, echoing Dioscorides.

**Sushruta / Ayurveda (SS):** The Indian tradition uses lupine and related bitter pulses within the bitter/astringent (*tikta-kashaya*) cleansing category — anthelmintic and skin-clearing, consistent with the bitter-alkaloid pharmacology.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Every tradition tells the same story: lupine is a *bitterness that must be tamed*. Soaked and washed, it feeds; unwashed, it purges and kills worms. The symbolism of "cleansing through bitterness" maps precisely onto the chemistry — the quinolizidine alkaloids that make the raw seed bitter and toxic are the same principles behind its anthelmintic and detergent reputation, while removing them by soaking converts the drug into a high-protein food.

### Active Components

- **Quinolizidine alkaloids (QAs)** — chiefly **lupanine**, with **sparteine**, **angustifoline**, and **13α-hydroxylupanine**. These are the bitter, toxic, physiologically active principles; in bitter *L. albus* seed lupanine alone can reach ~1.5% of seed mass.
- **Storage protein** — very high (roughly 35–40% by dry weight), making the debittered seed a valuable pulse protein.
- **Dietary fibre** and **oligosaccharides**.
- **Lupin allergens** (conglutins) — clinically important cross-reactive proteins (see Contraindications).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Lupine is a **dual-natured** agent, and Kenetica treats it as two different substances depending on processing:

- **Debittered seed (food form):** a Building-phase protein pulse — dense, sustaining nourishment, low glycaemic, useful where the goal is to feed tissue without heating the blood excessively.
- **Bitter seed / bitter water (drug form):** a Clearing-phase intervention — the traditional vermifuge and skin-detergent. This form is *incisive and cleansing*, not nourishing, and carries real toxicity; it is a short-course, carefully-dosed traditional use, not a food.

The bridge between the two — the soaking/debittering step — is itself the "training," and is non-negotiable for the food use.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the toxic principle and its risk profile are well characterised. A European Food Safety Authority scientific opinion identifies the quinolizidine alkaloids in lupins as the hazard of concern, taking **sparteine** as the reference compound and a lowest single oral effective dose of **0.16 mg sparteine/kg body weight** as the reference point; it warns that anticholinergic effects and changes in cardiac electrical conduction are the critical acute effects, and that consumers who eat lupin seeds *without a debittering step* — or debittered seeds still high in alkaloid — may be at risk (EFSA CONTAM Panel, *EFSA J*, 2019; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2019.5860)). Analytical work quantifying the alkaloids across seven *Lupinus* species by capillary electrophoresis found lupanine present in all of them, ranging up to **1.47% of seed mass in *L. albus***, confirming that white lupine is among the higher-alkaloid species and underscoring the necessity of debittering (Ganzera, Krüger & Wink, *J Pharm Biomed Anal*, 2010; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpba.2010.05.030)).

**Evidence grade: Well-characterised toxicology; traditional efficacy.** The alkaloid hazard is robustly documented in modern analytical and risk-assessment literature. The traditional vermifuge and skin-clearing uses are historically attested but not validated by modern controlled human trials — graded traditional.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani (Avicenna):** hot and dry (1st–2nd degree); bitter; deobstruent, detergent, anthelmintic.
- **Ayurveda (as a bitter pulse):** *Rasa* — bitter/astringent (*tikta-kashaya*); *Virya* — heating; *Vipaka* — pungent; anthelmintic (*krimighna*), skin-clearing (*kushtaghna*).
- **Dioscoridean:** heating, cleansing, worm-expelling, wound- and skin-detergent.

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ HAZARD — quinolizidine alkaloids.** Bitter (undebittered) lupins contain **lupanine and sparteine**, which produce an **anticholinergic-type toxidrome**: dry mouth, dilated pupils, blurred vision, flushing, tachycardia, tremor, confusion, and — because sparteine affects cardiac conduction — potentially dangerous cardiac effects at higher exposures. Poisonings ("lupin bean poisoning") occur when the debittering step is skipped or incomplete. **The bitter taste is the warning sign: if the seed tastes bitter, it is not safely debittered — do not eat it.** Only fully debittered seed is food.
- **⚠️ Lupin allergy / peanut cross-reactivity.** Lupin is a legume and its allergens **cross-react with peanut**; people with peanut allergy (and other legume allergies) can react to lupin, sometimes severely. Lupin flour is an increasingly common hidden ingredient in gluten-free and bakery products — a labelled allergen in the EU/UK for this reason. Peanut-allergic individuals should avoid lupin unless cleared by an allergist.
- **Pregnancy/lactation:** avoid medicinal (bitter) use entirely; alkaloids are teratogenic in livestock ("crooked calf disease" from related *Lupinus* QAs).
- **Sparteine-sensitive individuals / those on cardiac or anticholinergic medication:** avoid bitter-seed exposure.
- Do not self-administer the traditional bitter vermifuge preparation.
