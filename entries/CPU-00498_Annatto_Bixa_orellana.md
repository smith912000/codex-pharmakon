# Annatto / Achiote / Urucum

## *Bixa orellana* L. | Family: Bixaceae

**Tradition Tags:** SA
**Energetic Classification:** No humoral assignment can be sourced from the indigenous South American traditions that actually use this plant. Amazonian and Mesoamerican ethnomedicine does not operate a Galenic hot/cold grid, and where later Caribbean and Latin American folk practice assigns annatto a "cooling" quality, that is a **post-Columbian creole overlay of Iberian humoralism onto an indigenous plant**, not an indigenous classification. Recorded here as **outside humoral systems — attribution uncertain**.
**Training Phase:** Foundation — Signature Study

### Origin & Tradition

*Bixa orellana* is a fast-growing shrub or small tree of tropical Central and South America, now cultivated pantropically. It is one of the more visually unmistakable plants a cultivator will grow: glossy heart-shaped leaves, pink or white five-petalled flowers, and — the whole point of the plant — spiny crimson seed capsules that split to reveal a cluster of small seeds coated in a waxy, intensely red-orange aril. That aril is the drug, the dye, the food colourant and the trade commodity, all at once. The specific epithet honours Francisco de Orellana, the Spaniard who descended the Amazon in 1541–42, which tells you how early the plant entered European record.

**SA** in this Codex denotes South American indigenous ethnobotany. Annatto's place there is exceptionally secure, because unlike most ethnobotanical claims it is corroborated by continuous, visible, present-tense practice rather than by contested textual reconstruction. The aril paste is used as **body and face paint** across a wide range of Amazonian peoples — the Tsáchila of Ecuador are so identified with it that they are commonly called *colorados* — and the same paste is applied to the skin in contexts that are simultaneously ceremonial, cosmetic, and practical: as a barrier against sun and biting insects, and applied to skin complaints. Seeds are used as a culinary colourant and flavouring throughout Latin America and the Caribbean, most familiarly in *achiote* pastes and *recado rojo*.

The plant's second life is industrial. As **E160b**, annatto extract is one of the most widely used natural food colourants in the world — it is the yellow-orange in a great deal of commercial cheese, butter, margarine, smoked fish and snack food. This matters medically for a reason that surprises people: the largest human exposure to *Bixa orellana* on earth is not medicinal, it is dietary and involuntary, which is exactly why its allergy profile is better characterised than its therapeutics.

The leaf, distinct from the seed, has its own tradition of medicinal use in decoction, and has been the subject of a separate (small) research literature.

### Active Components

The pigment fraction is **apocarotenoid**, not carotenoid proper — these are cleaved, shortened carotenoid derivatives with carboxyl termini, which is why they behave so differently from β-carotene.

- **Bixin** — the fat-soluble, cis-configured methyl-ester apocarotenoid; the dominant pigment of the raw aril and the form used in oil-based colouring.
- **Norbixin** — the water-soluble dicarboxylic form, produced by saponification (alkaline hydrolysis) of bixin. This is the form used for aqueous food systems and, notably, the form that has attracted pharmacological interest.

According to PubMed, ethanol-based Soxhlet extraction of annatto seed gave a 31.4% dye yield, with TLC separating bixin and norbixin at Rf 0.56 and 0.42 and UV-vis absorption maxima at 457 and 453 nm respectively (Aluko, *ACS Omega*, 2024). Computational work on the four dye isomers present in achiote seed confirms strong absorption in the blue region across the visible spectrum (Lima et al., *Molecules*, 2022) — which is simply the physical chemistry of why the plant is red.

The second, less famous fraction is **vitamin E**: annatto seed is a notable and commercially exploited source of **tocotrienols**, and unusually the profile is overwhelmingly skewed to the **delta (δ) form**. Annatto seed tocotrienols are reported as **mostly (>90%) δ-tocotrienol** (Buckner et al., *Current Developments in Nutrition*, 2017). This is genuinely distinctive — most dietary tocotrienol sources (palm, rice bran) give mixed α/γ/δ profiles — and it is the reason annatto has a supplement industry attached to it that has nothing to do with its colour. Annatto seed is also described as a source of **geranylgeraniol** (de Oliveira Carvalho et al., *Inflammopharmacology*, 2023).

Alkaline aqueous seed extracts additionally contain **triterpenes** on GC-MS/LC-MS profiling, and the seed oil shows a more complex constituent profile than the isolated pigments alone.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Annatto is filed as a **Signature Study** because it teaches two lessons at once, and the second is the harder one.

The first lesson is **the colourant as legitimate technology**. Annatto is not a marginal folk dye; it is a globally traded, regulatory-approved pigment with well-understood chemistry, two solubility forms deliberately generated to suit different food matrices, and a documented indigenous origin. The cultivator's use case here is direct and real: grow it, harvest the capsules, extract the aril, and you have a functioning natural colourant for food, textile and cosmetic work. That use requires no medical claim whatsoever and is not diminished by the absence of one.

The second lesson is **fractionation discipline**. "Annatto" in the supplement aisle means δ-tocotrienol. "Annatto" in the cheese aisle means bixin/norbixin. These are chemically unrelated fractions of the same seed with entirely separate evidence bases, and conflating them — as marketing routinely does — produces claims that are false about both. A cultivator who cannot say which fraction a claim refers to cannot evaluate the claim.

Applied study uses: natural pigment production; comparative extraction practice (the bixin → norbixin saponification is an excellent teaching reaction); and as the Codex's index case for food-additive hypersensitivity.

### Preparation & Dosing

No therapeutic dose of annatto is established for any human medicinal indication. Figures below are reported experimental or food-use figures and are **not** dosing recommendations.

| Preparation | Context | Figure as reported | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aril paste, topical | Body paint; traditional skin use | Applied ad libitum | Traditional |
| Seed / aril, culinary | Colourant and flavouring | Culinary quantities | Established food use |
| Annatto extract, E160b | Commercial food colourant | Set by food-additive regulation, not by this entry | Regulated food additive |
| Leaf powder, oral | Human safety observation | 750 mg/day for 6 months — no significant or serious adverse effects reported | Single uncontrolled human safety observation |
| δ-tocotrienol, isolated | Preclinical oncology / inflammation | Cell and rodent models only | Preclinical |
| Standardised seed-oil nanodispersion, intramuscular | Rat acute toxicity | Non-toxic 1–5 mg/kg; muscle damage at 10 mg/kg | Animal toxicology, not a human route |

Safety caveats attached to preparation: the leaf and the seed are **different drugs** and their data do not transfer. The 750 mg/day leaf figure is a safety observation, not an efficacy dose, and comes from a review noting that **no subchronic animal toxicity studies had been conducted**. The intramuscular nanodispersion is experimental pharmaceutics; injection is not a preparation route available to any cultivator and is recorded here only because it is where the dose–toxicity boundary was actually measured.

### Scientific Correlation

**Claim: the pigments are the apocarotenoids bixin (fat-soluble) and norbixin (water-soluble).**
Grade: **Well supported (analytical chemistry).** Confirmed by extraction, TLC, UV-vis and FTIR, with the structural distinction between the two (the -CO- stretch and methylene vibration absent in norbixin) directly observed.

**Claim: annatto is a notable δ-tocotrienol source.**
Grade: **Well supported (compositional).** Reported as >90% δ-form. This is a compositional fact and stands on its own.

**Claim: antioxidant activity.**
Grade: **Supported in vitro only.** Alkaline aqueous seed extracts and extracted seed oil gave IC50 < 50 µg/mL across DPPH, ABTS+ and CUPRAC assays. Note the important internal control: **commercial annatto oil showed no detectable antioxidant activity under the same conditions.** In-vitro antioxidant assays are not evidence of an effect in a human being, and this result additionally shows that the commercial product may not carry the activity of the fresh extract at all.

**Claim: annatto is a traditional sunscreen — REFUSED AS STATED, and corrected.**
Grade: **Contradicted in the specific form the folklore implies.** The traditional use as a sun barrier is real and is not disputed here. But the pharmacological test does not support annatto extract as a photoprotective agent. Formulations containing annatto alkaline extracts raised in-vitro SPF sharply *before* irradiation (86.7 and 86.3 versus 23.3 for base formulation) — and then **collapsed after irradiation, to 11.51 and 21.36, i.e. at or below the base formulation**. The authors' conclusion is explicit: limited photostability restricts direct application as a standalone photoprotective agent; the extracts are better positioned as *auxiliary antioxidant* ingredients. **The correction the cultivator must carry: annatto degrades under exactly the light it is supposed to be blocking. An impressive unirradiated SPF number is a laboratory artefact, and any product marketed on that number is being sold on a figure its authors disowned in the same paper.** A physical aril paste on skin may well shade and deter insects; that is opacity and chemistry of a different kind, and should not be described as SPF.

**Claim: annatto causes hypersensitivity reactions.**
Grade: **Documented, and confirmed by controlled challenge — but genuinely uncommon.** A 1991 case report described urticaria, angioedema and severe hypotension within 20 minutes of ingesting annatto-coloured food; skin testing to annatto was strongly positive with negative controls, and immunoblotting demonstrated patient-specific IgE to a ~50 kD protein band — implicating **residual seed protein contaminating the dye**, not the pigment molecule. A 2021 case confirmed annatto hypersensitivity by **placebo-controlled oral challenge**, which is the strongest study design in this entry. A review of natural colour additives concluded that in rare cases annatto may provoke a severe reaction in individuals with an uncommon hypersensitivity and may aggravate recurrent urticaria, while noting only one reported anaphylaxis case across annatto's long history of use, and that the recurrent-urticaria studies lacked double-blind and placebo controls. **Both halves of that are true and must be stated together: the reaction is real, mechanistically characterised and challenge-confirmed; and it is rare.**

**Claim: annatto treats diabetes.**
Grade: **Deflated — preclinical, old, and thin.** A hypoglycaemic effect was reported in anaesthetised dogs in 1985; a 2013 review lists hypoglycaemic activity among animal-study findings for leaf extracts. There is no human efficacy evidence. **Annatto does not treat diabetes.**

**Claim: annatto treats cancer.**
Grade: **Deflated — δ-tocotrienol only, cell and animal models only, and even then as a sensitiser rather than an agent.** In pancreatic cancer models, δ-tocotrienol augmented TRAIL-induced apoptosis via proteasome-mediated c-FLIP down-regulation. That is a mechanistic finding about an isolated vitamin E isomer used *in combination with* a biologic agent. It is not a finding about annatto, about seeds, about food colouring, or about people. **Do not permit the inferential chain "δ-tocotrienol did something in a dish → annatto is anticancer."**

**Claim: broad antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antidiarrhoeal action of leaf extracts.**
Grade: **Preclinical / traditional. Human efficacy NULL.** The 2013 review states plainly that few human studies have been conducted and published, that no studies assessed urogenital infection inhibition despite the folk use for gonorrhoea, and that additional controlled safety and efficacy studies are needed.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

No indigenous South American energetic classification is available to this entry, and the Codex does not invent one. Amazonian ethnomedical frameworks are not built on elemental qualities in the Galenic sense; where they classify at all, they classify by relational and ecological categories that do not translate into hot/cold/wet/dry without distortion. The "cooling" designation sometimes attached to annatto in Caribbean and Latin American herbalism is best read as Iberian humoral theory absorbing a New World plant after contact — a real tradition, but a colonial-era one, and not the tradition the **SA** tag denotes.

The one traditional read that is both indigenous and defensible is non-energetic and functional: annatto is a **surface** plant. Its uses cluster on the skin and on the outside of things — paint, sun and insect barrier, skin complaints, and the colouring of food rather than its nourishment. That the modern evidence base is also strongest at the surface (pigment chemistry, topical/cosmetic, contact and ingestion hypersensitivity) and weakest in the interior (no human therapeutic efficacy anywhere) is a coincidence worth noticing without over-reading.

### Contraindications

- **Hypersensitivity — the principal real risk.** Annatto is a documented cause of IgE-mediated food-colour hypersensitivity, with urticaria, angioedema and at least one case of anaphylaxis with severe hypotension. Confirmed by placebo-controlled oral challenge. Anyone with a known annatto reaction must avoid it — and must be warned that avoidance is difficult, because E160b is pervasive in cheese, butter, margarine, smoked fish, cereals and snack foods and is not always conspicuous on labels.
- **The allergen is probably residual seed protein, not the pigment.** This has a practical consequence: crude or home-prepared aril extracts may carry *more* protein than a purified commercial colourant, not less. "Natural and unrefined" is the wrong safety heuristic here.
- **Contact sensitisation.** Case literature includes contact dermatitis alongside oral reaction. Body-paint and cosmetic use is a sensitisation route, and topical sensitisation followed by dietary exposure is a known pattern for colourant allergens generally.
- **Recurrent urticaria.** Annatto has been reported to aggravate symptoms in recurrent-urticaria patients, though the studies supporting this lacked double-blind and placebo controls. Consider as a possible dietary trigger; do not assert it as established.
- **Do not treat as a sunscreen.** See the correction above. Relying on an annatto preparation for UV protection risks burn injury, because its in-vitro SPF collapses on irradiation.
- **No established human medicinal dose or indication.** All therapeutic grading in this entry is preclinical or NULL. Do not substitute annatto for treatment of diabetes, infection, or cancer.
- **Do not extrapolate from δ-tocotrienol supplements to annatto seed, or vice versa.** They are different exposures with different evidence.
- **Injection is not a route.** The intramuscular data cited exist only as pharmaceutical toxicology; muscle damage was observed at 10 mg/kg in rats. Nothing in this entry authorises parenteral use.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, paediatric therapeutic use:** no controlled safety data retrieved beyond ordinary dietary exposure. Avoid medicinal quantities.
- **Toxicological gap.** As of the 2013 review, no subchronic toxicity studies had been conducted in animals for leaf extracts. Long-term safety at medicinal doses is unknown, not established-safe.
