# I-499 — Valerian
## *Valeriana officinalis* L.

**Family:** Valerianaceae (Caprifoliaceae s.l.) | **Volume:** I — Plant-Based | **Entry:** I-499

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted Name:** *Valeriana officinalis* L. (1753)
**Common Names:** Valerian, garden valerian, common valerian, all-heal, setwall, capon's tail, cat's lovage, vandal root
**Synonyms:** *Valeriana officinalis* var. *latifolia* Miq.; *Valeriana officinalis* var. *officinalis*; *Valeriana exaltata* Mik.; species complex with significant morphological/chemical variation

**Etymology:** *Valeriana* from Latin *valere* (to be strong, to be healthy) — the name records the plant's perceived strength as a medicine; alternatively from Valeria, a Roman province in modern Pannonia (Hungary/Croatia) where the plant grew abundantly; *officinalis* = of the apothecary; "all-heal" reflects the broad traditional applications; "setwall" from Old French *Ciperus* → *Setwall* (folk etymology unclear); "vandal root" from a supposed Vandal tribe association (unreliable etymology)

**Botanical Description:** Robust perennial, 50–150 cm; rhizomatous, with thick, aromatic rootstock that develops the characteristic fetid odor upon drying (valerenic acid oxidation products); stems erect, hollow, furrowed; leaves pinnate with 7–12 pairs of lanceolate leaflets; flowers small, white to pale pink, in terminal corymbs; bloom June–August; the dried root's distinctive odor — described variously as musty, earthy, fetid, socks-like — is immediately recognizable and serves as a practical authentication test; the fresh root has a milder, more pleasant aroma

**Distribution:** Native to Europe and western Asia; widely naturalized in North America, Australia; common in moist habitats, stream margins, fens, woodland margins, meadows; cultivated commercially in Germany, Belgium, Netherlands, China; the Netherlands and Belgium supply the majority of commercial valerian root for European phytomedicine

**Plant Part Used:** Root and rhizome (dried); fresh root (for fresh plant preparations); must be dried — fresh root lacks the characteristic valepotriate and valerenic acid profile that develops during drying through enzymatic and chemical transformation

**Adulterants/Substitutions:** *Valeriana wallichii* DC. (Indian valerian; used in Ayurvedic tradition as *tagara*; lower valerenic acid content); *V. jatamansi* Jones (similar Himalayan species, also *tagara*); *Centranthus ruber* (red valerian; ornamental; not medicinal; same family); *Patrinia* spp. (Chinese valerian relatives)

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

### The Universal Sedative Root

Valerian achieves 5/9 confirmed convergence for the same indication across all confirmed traditions: nervous system calming, sleep facilitation, and sedative properties. This cross-cultural consensus — Mediterranean (Dioscorides), Islamic (Ibn Sīnā), Chinese, and Indian traditions all independently identifying the same root for the same primary application — represents one of the clearest examples of pharmacological convergence in the Codex. The consistency is remarkable because the traditions differ in everything else (framework, methodology, theoretical basis) while agreeing on the specific application.

The variation across traditions in secondary applications is instructive: Dioscorides emphasizes diuretic and emmenagogue properties; Ibn Sīnā extends to digestive and cardiac complaints; Sushruta Samhita uses *tagara* specifically for headache and fever alongside sedation; TCM applies it to pain syndromes. These secondary variations reflect different clinical emphases while preserving the primary nervine-sedative consensus, suggesting that the primary application (sedation) is so pharmacologically dominant that it overrides cultural filtering, while secondary applications reflect the particular medical needs and observational systems of each tradition.

### The Chemistry Complexity Problem

Valerian is pharmacologically unusual in the Codex because its mechanism remains genuinely contested despite extensive research. Unlike lemon balm (rosmarinic acid GABA-T inhibitor — clearly established) or lavender (linalool GABA-A PAM + VDCC — clearly established), valerian's sedative mechanism involves at least three distinct chemical fractions (valerenic acids, valepotriates, and GABA-related phenolics) that have different physicochemical properties, different stability profiles, different absorption patterns, and partly contradictory pharmacological results across studies.

This chemical complexity — unusual for a well-studied plant — means that different commercial preparations (aqueous extracts, ethanolic extracts, fresh plant tinctures, dry extracts, standardized valerenic acid extracts) have genuinely different pharmacological profiles. The result is a clinical trial literature plagued by inconsistency that is partly a preparation-standardization problem rather than a fundamental pharmacological problem.

### Valerian and Medieval Medicine

In the medieval European pharmacopoeia, valerian achieved an unusual distinction: it was simultaneously used as a medicinal herb (sedative, nervine), a spice (roots preserved in wine), and a perfume ingredient. The name "spikenard of the north" — applied to valerian in contrast to genuine spikenard (*Nardostachys jatamansi*) from the Himalayas — reflects its aromatic importance in medieval perfumery. The scent that modern noses find unpleasant (the dried root's fetid quality) was apparently valued differently in a medieval context where aromatic substances were rare and the sensory context of "strong and medicinal aroma" was culturally associated with therapeutic power.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

### Valerenic Acids (Primary Standardization Target)
**Valerenic acid:** 0.05–0.7% dry weight root; sesquiterpene carboxylic acid; GABA-A PAM at β2/β3 subunits (distinct BZD-adjacent binding site); also AChE inhibition; the primary standardization marker for modern commercial preparations; formed during drying from valerenic acid glycoside precursors

**Acetoxyvalerenic acid, hydroxyvalerenic acid:** valerenic acid structural analogs; similar activity

**Valeranone, valerenal:** sesquiterpene ketone and aldehyde; minor; CNS active in animal models

**Valerenic acid concentration note:** the characteristic odor of dried valerian root is produced primarily by isovaleric acid (formed from valerenic acid oxidation + ester hydrolysis during drying), not valerenic acid itself; high-quality root has both the odor AND the valerenic acid content; the odor without valerenic acid (as in adulterated products) indicates poor-quality material

### Valepotriates (Unstable Bioactive Fraction)
**Valtrate:** iridoid monoterpene ester; 0.05–0.5% fresh root; sedative; cytotoxic in vitro (alkylating agent); unstable — hydrolyzes rapidly to baldrinal and homodiene baldrinal upon drying, contact with water, or acidic conditions; ABSENT in most commercial dried preparations and teas (hydrolyzed before consumption)

**Isovaltrate, acevaltrate:** related valepotriates; similar instability

**Baldrinal, homodiene baldrinal:** valepotriate hydrolysis products; present in dried preparations; demonstrate sedative activity in animal models; less potent than intact valepotriates; possible cytotoxicity (alkylating intermediates) at high doses — this is the primary safety concern with high-dose valerian preparations; responsible for "hangover" effect in some users

### Amino Acid and Neurotransmitter Fraction
**GABA:** free GABA has been identified in valerian aqueous extracts at pharmacologically relevant concentrations in some studies; GABA is quaternary (charged), and blood-brain barrier penetration is very limited; whether exogenous GABA from valerian extracts contributes meaningfully to CNS effect remains debated; some evidence for intestinal GABA → indirect CNS signaling via vagal afferents

**Glutamine:** GABA precursor; present in aqueous extracts

**Isovaleric acid (from valerenic acid transformation):** contributes to the characteristic odor; some evidence for direct GABA-ergic activity

### Phenolic Fraction
**Linarin (acacetin-7-O-rutinoside):** flavone glycoside; sedative in animal models; enhances sleep quality in benzodiazepine-naive animals; works synergistically with valerenic acid in combination

**Hesperidin:** flavanone glycoside; antioxidant; mild sedative

**Chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid:** hydroxycinnamic acid antioxidants

**Luteolin, apigenin:** flavones; GABA-A PAM (the apigenin mechanism documented for chamomile and betony)

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 GABA-A Positive Allosteric Modulation (Primary Mechanism)

**Valerenic acid → GABA-A β2/β3 subunit PAM:**
Benke D et al. 2009 (*Molecular Pharmacology* 75(4):991-998): Key mechanistic paper; valerenic acid demonstrated direct positive allosteric modulation of GABA-A receptors at β2 and β3 subunits — a binding site DIFFERENT from the classical benzodiazepine binding site (α/γ subunit interface); this means valerenic acid works through a pharmacologically distinct but functionally similar mechanism to BZDs; importantly, the β2/β3 site selectivity means different subtype selectivity and potentially different clinical profile (less anxiolytic specificity, more sedative emphasis) than classical BZDs; no cross-tolerance with BZDs established at this site

**Combination mechanism (linarin + valerenic acid):** When valerian's flavone (linarin) and sesquiterpene (valerenic acid) fractions are combined, they show synergistic GABA-A potentiation greater than either alone — suggesting the extract's polypharmacological profile is greater than any single constituent, which may explain why isolated constituents often fail to replicate whole-extract efficacy in clinical trials

### 5.2 GABA Reuptake Inhibition

**Aqueous extracts → GABA transporter 1 (GAT-1) inhibition:**
Cavadas C et al. 1995 (*Planta Medica* 61(3):213-216): Aqueous valerian extract inhibits synaptosomal GABA uptake; this is a functionally significant mechanism — increasing synaptic GABA dwell time produces prolonged GABAergic signaling without requiring GABA-A direct modulation; the GAT-1 inhibitor mechanism of action is shared with the anticonvulsant drug tiagabine; it is not the same as GABA-A PAM and the two mechanisms are additive in practice

### 5.3 Adenosine Activity

**Lignans → adenosine A1 receptor agonism:**
Some valerian preparations contain lignans (pinoresinol and related) that demonstrate adenosine A1 receptor binding; adenosine A1 agonism is sedative/anxiolytic (caffeine's stimulant effect is mediated by adenosine A1/A2A antagonism — adenosine agonism would be the functional opposite); this mechanism may contribute to the sedative effect particularly with certain extraction methods

### 5.4 Serotonergic / 5-HT5A Mechanism

**Valerenic acid → 5-HT5A receptor agonism:**
Müller CE et al. 2002: Valerenic acid demonstrates partial agonism at serotonin 5-HT5A receptors; 5-HT5A agonism is associated with sedation and sleep facilitation; this mechanism is pharmacologically distinct from GABA modulation and may explain valerian's efficacy in some patients who do not respond to pure GABA-modulatory sedatives

### 5.5 Mechanism Summary — The Polypharmacological Profile

Valerian's sedative mechanism is genuinely multi-target: GABA-A β2/β3 PAM (valerenic acid) + GABA reuptake inhibition (aqueous extract ligands) + adenosine A1 agonism (lignans) + 5-HT5A partial agonism (valerenic acid) + GABA-A BZD-site PAM (apigenin/linarin). This polypharmacological profile accounts for valerian's effectiveness in some patients who have not responded to single-mechanism sedatives, and explains why it is pharmacologically distinct from benzodiazepines despite producing qualitatively similar effects.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 Sleep / Insomnia

**Evidence Level: A-B (multiple RCTs; mixed results)**

- Leathwood PD et al. 1982 (*Pharmacology, Biochemistry and Behavior* 17(1):65-71): First double-blind RCT; aqueous extract 400 mg vs placebo; sleep quality significantly improved (self-report); no "hangover" effect; Level A
- Balderer G & Borbély AA 1985 (*Pharmacology Biochemistry and Behavior* 23(3):447-452): Polysomnography study; valerian improved sleep quality without altering sleep structure; Level A
- Donath F et al. 2000 (*Pharmacology Biochemistry and Behavior* 65(4):591-596): Chronic insomnia; valerian 600 mg/day; 14 nights; improved sleep quality on polysomnography; RCT Level A
- Bent S et al. 2006 (*American Journal of Medicine* 119(12):1005-1012): Meta-analysis of 16 RCTs; concluded "inconclusive" for objective sleep measures; valerian may improve subjective sleep quality; highlighted preparation standardization problem as source of heterogeneity
- Fernández-San-Martín MI et al. 2010 (*Sleep Medicine* 11(6):505-511): Meta-analysis; evidence for subjective sleep quality improvement; insufficient for objective polysomnographic endpoints due to study heterogeneity

**Summary:** Valerian improves subjective sleep quality consistently across multiple RCTs; objective polysomnographic improvements are less consistent and preparation-dependent; the 1982–2000 positive RCTs used aqueous or ethanolic extracts; trials showing negative results often used preparations without validated valerenic acid content

### 6.2 Anxiety

**Evidence Level: B (some RCTs; less evidence than for sleep)**

- Andreatini R et al. 2002 (*Phytotherapy Research* 16(7):650-654): Generalized anxiety disorder; valerian vs diazepam vs placebo; 4 weeks; valerian significant effect on psychic but not somatic anxiety (HAM-A subscales); Level A but small sample
- Kennedy DO et al. 2006: Combination studies (valerian + lemon balm for anxiety); Level A; see I-478

### 6.3 Regulatory Status

- **Commission E (Germany):** APPROVED for states of excitability, difficulty in falling asleep; daily dose 2–3 g root or equivalent preparations
- **ESCOP:** Monographed; sleep disturbance, restlessness; well-established use status
- **EMA:** Community herbal monograph; traditional use for tension, restlessness, sleep disturbance; well-established use for sleep disturbance (based on 30-year + clinical data)
- **USP (USA):** Valeriana listed in USP Dietary Supplements compendium; not an FDA-approved drug

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

### 7.1 The Pied Piper — Rats and Valerian

A persistent folk association (and occasional claim) connects valerian root to the story of the Pied Piper of Hamelin, suggesting that the piper used valerian to attract rats (as well as the distinctive pipe music). The pharmacological basis for this is the intense attraction of rats and cats to dried valerian root — an attraction documented in animal pharmacology (different mechanism from catnip's nepetalactone; valerian attracts both rats and cats through volatiles including isovaleric acid that mimic certain pheromone compounds). Whether valerian was actually used to attract rats for pest control — and whether this influenced the Pied Piper story — is historically uncertain but pharmacologically plausible: the attraction of rats to valerian root is a real phenomenon.

### 7.2 Medieval "Spice" and Perfume

The use of valerian root as a fragrance ingredient in medieval Europe reflects the dramatic difference in olfactory culture between the medieval and modern periods. The strong, musky, earthy odor of dried valerian root — which modern noses often find unpleasant — was valued in the 12th–16th centuries as a rare, exotic, and powerful aromatic. Valerian root was used in sachets, incorporated into compound perfumes, and used to scent linen. Hildegard von Bingen (1098–1179) recommended it in her *Physica* for nervous complaints, and its presence in medieval pharmacy recipe collections as both medicine and aromatic ingredient documents the dual role.

### 7.3 The 20th-Century Anxiolytic Before Benzodiazepines

From approximately 1880 to 1960 — the era between the introduction of chloral hydrate (1869) and diazepam (1963) — valerian was one of the primary sedative/anxiolytic treatments available in Western medicine. It appeared in the official pharmacopoeias of the United States (USP from 1820 to 1942), United Kingdom, Germany, and most European countries. The era's physicians prescribed valerian for "neurasthenia," "nervous exhaustion," "hysteria," and "anxiety states" — the 19th and early 20th century diagnostic categories that correspond to modern anxiety disorder and insomnia diagnoses. The plant's 60-year pharmaceutical era as a primary anxiolytic provides an important clinical database, even without the RCT methodology that came later.

### 7.4 Tagara — The Indian Valerian Tradition

The Ayurvedic *tagara* (*V. wallichii* / *V. jatamansi*) occupies a position in Indian medicine comparable to valerian's in European medicine: the primary nervine-sedative botanical, used for headache, sleep disorders, epilepsy (supportive), palpitations, and mental fatigue. The *tagara* tradition dates to the Sushruta Samhita (pre-6th century CE) and continues in contemporary Ayurvedic practice, representing over 2,000 years of clinical use for substantially the same indications. The independent convergence of European and Indian medicine on Valeriana species for the same primary indication — through pharmacological systems with completely different theoretical frameworks — is among the strongest convergence arguments for the efficacy of valerian sedation.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Indications (Evidence-Based, Within Safety Parameters)

**Sleep Onset Difficulty / Mild Insomnia (Primary Indication — Commission E Approved):**
Standardized extract; Level A-B (multiple RCTs); 400–900 mg valerenic acid-standardized extract 30–60 minutes before bed; most effective for sleep-onset insomnia (difficulty falling asleep) vs sleep-maintenance insomnia; effects may take 2–4 weeks of regular use to fully manifest; appropriate first-line botanical for mild-moderate insomnia

**Anxiety / Nervousness (Commission E Approved):**
Standardized extract; Level B; 300–600 mg standardized extract divided over the day; appropriate for mild anxiety, nervous tension; not for panic disorder or severe GAD without medical supervision

**Restlessness / Nervous Excitability:**
Commission E approved; herb tea or standardized extract; Level C-B; appropriate for nervous excitability, restlessness, mild agitation

### 8.2 Preparations and Dosing

**Standardized Extract (Clinical Use):**
- Valerenic acid standardized: 0.3–0.8% valerenic acid equivalent
- Insomnia dose: 400–600 mg extract (providing 1–3 mg valerenic acid) 30–60 minutes before bed
- Anxiety dose: 100–200 mg three times daily during the day; 400 mg at bedtime
- Duration: trials typically 2–4 weeks; Commission E allows continuous use

**Herb Tea (Lower Potency):**
- 3 g dried root in 150 mL cold water; macerate 8–10 hours (cold extraction preferred to preserve valerenic acid); OR steep 10 minutes boiling water (some valerenic acid loss)
- 1 cup 30–60 minutes before bed; up to 3 cups daily
- The strong odor is unavoidable; palatable when combined with passionflower, lemon balm, or hops

**Tincture:**
- 1:5 in 60% ethanol; 2–4 mL 30–60 minutes before bed; or 1–2 mL three times daily for anxiety

**Combination Products:**
- Valerian + Lemon Balm: most RCT-validated combination for sleep (Kennedy 2006 et al. Level A)
- Valerian + Hops: traditional European combination; hops provide additional GABA-modulatory methylbutenol; Commission E-approved combination
- Valerian + Passionflower: traditional combination; additive GABA-A modulation mechanisms

### 8.3 Contraindications and Safety

**CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- **Pregnancy:** Insufficient safety data; avoid; cytotoxic valepotriate metabolites (baldrinals) represent theoretical teratogenic concern
- **Children under 12:** Insufficient pediatric safety data; avoid
- **Planned anesthesia:** GABAergic activity may potentiate anesthetic agents; discontinue at least 2 weeks before elective surgery; inform anesthetist

**DRUG INTERACTIONS:**
- **CNS depressants (alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, opioids, antihistamines):** Additive CNS depression; clinically significant; avoid concurrent heavy use; modest potentiation at typical herb doses; monitor
- **CYP3A4 substrates:** Some evidence for weak CYP3A4 inhibition from valerian constituents; monitor CYP3A4-metabolized drugs (cyclosporine, statins, some antiretrovirals) if using high-dose valerian
- **Benzodiazepine withdrawal:** Valerian may assist BZD withdrawal (shared GABAergic mechanism); some practitioners use it as BZD-taper support; clinical evidence limited; do not combine with full therapeutic BZD doses

**COMMON ADVERSE EFFECTS:**
- "Valerian hangover": morning grogginess with high doses (>900 mg); dose-reduce if occurring
- Paradoxical stimulation: rare; most common in children or elderly; may increase restlessness in these populations
- GI upset: rare; mild
- Headache: reported with chronic high-dose use

**SAFETY NOTE ON BALDRINALS:** The valepotriate hydrolysis products (baldrinals) have demonstrated cytotoxic activity in vitro. This concern prompted ESCOP to recommend against prolonged use (>4 weeks continuous) in the absence of physician supervision. In practice, decades of use without clear clinical cytotoxicity evidence suggests that baldrinal absorption and systemic exposure at oral doses is limited; however, the theoretical concern warrants this duration limitation in self-medication contexts.

### 8.4 Energetics

**Energetic Tier:** ☿ Shen (neuromodulatory) + ◉ Qi
**Thermal Quality:** Cool (Galenic classification); the sedative herbs are traditionally "cool" by convention
**Directional Action:** Downward, inward, consolidating (sleep-promoting, consciousness-contracting)
**TCM Classification:** Bitter, pungent; warm (unusual — TCM classifies differently from Galenic); liver and heart channels; calms the spirit, relieves depression, stops pain

### 8.5 Quality and Authentication

**Authentication (Critical — Preparation Determines Clinical Outcome):**
- Valerenic acid content: minimum 0.17% dry root (European Pharmacopoeia standard); commercial preparations should specify mg valerenic acid per dose
- HPLC profile: valerenic acid + acetoxyvalerenic acid + hydroxyvalerenic acid pattern distinguishes *V. officinalis* from *V. wallichii* (tagara) and adulterants
- Smell test: strong, characteristic fetid-earthy odor from dried root = isovaleric acid present = quality indicator; no smell = possible extraction problem or different species
- European Pharmacopoeia: *Valerianae radix* monograph; required valerenic acid content ≥0.17%; optional valepotriate content not standardized (preparation-dependent)
- **Avoid:** Products without valerenic acid specification; products claiming "all valepotriates preserved" (valepotriates are unstable; any preparation claiming full valepotriate content has likely been stored/processed inadequately or is making false claims)
