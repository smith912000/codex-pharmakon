# Trifolium arvense — Lagopous, lagopus, "hare's foot" (Dioscorides 4-17); "Field Clover, Hare's Foot"

## *Trifolium arvense [Linnaeus], the fourth name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-17, glossed "Field Clover, Hare's Foot"; a second line gives Filago lagopus ("Cotton Rose, Hare's Foot") as "other usage"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk lists the plant under this name (the name indexes in `Sources/*/extract/names.csv` and the list files in `_sources/` were searched). The entry rests on one very short chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain, and the translators print a different plant (*Filago lagopus*) as an "other usage" on a second line. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-17. LAGOPOUS* at line 8159, first SUGGESTED line at 8161 ("Lagopus, Leporinus pes, Trifolium humile [Fuchs], Trifolium arvense [Linnaeus] — Field Clover, Hare's Foot"), the "[other usage]" line at 8162 ("Filago lagopus — Cotton Rose, Hare's Foot"), and the text at line 8163. The page number 560 follows at line 8165. The chapter stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), after *4-16. LEIMONION* (line 8150) and before *4-18. MEDION* (line 8176). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 597, and the translators' index prints "Trifolium arvense 560" (line 17903). The woodcut captions "Limonium from FUCHS — 1545" (line 8168) and "Lycopsis from FUCHS — 1545" (line 8172) stand on the following pages, 561 and 562, and belong to other chapters (the first to 4-16, whose entry is I_1202).

The whole chapter reads: "Lagopus is restrictive to the intestines if a decoction is taken as a drink with wine (but for those with a fever with water). It is also hanged about one for inflammation of the groin. It grows in the ranks of corn. It is also called cuminum leporis."

That is all the chapter says. It gives no description of the plant's stalk, leaf, flower or root. Its only statements about the plant itself are where it grows ("in the ranks of corn", that is, among the grain crop) and the second name *cuminum leporis*, which is Latin and means in effect "hare's cumin". The Greek name itself, *lagopous*, is the "hare's foot" that the translators' gloss also prints. Two uses are recorded. The first is internal: a decoction taken as a drink "restrictive to the intestines", with wine, or with water "for those with a fever". The second is that the plant is "hanged about one for inflammation of the groin". The chapter does not say what is hung, on what, or for how long. It does not say whether the hanging is a drug or an amulet, and it does not comment on the practice. The Codex records it and does not read more into it.

**Overlap.** A chapter-level search of the entries folder for 4-17, the Greek heading, *Lagopus*, *Trifolium arvense* and *Hare's Foot* found no entry on this chapter. I_1202 (*Statice thouini*, chapter 4-16) names the chapter in passing as the one after its own (its line 12). I_1088 (*Filago germanica*, chapter 3-132) says that a third chapter prints *Filago lagopus* on a SUGGESTED line (line 8162) and that it is separate (its line 18). That is the same "other usage" line as here, so the two entries touch only through that printed name. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is a general fact that *Trifolium arvense* is a clover in the pea family (Fabaceae), and that *Filago lagopus*, the translators' alternative, belongs to a different family (the daisy family, Asteraceae). The chapter itself supports no statement about the composition of either plant. The word "restrictive" is Dioscorides' description of an effect, and it is not a statement of composition. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records an astringent drink for the bowels and a plant hung on the person for inflammation of the groin. These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol from a three-sentence chapter and an identification that is only a suggestion, and it does not endorse hanging a plant on the body for inflammation.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation: "a decoction" taken "as a drink with wine (but for those with a fever with water)". It gives no amount, no frequency, no duration and no method for making the decoction. The hanging is mentioned without any detail. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that astringent plant preparations are traditionally used for loose bowels, and that diarrhoea with fever needs medical assessment because of the risk of dehydration and infection. Nothing is stated about the effect of *Trifolium arvense*, or of *Filago lagopus*, in particular.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 4-17 prints one quality, that the decoction is "restrictive to the intestines" (an astringent, binding action). It prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality and no taste.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is thin: a habitat (among the corn), a distinction in how the drink is made up (wine, or water for the feverish), and the second name *cuminum leporis*. It gives no description for recognition and no assay by smell or taste. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The hanging "about one" is recorded without comment, and recording is not endorsing; the text neither distances itself from the practice nor gives it a name, and the Codex adds none. The identification is the translators', and they print two different plants. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The chapter is a very short drug note, and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 4-17 prints no warning. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, and the translators offer two unlike plants, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. The drink is for looseness of the bowels, and persistent diarrhoea, particularly with fever, needs medical assessment and should not be treated by an unidentified plant. The chapter gives no dose and no safety information for pregnancy or children. [USER: verify]
