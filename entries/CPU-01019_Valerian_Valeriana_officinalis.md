# Valerian — Phu (Dioscorides), All-heal, Amantilla, Setwall

## *Valeriana officinalis* L. | Family: Caprifoliaceae (formerly Valerianaceae)

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM, SS, FMT
**Energetic Classification:** Warm and dry (Galenic, 1st–2nd degree); heating nervine
**Training Phase:** Descent / Stilling (parasympathetic downshift, pre-sleep)

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Dioscorides (De Materia Medica).** In the first-century Greek herbal the plant appears as *phu* (φοῦ) — a name usually read as onomatopoeia for the odour of the dried root, which sours with age into something distinctly cheese-like. Dioscorides grouped it with the warming aromatic roots and directed it as a diuretic and warming agent that "moves the urine" and warms the interior. The Greek *phu* is the root of the later apothecary label *Valeriana*, and through the Latin *valere* ("to be strong, to be well") the plant slid into the medieval European name **All-heal** — a promiscuous title it shared with several other panaceas.

**Galenic / Arabic nervine tradition.** Medieval and early-modern Galenic practice classed valerian among the warming, drying nervines: agents that "comfort the nerves and brain," dispel wind and flatus, and settle the *tremors* and *palpitations* attributed to a cold, unsettled pneuma. This is where valerian earned its enduring vernacular role as a calmative for "hysteria," restlessness and wakefulness — a role that peaked when it was used widely for shell-shock and nervous complaint in the early twentieth century.

**Sushruta / Ayurvedic adjacency.** Ayurveda's principal *tagara* is the closely related *Valeriana jatamansi* (syn. *V. wallichii*), and the aromatic **jatamansi** (*Nardostachys*) sits in the same therapeutic neighbourhood. These are classed as *medhya* (mind-supporting) and nervine-sedative botanicals, kapha- and vata-pacifying, used for *unmada* (disordered mind) and *anidra* (sleeplessness). *V. officinalis* is the European vicariant of this same *tagara* function; the doctrine — a warm, earthy, grounding root that pulls a scattered mind downward — is remarkably consistent across the Greek and Indian materia medica. **The cat signature.** Across traditions the root's notorious attractiveness to cats (the same actinidine/valepotriate volatiles that hit feline olfaction like catnip) was read as evidence of its potency and its affinity for the animal nervous system — a folk "doctrine of the excited beast" that survives in the herb's common lore rather than in any formal humoral text.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The doctrine is coherent and, unusually, half-vindicated by chemistry. "Warm and dry" nervine that "grounds a scattered mind" maps onto a genuine, if modest, positive allosteric modulation of the GABA-A receptor — the brain's principal inhibitory brake — by **valerenic acid**. Where the doctrine says *warms and gathers the pneuma*, the pharmacology says *enhances chloride-channel inhibition at β2/β3-containing GABA-A receptors*, damping cortical and limbic excitation. The "all-heal" universalism, though, is where doctrine over-reaches: a mild, non-benzodiazepine GABAergic tone is real but is not a panacea, and the evidence base (below) refuses to confirm the grand claim. The pungent, souring odour that folk tradition read as the sign of a "strong" root is the volatile valerenic/valepotriate fraction degrading — the very instability that plagues its clinical reliability.

### Active Components

- **Sesquiterpenic acids — valerenic acid, acetoxyvalerenic acid, valerenol** (the officinalis-defining fraction; the modern standardisation marker).
- **Valepotriates** — valtrate, isovaltrate, didrovaltrate (epoxy-iridoid esters; chemically unstable, decomposing on storage/heat to baldrinals).
- **Volatile oil** — bornyl acetate, β-caryophyllene, valeranone, actinidine (the feline-attractant fraction).
- Trace **GABA and glutamine** in aqueous extracts (proposed but pharmacokinetically dubious as a direct CNS contributor — GABA poorly crosses the blood–brain barrier).
- Lignans and low-level flavonoids (minor).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Filed as a **Descent / Stilling** agent: a pre-sleep or acute-restlessness nervine to take the edge off sympathetic overdrive. Not a knock-out hypnotic; better understood as an anxiolytic-leaning sedative that lowers the threshold to sleep rather than forcing it. Pairs conventionally with lemon balm, hops or passionflower. Best used as a short course or situationally, not as an indefinite nightly crutch.

### Scientific Correlation

*Based on articles retrieved from PubMed:*

- **Mechanism — GABA-A is the real substrate.** Benke et al. identified a specific nanomolar binding site for valerenic acid and valerenol on GABA-A receptors; both enhanced GABA responses at recombinant receptors, and a β3 (N265M) point mutation *abolished* the anxiolytic effect in mice — direct genetic evidence that β3-containing GABA-A receptors mediate valerian's action ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropharm.2008.06.013)). Becker et al. confirmed that the extract's anxiolytic effect tracks specifically with valerenic acid content, with no affinity for benzodiazepine binding sites — a distinct, non-benzodiazepine GABAergic mechanism ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6882-14-267)).
- **Sleep evidence — real signal, weak floor.** Bent et al.'s meta-analysis (16 trials, 1,093 patients) found a statistically significant benefit for the dichotomous outcome "improved sleep" (relative risk 1.8, 95% CI 1.2–2.9) — *but explicitly flagged evidence of publication bias and significant methodological problems*, with widely varying doses and preparations ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjmed.2006.02.026)). The larger systematic review/meta-analysis by Shinjyo et al. (60 studies, n≈6,894) attributed the *inconsistent* trial outcomes to variable extract quality and the instability of the active constituents, concluding valerian is plausibly effective and safe but that the field needs standardisation and better shelf-life control ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/2515690X20967323)). Honest reading: a genuine but modest anxiolytic/sleep signal, not a robustly proven hypnotic; heterogeneity and probable publication bias inflate the apparent effect.
- **Hepatotoxicity — rare but reported.** Vassiliadis et al. published a case of acute hepatotoxicity associated with a valerian-containing preparation ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2008.09.009)). Such reports are uncommon and frequently involve multi-herb combination products, making single-agent causation hard to establish — but they warrant caution.

**Evidence grade:** *Mechanism* — strong (receptor-level, genetically confirmed). *Anxiolytic effect* — moderate (consistent preclinical, plausible clinical). *Sleep efficacy* — weak-to-moderate and contested (positive pooled estimates undercut by heterogeneity and publication bias). *The "all-heal" claim* — unsupported and deflated.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** *V. officinalis* not a classical Chinese materia medica staple; the related *Valeriana jatamansi* is used regionally (Xie Cao / 缬草) as a calming, qi-moving, damp-transforming herb — not attested in the core canon and marked as adjacent rather than central.
- **Ayurveda (Sushruta-adjacent):** via *tagara* (*V. jatamansi*) — *rasa* (taste): bitter, pungent, astringent; *guna* (quality): light, dry; *virya* (potency): heating; *vipaka* (post-digestive): pungent; action *medhya*/nervine, vata- and kapha-pacifying.
- **Galenic / Unani:** warm and dry (1st–2nd degree); *muqawwī al-a'sāb* (nerve-strengthening) nervine, carminative, diuretic.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** In *De Materia Medica* the plant is *phu* (φοῦ), grouped with the warming aromatic roots and directed as a heating diuretic that "moves the urine" and warms the interior. Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system into which such a root could be graded — the Galenic three-spirits doctrine is later and post-Dioscoridean — so there is no cultivation status to assign. What the tradition offers a cultivator of this root is his discipline of attention: the name *phu* is itself onomatopoeia for the odour, and the root's characteristic souring into a cheese-like smell as the volatile fraction degrades is a sensory assay of age and potency. Reading provenance and freshness by that odour is a real practice of judgement, not a vital-body doctrine, and it maps precisely onto the modern finding that the active valerenic/valepotriate fraction is unstable and decays on storage. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** In Galenic-Unani practice valerian is classed warm and dry (1st–2nd degree) and worked as *muqawwī al-a'sāb* — a nerve-strengthening nervine that comforts the nerves and brain, dispels wind, and settles the tremors and palpitations read as a cold, unsettled *pneuma*. The strongest cultivation idea in the corpus is the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture — ageing understood as depletion of a finite vital endowment, longevity therefore a matter of conservation rather than acquisition. That frame governs the whole regimen; it is not a status the tradition assigns to this warming nervine. The sources record valerian as a *muqawwī al-a'sāb* — a functional strengthener of the nerves in unsettlement — with no cultivation grade attached, and no *mufarrih* (cardiac-*ruh*) role from the *Kitāb al-Adwiya al-Qalbiyya* is claimed, nor are *Canon* chapter references invoked. Its standing is therapeutic, not cultivational. **(b) framed:** attested as a nervine, no cultivation status sourced.

**Ayurveda (SS).** Ayurveda's *tagara* is properly the closely related *Valeriana jatamansi* (syn. *V. wallichii*), classed *medhya* (mind-supporting) and nervine-sedative, vata- and kapha-pacifying, and used for *unmada* (disordered mind) and *anidra* (sleeplessness); *V. officinalis* is the European vicariant of this function and is not itself a drug of the classical Indian materia medica. *Medhya rasāyana* is a genuine cultivation category, but the sources retrieved assign it — and any *rasayana* or *vajikarana* standing — to *tagara*/*jatamansi*, not to *V. officinalis*, and no named classical source (Charaka, Sushruta) attests a rejuvenative grade for *officinalis* specifically. The Ayurvedic claim on this entry therefore rests on the neighbouring species, and for *V. officinalis* no cultivation status can be sourced. **(b) framed:** function attested by adjacency to *tagara*, no cultivation status for this species.

**Tibetan (FMT).** No Sowa Rigpa attestation for *Valeriana officinalis* was found in this entry or the sources retrieved: the body records Greek, Galenic-Unani and Ayurvedic use but no rGyud-bzhi material for this species. Sowa Rigpa in any case has no formal "substances for subtle-body practice" category — pathology is traced to the three *nyes pa* born of the three poisons, and medicine is only the third of the four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching the root; *bcud len* (essence-extraction) is a defined practice of the Four Tantras (Gerke) to which no source assigns this plant. With neither a species attestation nor a cultivation category to draw on, nothing can be claimed. **(c) absent.**

**Practitioner's note.** Valerian is a stilling agent, not a longevity substance: a pre-sleep or acute-restlessness nervine that lowers the threshold to sleep rather than forcing it, working through a modest, non-benzodiazepine GABA-A tone. What a cultivator does with it is situational and short — the fresh root infusion or a standardised extract in the evening, in short courses, paired conventionally with lemon balm or hops — and explicitly *not* as an indefinite nightly crutch. The "all-heal" name over-promises; the discipline the herb actually rewards is the Dioscoridean one of judging the root by its odour and keeping the material fresh, since the active fraction decays and with it the reliability. Nothing across the four tagged traditions grades it as something taken to build vitality; it is taken to quiet an overexcited system, then stopped.

### Contraindications

- **Additive CNS depression.** Valerian's GABAergic action can potentiate alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, opioids, and other sedative-hypnotics — do not combine; caution with driving/machinery after dosing.
- **Peri-operative.** Discontinue well before surgery given potential interaction with anaesthesia (GABAergic additive effect).
- **Hepatic caution.** Rare hepatotoxicity reports (often combination products); avoid in pre-existing liver disease and discontinue if jaundice, dark urine or right-upper-quadrant pain develop.
- **Valepotriate instability/genotoxicity concern.** Valepotriates are chemically unstable and have shown cytotoxic/alkylating activity in vitro; aged or poorly stored material has an uncertain profile — favour standardised, fresh preparations.
- **Pregnancy/lactation:** insufficient safety data — avoid. **Paediatric:** the sleep reviews included ages 7+ without severe adverse events, but routine paediatric use is not established. [USER: verify house policy on paediatric dosing.]
- Occasional paradoxical stimulation/vivid dreaming in a minority of users.
