# Aconitum naviculare — bong.nga.dkar.po, Bongnga karpo, "White Aconite" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Aconitum naviculare Stapf., the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for bong.nga.dkar.po (with Aconitum sinense and Aconitum heterophyllum); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints "Aconitum heterophyllum" for the same phonetic name* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts in this row.** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this binomial. Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and the row's binomial is the first of three on Clark's line. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 965).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads: "bong.nga.dkar.po: White Aconite: Aconitum naviculare Stapf.: Aconitum sinense: Aconitum heterophyllum: [has ten names and is of four types: white, red, yellow and black. The black type is highly toxic, whilst the white one has bitter taste and neutralizes poison. The latter has small, pointed leaves with seven or eight lobes. The stalk is small and supple, and the flowers are pale blue with a reddish hue]. It cures infectious fever with poisoning and bile fever."

Clark's preface (line 82) says "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". So everything but the last sentence is the commentary layer: the ten names, the four types, the statement that the black type is highly toxic, the white type's bitter taste and poison-neutralising action, and the description of leaf, stalk and flower. What stands outside the brackets is one sentence: the aconite "cures infectious fever with poisoning and bile fever". The root-text layer gives no taste, no power and no warning.

The next two entries in the run are the red aconite (line 968, "an antidote to poison") and the yellow aconite (line 969, which with the red "cure meat poisoning and poisoning from black aconite"). No line read gives a way of processing any aconite to make it safe.

Four more Clark lines name the white aconite. In chapter 21, "The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines" (heading at line 1204): line 1209, "The class of medicines which cure bile disorders includes Chiretta, Bitter Cucumber, Kurchi bark, White Aconite, Ixeris gracilis, Pyrola incarnata, Gentiana straminea and Indian Barberry"; line 1211, "The categories of medicines which cure infectious fevers includes Bile of elephant and so forth, Bitter Cucumber, White Aconite, ..."; and line 1212, the poison-neutralising class, "Red, Yellow and White Aconites". Clark's name index prints "White Aconite" (line 1661), "Aconitum naviculare" (line 1554) and "Aconitum sinense" and "Aconitum heterophyllum" (same index line).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011.* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. Chapter 20, line 3213, lists "Bongnga karpo" in the group of substances "having a bitter taste" (line 3214 closes the sentence). Line 3421 reads: "Bongnga karpo cures contagious diseases, poisoning, and hot disorders associated with tripa disorders." Line 3601 lists it in the group of medicines that cure contagious disorders ("Giwam, Sergyi metok, Bongnga karpo, Gadhur, Ji tsher, Dhewa, Parpata and Traagkang"), and line 3604 in the group that cures poisoning ("La tsi, Bongnga karpo, Bongnga serpo, Bongnga marpo, ..."). Line 3593 begins another list that names it ("Tikta, Sergyi metok, Dhungmo nyung, Bongnga karpo, Tsa tri, Yakyima,"); the group heading is not on that line and the Codex does not state which group it is. The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints, on one line (5114), "Bongnga karpo bong nga dkar po Aconitum heterophyllum", so the Tibetan name is paired there with Clark's third binomial and not the first. The index (line 7279) gives "Bongngakarpo 187,205,219,220".

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *bong.nga*, *Aconitum naviculare* and the Tibetan names found: I_407 (*Aconitum heterophyllum*, Ativisha, tags CM, SS, FMT), which is the entry for the binomial the glossary prints and Clark's third, and which already carries an FMT tag for the white-aconite material; I_903 (*Aconitum lycoctonum*, tags DMM, FMT), which quotes this Clark entry's neighbour at line 969; I_231 (*Aconitum tanguticum*), whose register mentions *Aconitum naviculare* in passing; and I_004 (*Aconitum napellus*). Whether Clark's *A. naviculare* and *A. heterophyllum* are one plant is not settled by the lines read, and the Codex does not merge them. No existing entry is on Clark's first binomial.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that the aconites (*Aconitum*) are known to contain toxic diterpenoid alkaloids. Which species the Tibetan "white aconite" is, and what its roots contain, was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a white aconite that cures infectious fever with poisoning and bile fever (Clark line 965), is listed among bile-disorder, infectious-fever and poison-neutralising medicines (Clark lines 1209, 1211, 1212), and in the Men-Tsee-Khang text cures contagious diseases, poisoning and hot disorders associated with *tripa* (line 3421). These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. Aconites are generally known to be deadly poisons, the botanical identity is unsettled, and the Codex gives no protocol for this plant.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, frequency or duration for the white aconite. Clark's commentary layer says only that "the black type is highly toxic" (line 965). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Aconite alkaloids are generally known to be potent cardiac and neurological toxins, and poisoning from *Aconitum* plants is a well-documented medical emergency. This is a statement about the genus. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identity of the drug is uncertain between three binomials, so a modern study of one species would not bear directly on it.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (inside the brackets of line 965) prints the white type as having "bitter taste" and as neutralizing poison. No power (hot, cool, heavy or light) is printed for it. The Men-Tsee-Khang text lists Bongnga karpo among the bitter-tasting substances (line 3213) and says it cures "hot disorders associated with tripa disorders" (line 3421). No other quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica places the white aconite among the medicines for infectious fever with poisoning and for bile fever (line 965) and lists it in the classes for bile disorders, infectious fevers and poisons (lines 1209, 1211, 1212). The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this entry places the white aconite in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice; the essence-extraction mentions in the herb section are for other plants (for example at lines 971 and 1121). Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a fever, bile and poison drug in the Tibetan texts, from a genus in which one named type is called highly toxic, and nothing more can be said from them. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The only warning in the lines read is Clark's bracketed statement on this entry: "The black type is highly toxic" (line 965). No statement says the white type is safe. Aconites are generally known to be deadly poisons and must not be gathered or taken on the strength of this entry; the botanical identity is unsettled. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
