# Carnation — Clove Pink, Gillyflower, Qumai-kin (阿拉伯石竹)

## *Dianthus caryophyllus* L. | Family: Caryophyllaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Warm & Dry (2nd degree) — a cordial, spirit-lifting aromatic in the Galenic-Unani frame

**Training Phase:** Integration

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Dianthus caryophyllus*, the clove pink or carnation, is a Mediterranean perennial of the pink family whose spice-scented, fringed flowers gave it the epithet *caryophyllus* — literally "nut-leaf," the same Greek root (*karyophyllon*) used for the clove, because the two share a warm, penetrating aroma. Across the pre-modern world the flower was valued less as a food than as a *cordial*: an aromatic prized for lifting the spirits, steadying the heart, and perfuming syrups, wines, and vinegars.

**Assyrian (AT):** Aromatic pinks and their fragrant oils belong to the broad Mesopotamian tradition of scented flower-waters and febrifuge infusions recorded on the cuneiform medical tablets, where sweet-smelling flowers were steeped in wine or oil as heart-strengthening and fever-easing preparations. The carnation's later role as a "wine flower" (its petals floated in festive and medicinal wines) sits directly in this lineage of aromatic cordials.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** The pinks (Greek *Dios anthos*, "flower of Zeus" — the source of the genus name *Dianthus*) appear in the classical materia medica among the fragrant garden flowers used to make perfumed oils and scented wines. Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* treats such aromatic flowers as gently warming and cheering, suited to preparations that comfort a faint or oppressed heart.

**Avicenna (CM):** In the Galenic-Unani system carried through Avicenna's *Qanun*, warm and dry aromatics of this kind are classed as *mufarrih* — "gladdeners of the heart." The clove pink entered European and Islamicate pharmacy as a cordial flower: its conserve and syrup (the *syrupus caryophyllorum* of later European pharmacopoeias) were given for palpitation, faintness, low spirits, and as a mild febrifuge, following exactly the humoral logic Avicenna set out for warm aromatic cardiotonics.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The carnation's doctrine is legible in its chemistry. Its clove-like scent comes from aromatic phenylpropanoid and terpene volatiles — the same class of eugenol-adjacent aromatics that make true clove a warming carminative — which is why every tradition independently filed it as a *warming, heart-cheering* flower rather than a cooling or purgative one. The "flower of Zeus" that gladdens the heart is, pharmacologically, a store of aromatic volatiles and flavonoid pigments; symbol and substance point the same way.

### Active Components

- **Flavonoids / flavonol glycosides** — the genus *Dianthus* is characterised by anthocyanin and flavonol pigments (kaempferol and quercetin glycosides); *D. caryophyllus* is a documented site of flavonol-synthase activity, the enzyme that builds quercetin-type flavonols.
- **Triterpenoid saponins** — a hallmark of the Caryophyllaceae; abundant in the closely related medicinal *Dianthus* species and responsible for their surfactant, mildly diuretic character.
- **Anthocyanin pigments** — pelargonidin/cyanidin glycosides colouring the petals.
- **Aromatic volatiles** — clove-like phenylpropanoid and terpenoid constituents giving the characteristic scent.
- **Cyclic peptides and phenolic acids** — reported across the genus.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Carnation is an **Integration-phase** aromatic: not a driver or a purge but a *cordial* — something taken to settle, gladden, and reconcile the practitioner to the work after the harder tempering has been done. Its traditional indications (faintness, low spirits, palpitation, mild fever) map onto its use in Kenetica as a gentle heart-and-spirit steadier, most naturally deployed as a scented syrup, conserve, or petal-wine at the close of a cycle. It is emblematic rather than heroic: a fragrance-led tonic whose value is as much in the ritual of the aromatic draught as in any strong pharmacological push.

### Scientific Correlation

Direct clinical pharmacology on *Dianthus caryophyllus* itself is **sparse** — most modern data concern medicinal congeners in the same genus, and should be read as suggestive of a shared chemistry rather than proof for the carnation specifically.

- According to PubMed, Liu et al. (2022), *Chinese Medicine* — a comprehensive review of *Dianthi herba* (the medicinal *Dianthus superbus*/*chinensis*) — catalogues ~194 compounds dominated by triterpenoid saponins, flavonoids, and volatile oils, and reports antiviral, anticancer, antioxidant and antimicrobial activities for the genus. DOI: [10.1186/s13020-022-00570-2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13020-022-00570-2)
- According to PubMed, Kim et al. (2019), *Food and Chemical Toxicology* — showed that flavonol glycosides from *Dianthus superbus* (notably quercetin-3-rutinoside and isorhamnetin-3-glucoside) drive antioxidant and anti-influenza (neuraminidase-inhibiting) activity in vitro, with strong antiviral IC values against influenza A and B. DOI: [10.1016/j.fct.2019.01.013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fct.2019.01.013)
- According to PubMed, Rosdianto et al. (2020), *Pakistan Journal of Biological Sciences* — cites *Dianthus caryophyllus* specifically as a documented site of flavonol-synthase (FLS) expression, the enzyme catalysing quercetin-type flavonol formation, confirming the carnation's own flavonol-producing capacity. DOI: [10.3923/pjbs.2020.264.270](https://doi.org/10.3923/pjbs.2020.264.270)

**Evidence grade: TRADITIONAL (weak-modern).** The cordial/febrifuge use rests on classical and Unani textual tradition; modern pharmacology supports flavonoid-driven antioxidant/antiviral potential *for the genus*, but species-specific human evidence for *D. caryophyllus* as a medicine is essentially absent. Claims should stay genus-level and traditional.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani (as attested):** Warm and dry (2nd degree); *mufarrih al-qalb* — a gladdener of the heart; cordial and mildly febrifuge.
- **Ayurvedic (by analogy, not classically attested for this species):** would read as *katu/tikta* (pungent-bitter) rasa, *ushna* (heating) virya — offered only as a cross-tradition analogy, not as a Sushruta citation.
- No attested Chinese (Shennong) classification for *D. caryophyllus* itself; the TCM "Qumai" drug is the related *D. superbus/chinensis*, not the carnation.

### Contraindications

- Pregnancy and lactation: avoid medicinal/concentrated preparations — saponin-bearing *Dianthus* species (e.g. Qumai) are traditionally regarded as emmenagogue/uterine-active and are contraindicated in pregnancy; extend the same caution to carnation.
- Possible allergy/contact sensitivity to Caryophyllaceae; ornamental cut-flower carnations may carry pesticide residues and are not food-grade.
- Saponin content: large quantities may irritate the gut. Keep to culinary/cordial amounts.
- Do not substitute for the TCM drug Qumai — different species, different indications.
