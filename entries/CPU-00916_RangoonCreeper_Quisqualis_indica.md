# Rangoon Creeper / Shi Jun Zi

## *Combretum indicum* (L.) DeFilipps (syn. *Quisqualis indica* L.) | Family: Combretaceae

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Warm / Sweet (attributed; Chinese materia medica)
**Training Phase:** Foundation — Signature Study

---

### Origin & Tradition

Rangoon creeper is a vigorous woody climber of tropical and subtropical Asia, cultivated across India, southern China and South-East Asia for its spectacular clusters of tubular flowers that open white and, over a day or two, deepen through pink to crimson. That colour-shift is not a footnote — it is one of the few things about this plant that has been chemically pinned down (see Active Components), and it is the reason the vine is planted on so many verandas. The name that scientists gave the genus, *Quisqualis*, is itself a small joke: Latin *quis? qualis?* — "who? what kind?" — a botanist's shrug at a plant whose habit and flowers seemed to change identity as it grew. Modern taxonomy has folded the genus into *Combretum*, so the correct current binomial is *Combretum indicum*, with *Quisqualis indica* retained as the familiar synonym.

**Shennong / Chinese materia medica (SBJ).** In Chinese medicine the roasted ripe seeds are the drug **Shi Jun Zi** (使君子, "Envoy Jun's seeds"), one of the classic paediatric **anthelmintics** — given to expel roundworm (*Ascaris*) and pinworm from children, and to treat the malnourished, pot-bellied "childhood accumulation" (*gan ji*) that worm burden produces. The drug is characteristically **sweet and warm**, which in the tradition's own logic is unusual for a vermifuge and is precisely why it was favoured for children: sweet-warm agents were held to be gentle on the young digestion. It is typically given lightly roasted and in a strictly limited number of seeds.

**Sushruta / Ayurveda (SS).** The same anthelmintic reputation appears in the South-Asian materia medica, where the plant (Sanskrit *Rangoon-ki-bel* in later usage) is used against intestinal worms and, folklorically, for digestive complaints and skin conditions. **[USER: verify]** — the specific attestation of *this* species in the classical Sushruta Samhita text should be checked; the anthelmintic use is securely traditional across the region, but the direct Sushruta citation is carried on later Ayurveda practice rather than a verified passage.

**Doctrinal & symbolic layer.** The flower that changes colour as it ages made the vine an emblem of transformation and of the passage from innocence (white) to maturity (red) — apt for a remedy of childhood. In Kenetica it sits in Foundation as a study in how a plant's *most conspicuous* trait (the colour change) and its *most used* property (the vermifuge seed) can be two entirely separate stories that the tradition nonetheless binds into one plant-identity.

### Active Components

- **Anthocyanins (flower).** The white-to-red transformation is driven by the **temporal accumulation of cyanidin 3-*O*-glucoside** as the major anthocyanin in the petals, confirmed by HPLC-DAD with authentic standards; the red-stage petals showed the highest antioxidant (DPPH/FRAP/ABTS) activity (Ghissing et al., 2021). This is real, species-specific chemistry.
- **Quisqualic acid (seed) — the famous constituent, correctly framed.** The seeds are the historical source of **quisqualic acid**, and the plant is the compound's *namesake*. In neuroscience quisqualic acid is a potent **excitatory amino acid**: a full agonist at **AMPA-type glutamate receptors** (and group-I metabotropic glutamate receptors), used as a pharmacological tool and, at high exposure, an **excitotoxin**. Its AMPA-receptor pharmacology is well characterised — it is among the higher-potency full agonists at GluA channels (Zhang et al., 2006; Clements et al., 1998; Shreve & Uretsky, 1988). **Framing caveat:** this neuropharmacology is *molecule-level* work on isolated quisqualic acid, not a study of the whole roasted seed as swallowed; it explains *why* the plant carries a neurotoxic ceiling, not that ordinary culinary-scale use reliably produces central effects. It is named here as compound data, not as a species-specific clinical claim.
- **Other constituents.** The genus *Combretum* is rich in phenolics, tannins and triterpenoids; the seed's traditional anthelmintic action is usually attributed to quisqualic acid and potassium quisqualate together with the crude seed matrix, but a clean isolated mechanism of worm expulsion has not been established in the retrieved literature.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica, Rangoon creeper is positioned as a **traditional anthelmintic of dose-limited character** — a plant whose usefulness and whose danger arise from the *same* chemistry, and which therefore teaches the practitioner about the narrow therapeutic window.

1. **The vermifuge use (traditional, dose-bounded).** The roasted seed's reputation for expelling roundworm and pinworm in children is broad and old across Chinese and South-Asian practice. Kenetica records it as **traditional**: the retrieved modern literature does not provide a controlled anthelmintic efficacy trial, so the claim is graded on ethnomedical usage, not on measured cure rates.
2. **A modern, unexpected preclinical signal.** The best recent *experimental* data on the plant are not about worms at all: *Quisqualis indica* seed extract reduced prostate weight, dihydrotestosterone and 5α-reductase expression, and improved lower-urinary-tract pressure dynamics, in **testosterone-induced benign prostatic hyperplasia rat models**, with supporting anti-proliferative/pro-apoptotic effects in prostate-cancer cell lines (Kim et al., 2020; Wijerathne et al., 2017). This is **preclinical, animal/in-vitro only** — reported here as a live research direction, not a human indication.
3. **The teaching point.** The compound that makes the seed a medicine (quisqualic acid) is also a documented excitotoxin. The tradition's own insistence on a *small, counted* number of seeds and its warnings around overconsumption are, read forward, a folk expression of a real toxicological ceiling. Deflation stated plainly: **there is no verified modern efficacy trial for the anthelmintic use, and the neuroactive constituent sets a hard safety limit** — this is not a plant to scale up on enthusiasm.

### Preparation & Dosing

Provided for the historical/traditional record only. **Kenetica does not endorse self-treatment of parasitic infection with this seed**; confirmed worm infection has safe, standard pharmacological treatment.

| Preparation | Part | Traditional handling | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roasted seed (Shi Jun Zi) | Ripe seed kernel | Lightly dry-roasted, a small counted number taken on an empty stomach | Strictly dose-limited; **[USER: verify]** — no standardised modern dose; overconsumption is the documented hazard |
| Decoction | Seed / fruit | Simmered | Traditional; efficacy unverified |

Traditional caution, widely repeated: do **not** take the seeds with hot tea, and do not exceed the counted dose — folk sources tie both to hiccups, dizziness and vomiting. This caution is **traditional**, consistent with the neuroactive constituent, and should be treated as a real limit.

### Scientific Correlation

Graded honestly, nulls included:

- **Anthelmintic (roundworm/pinworm) efficacy:** **Traditional — not established in retrieved trials.** The use is broad and old, but no controlled human anthelmintic study was retrieved.
- **Benign prostatic hyperplasia / 5α-reductase:** **Preclinical, promising.** Rat and cell-line data show 5α-reductase inhibition, DHT reduction and anti-proliferative/pro-apoptotic effects (Kim et al., 2020; Wijerathne et al., 2017). No human data.
- **Antioxidant (flower anthocyanin):** **Preclinical, moderate.** Cyanidin-3-*O*-glucoside accumulation tracks antioxidant capacity, peaking at the red flower stage (Ghissing et al., 2021).
- **Quisqualic acid neuropharmacology:** **Established at the molecule level.** Potent AMPA/mGluR agonist and excitotoxic tool (Zhang et al., 2006; Clements et al., 1998; Shreve & Uretsky, 1988). Basis of the toxicity ceiling; not a therapeutic claim.
- **Photoallergic potential:** **Documented case.** The flower has caused **photoallergic contact dermatitis** in a gardener (Ngarmjiratam & Wattanakrai, 2016).

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Sweet and warm, entering the Spleen and Stomach channels in the Chinese scheme — the "gentle-on-children" profile that made it the paediatric worm remedy of choice. The energetic reading is coherent with the tradition; the sweetness is a genuine sensory trait of the roasted seed, not merely a doctrinal assignment.

### Contraindications

- **Dose ceiling is the governing safety fact.** Do not exceed the small traditional seed count. Overconsumption is associated with hiccups, dizziness, vomiting and — given the excitotoxic quisqualic acid content — a real neurotoxic risk.
- **Do not combine the seeds with hot tea/hot drinks** (a consistent traditional caution linked to adverse reactions).
- **Not for unsupervised paediatric self-treatment.** Confirmed worm infection should be treated with standard, dosed anthelmintics; a child is exactly the population in which an overdose is most dangerous.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** avoid — no safety data and a neuroactive constituent.
- **Contact/photoallergic dermatitis** can occur from handling the flowers in sunlight (documented case).
- **Correct identification and preparation required**; the neuroactive seed is not an ornamental-flower substitute.
