# Tamarisk / Manna Plant

## *Tamarix gallica* L. | Family: Tamaricaceae

**Tradition Tags:** EP, AT, DMM, CM, SBJ, SS

**Energetic Classification:** Cool, Dry (Second Degree) — Astringent, Binding, Descending

**Training Phase:** Consolidation — for the practitioner whose tissue is lax, whose fluids leak, whose ground will not hold.

---

### Origin & Tradition

The tamarisk is a tree of the margins. It grows where nothing serious grows: salt flats, wadi beds, the brackish rim of the desert where the water table is poison to anything with ordinary roots. It survives by *excreting* salt — through specialised glands on its scale-like leaves it pushes brine out onto its own surface, so that at dawn the foliage is faintly crystalline and, in the right season, faintly sweet. This single physiological fact generated most of the tree's doctrine across every tradition that met it. A plant that drinks bitterness and secretes sweetness is not, symbolically, a neutral object.

**Egyptian & Near Eastern Practice (EP)**
Tamarisk (*jsr* in Egyptian usage is more usually applied to *T. aphylla* and allied species; the *gallica*/*aphylla* boundary is not reliably resolvable in the ancient sources — **[USER: verify]** any specific species attribution here) was a temple and funerary timber, valued because it is one of the very few substantial woods obtainable in a desert without importing cedar. The wood was worked into implements and coffin furniture; the tree was associated with the Osiris cycle, the tree that grows over the buried god and encloses him. The doctrinal move is legible: a tree that thrives in the salt-dead ground becomes the tree that holds the dead and returns them. Medically, Near Eastern practice used tamarisk galls — the hard insect-induced swellings on the twigs — as a binding agent for flux and for eye complaints. The galls are the most tannin-dense material the tree produces, and this is not a coincidence the traditions could name but is one they could *taste*.

**Greek & Arabic-Galenic Tradition (AT / DMM)**
Dioscorides treats tamarisk (*myrike*) in the *De Materia Medica* as a decoction of leaf and bark taken in wine, and his flagship indication is the **hardened spleen** — splenomegaly, the great swollen organ of the malarial Mediterranean. Galen accepted and systematised this. The tree was classified as cooling and drying, cutting and attenuating, and thereby capable of dissolving indurated organ tissue. A related folk-practical claim, which recurs into the Renaissance, is that vessels turned from tamarisk wood were themselves splenic medicine — drink from a tamarisk cup and the spleen shrinks. This claim is doctrinally coherent and pharmacologically empty; see the deflation below.

Arabic practice (*tarfa* / *athl*) inherited the Galenic splenic indication and extended it, using decoctions as a mouthwash for lax and bleeding gums, as a wash for weeping skin, and internally for diarrhoea and for what the sources describe as jaundice and liver heat. The *manna* obtained from Near Eastern tamarisks was collected separately and treated as a gentle laxative and demulcent — a different drug entirely from the astringent bark, and worth keeping distinct.

**Chinese Practice (SBJ)**
*Cheng liu* (檉柳), the tamarisk twig, is used in Chinese materia medica as an acrid, sweet, neutral-to-warm agent that **releases the exterior and vents rash** — the classical indication is measles that will not surface, where the eruption is stuck under the skin and the patient is not clearing. This is a genuinely different doctrine from the Mediterranean one: not binding but *dispersing*, not descending but *outward-moving*. Note that the species conventionally used in Chinese practice is *T. chinensis* Lour., not *T. gallica*; the two are congeners with substantially overlapping chemistry but they are not interchangeable, and the venting indication should not be transferred to *T. gallica* without evidence. **[USER: verify]** before any clinical substitution.

**Biblical / Levantine Stratum (SBJ)**
Abraham plants a tamarisk at Beersheba (Gen. 21:33) and Saul is buried under one at Jabesh (1 Sam. 31:13) — the tree marks covenant and marks graves, consistent with its Egyptian valence. But the claim that draws most attention is the **manna** identification. The proposal, developed in the twentieth century from Bodenheimer's Sinai fieldwork, is that the Sinai "manna" is the honeydew excreted by scale insects (*Trabutina mannipara* and allies) feeding on tamarisk sap: the insects take in far more phloem sugar than they can metabolise and void the excess, which crystallises in the dry night air into pale sweet grains that must be gathered before the sun and the ants take them.

This must be stated with the right grade of confidence. The Sinai tamarisk exudate **is real** — it is a documented, collectable, edible, largely sucrose/fructose/glucose substance, and its gathering behaviour (collect at dawn, spoils by day) matches the Exodus text with uncomfortable neatness. Whether it *is* the manna of the narrative is a historical and literary question that no botanical fact can settle, and the honest position is that tamarisk honeydew is a **strong candidate**, not an identification. Two further cautions. First, the quantities recorded in modern Sinai are small — a few hundred kilograms across a good season for the whole peninsula — which does not obviously feed a nation, and this is the standing objection to the theory. Second, and more important for this codex: **tamarisk manna is not the same substance as the manna of European pharmacy.** That manna is the dried exudate of *Fraxinus ornus* L., the manna ash (see cross-reference below), and its active principle is **mannitol**, a sugar alcohol which is a real osmotic laxative. Tamarisk honeydew is insect-processed phloem sugar and does not act this way. The two "mannas" share a name and nothing else. Conflating them is the single most common error in the literature on this plant.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

The tamarisk's doctrine is *the transmutation of the ground*. Every tradition read the same gesture: the tree stands in salt and produces sweetness; it accepts what would kill others and gives back what feeds them. From this the funerary symbolism (that which holds corruption and yields life), the covenant symbolism (the tree that endures where nothing endures), and the manna narrative (bread from the wasteland) all follow with almost no argument required. It is one of the cleanest cases in the codex of a whole symbolic complex generated by one visible physiological trait.

Now map it onto the chemistry, which is where the doctrine partly earns its keep and partly does not.

The **astringency doctrine is chemically true**. Tamarisk is a tannin plant. The Galenic reading — cooling, drying, binding, closing the leaking tissue — is a phenomenological description of hydrolysable tannins and gallic-acid derivatives precipitating surface proteins. The tree loads polyphenols heavily, plausibly because a halophyte living under permanent osmotic and oxidative stress needs a large radical-quenching reserve; the salt that makes the symbolism makes the chemistry. When the tradition says "this closes what is open," it is naming tannin-protein complexation in the only vocabulary it had. This is doctrine that resolves to mechanism.

The **splenic doctrine is chemically hollow** at the strength it was asserted. A tannin astringent does not dissolve an enlarged spleen, and a *cup turned from the wood* certainly does not: the sympathetic logic — the tree that survives the swollen poisoned ground treats the swollen poisoned organ — is the tell of a doctrine reasoned from resemblance and then defended by authority for fifteen centuries. That the underlying splenomegaly was usually malarial makes the whole indication a misfire on the target, however elegant the reasoning. Deflate it. Keep the astringency, discard the spleen.

The **manna doctrine is a category error** dressed as a pharmacology. Whatever the Sinai exudate is, it is a food, not a drug, and the codex's laxative manna is a different plant.

### Active Components

- **Hydrolysable tannins and gallotannins** — the dominant class; concentrated in bark, twig and especially gall. Direct basis of the astringent action.
- **Gallic acid and ellagic acid derivatives** — breakdown and constituent units of the tannin fraction; potent radical scavengers.
- **Flavonoids** — HPLC of *T. gallica* leaf and flower extracts identifies **catechin, isorhamnetin-3-O-glucoside and isoquercetin** as major phenolics in 70% methanolic extract (Zar Kalai et al., *Int J Environ Health Res*, 2023; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/09603123.2023.2256679)). Quercetin derivatives recur across the genus.
- **Salt-gland exudate** — NaCl and mixed mineral salts on the leaf surface; pharmacologically inert but the origin of the sweetness/bitterness symbolism.
- **Insect honeydew (seasonal, Sinai/Near East)** — sucrose, fructose, glucose. **Not mannitol.** No laxative principle.

Content is highly variable by organ, season, provenance and extraction solvent — leaf and flower carry the highest phenolic load, and ethanol/70% methanol extract substantially more than water.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Tamarisk is a **consolidation** agent. Its work is on the practitioner whose boundaries have gone slack — gut that will not hold, gums that bleed, skin that weeps, tissue that has lost its tone. It does not build. It does not move. It closes.

The training-phase logic follows the tree: before you can grow in difficult ground, you must stop losing what you take in. Tamarisk is prescribed at the point where intake is adequate and retention is not. Its symbolic instruction to the practitioner is exactness about the ground — the tamarisk does not pretend the salt is not there; it processes it, continuously, at cost, and this is the posture the consolidation phase asks for.

Use it topically and locally in preference to systemically. The astringency is a surface phenomenon and it is where the evidence lives.

**Cultivator's note:** *T. gallica* propagates readily from hardwood cuttings and tolerates saline and alkaline soil that will kill almost anything else — this is precisely why it is an aggressive invasive across the American southwest as saltcedar, where it excretes salt into the topsoil and suppresses the natives. **Do not plant it outside its native range.** Where it is already established, harvest it freely; the plant is a documented heavy-metal bioindicator and accumulator in polluted riparian zones (Mherzi et al., *Environ Monit Assess*, 2020; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10661-020-8205-6)), so **do not harvest from wastewater margins, roadsides or contaminated wadis** — the same salt-tolerance that makes it grow there makes it take up what is there. Harvest twig bark in autumn; leaf and flower at full bloom.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the evidence base for *T. gallica* is **preclinical only** — in vitro assays and rodent models. There is no clinical trial literature. Grade accordingly.

**Antioxidant activity — GRADE: WELL-SUPPORTED (in vitro).** Consistently and independently replicated. Methanol extract of Algerian *T. gallica* aerial parts showed DPPH IC₅₀ 14.05 ± 0.66 µg/mL with total phenolics 190.27 ± 0.74 mg GAE/g (Lefahal et al., *Comb Chem High Throughput Screen*, 2021; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2174/1386207323666201204141541)). Ethanolic extract likewise gave the strongest DPPH/FRAP/ABTS activity of three solvents tested, correlating directly with total phenolic and flavonoid content (Nisar et al., *Exp Biol Med*, 2022; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/15353702221139208)). This is a real and reproducible property of the polyphenol fraction. It is also a property of most tannin-rich plants and should not be over-read as therapeutic.

**Anti-inflammatory activity — GRADE: PRELIMINARY.** The 70% methanolic leaf extract showed "considerable" anti-inflammatory activity alongside its phenolic profile (Zar Kalai et al., 2023; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/09603123.2023.2256679)). In alloxan-diabetic rats, a methanolic aerial-part fraction at 500 mg/kg significantly reduced TNF-α (1.28 ± 0.13 g/L) and IL-6 (98 ± 10.4 pg/L) (Malik et al., *Arch Physiol Biochem*, 2020; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/13813455.2020.1788099)). Rodent, single lab, high dose. Directionally consistent with the traditional anti-inflammatory use; nowhere near clinical.

**Antidiabetic / enzyme inhibition — GRADE: PRELIMINARY.** Ethanolic extract inhibited α-amylase (IC₅₀ 1.116 ± 0.051) and α-glucosidase (IC₅₀ 0.402 ± 0.2) (Nisar et al., 2022; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/15353702221139208)); methanolic extract gave α-amylase IC₅₀ 18.75 µg/mL and reduced alloxan-induced hyperglycaemia with improvement in lipid profile, CRP, LFTs and RFTs (Malik et al., 2020; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/13813455.2020.1788099)). Note that polyphenol-rich extracts routinely inhibit these enzymes in vitro; this is a class effect, not a distinguishing property of tamarisk.

**Photoprotection — GRADE: PRELIMINARY (in vitro).** Methanol extract and ethyl acetate fraction gave spectrophotometric SPF values of 37.03 ± 0.22 and 36.08 ± 0.03 respectively in the UV-B region (Lefahal et al., 2021; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2174/1386207323666201204141541)). This is a **spectroscopic** SPF, measured on an extract in a cuvette, not an in-vivo sun protection factor on human skin. The gap between those two numbers is usually large. Interesting; not a sunscreen.

**Hepatoprotection — GRADE: TRADITIONAL.** The plant is described in the literature as "a halophytic medicinal shrub traditionally used in the treatment of liver disorders" (Zar Kalai et al., 2023), and the rodent work reports improved LFTs in a diabetic model — but a dedicated hepatoprotection study for *T. gallica* did not surface in this run. A direct PubMed search for *Tamarix gallica* hepatoprotective polyphenol antioxidant returned **zero results**. The classical liver/spleen indication, which is the plant's flagship traditional use, is therefore **unsupported by any evidence retrieved here**. This is a null and it is reported as one.

**Splenic indication — GRADE: DEFLATED.** No evidence. No mechanism. The Dioscoridean indication and the tamarisk-cup practice should be treated as historically important and clinically void.

**Tamarisk manna as laxative — GRADE: DEFLATED (category error).** The laxative manna of pharmacy is *Fraxinus ornus* mannitol. Tamarisk honeydew is not mannitol and has no such action.

The bulk of the modern *T. gallica* literature is not medical at all — it concerns the plant as a phytoremediation species, a salt-stress rhizosphere model (Bakelli et al., *Curr Microbiol*, 2025; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00284-025-04217-x)), a constructed-wetland macrophyte (Ennabili & Radoux, *J Environ Manage*, 2020; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvman.2020.111806)), and a feedstock for activated carbon (Benaoune et al., *Materials*, 2025; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ma18030511)). That is an honest summary of where the science is: the world is more interested in what tamarisk does to soil than in what it does to people.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

| System | Classification |
|---|---|
| Galenic / Unani | Cold and dry in the second degree; astringent (*qābiḍ*), attenuating, opening obstructions of spleen and liver |
| Ayurvedic (*jhau*) | Kashaya (astringent) rasa; sheeta virya; kapha–pitta reducing, vata-aggravating in excess |
| Chinese (*cheng liu*, *T. chinensis*) | Acrid, sweet, neutral; Lung, Stomach, Heart channels; releases exterior, vents rash — **doctrinally opposite to the Western reading** |
| Kenetica | Cool, dry, descending, binding. Consolidation-phase. Closes the boundary; does not build the interior. |

The Chinese/Galenic conflict is not resolvable and should not be smoothed over. They are describing different species, different plant parts, different preparations and different clinical targets. A codex that pretends they agree is lying.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian & Near Eastern Practice (EP).** The tamarisk's Egyptian standing is funerary and cosmological, not cultivational. In the Osiris cycle the tree grows over and encloses the dead god — the version best known to us is Plutarch's *De Iside et Osiride* (c. 45–120 CE), where the coffin lodges in a tree at Byblos that grows around it and is later cut into the palace pillar (the plant is given as tamarisk or *erica*; the identification is Plutarch's Greek testimony about an Egyptian myth, not an Egyptian source). This is myth and temple-timber symbolism, recorded historically here; it is not a *heka* intervention on a practitioner's constitution, and the corpus is indication-organised in any case, so a vital-constitution reading would be a modern imposition. **(c) absent; the funerary association is genuine but is symbolism, not cultivation status.**

**Assyro-Babylonian (AT).** Tamarisk (Akkadian *bīnu*) has a secure and named place in Mesopotamian purification: pure tamarisk wood is the material of the purifying figurines in *bīt mēseri*, and the tree belongs to the ritual-cleansing complex of the *āšipu*, of which *bīt rimki*, the royal "house of ablution," is the classic study (Læssøe, *Studies on the Assyrian Ritual and Series Bît Rimki*, 1955). That ritual-purificatory role is attested and sourceable. But it is a cultivation status only in the sense that the substance is classified by the *agency it opposes* — pollution, curse, portended evil — not by any vital endowment it builds in a person; and the standing blocking warning on cuneiform substance-and-tablet claims counsels caution even on the identification. So: the purification role is recorded historically and cited; the cultivation status proper is **(c) absent.**

**Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* offers no subtle-body system to house the tamarisk, and its flagship indication — the hardened spleen — is a therapeutic target, not a cultivation status; the tamarisk-cup practice built on it is sympathetic reasoning, deflated elsewhere in this entry. What the Greek stream genuinely gives the cultivator here is a discipline of attention: distinguishing the astringent bark and tannin-dense gall from the sweet insect *manna*, and either from the mannitol *manna* of *Fraxinus* — the exact provenance-and-identity work Dioscorides models. That is method, not vital constitution. **(c) absent, framed as practitioner discipline.**

**Avicenna & the Unani stream (CM).** The cultivational heart of this tradition is the conservation of *innate heat* and *radical moisture*, the finite endowment whose depletion is ageing. Tamarisk (*tarfa* / *athl*) is classed cold and dry in the second degree, an astringent (*qābiḍ*) taken to bind flux and reduce the indurated spleen and liver — a cooling, drying, corrective drug, not a warming conservation of the innate heat. No conservational status is attested for it; it acts on an organ complaint, not on the vital endowment. **(c) absent.**

**Chinese Practice (SBJ).** *Cheng liu* (檉柳) carries no *san pin* grade and its documented function — releasing the exterior and venting stuck rash (measles that will not surface) — is outward-dispersing, disease-clearing work, the opposite of the conservational *yang sheng* project. Two further cautions bar any cultivation claim: the drug of Chinese practice is *T. chinensis*, not the *T. gallica* of this entry, and its doctrine is, as the entry states, doctrinally opposite to the Western reading. A dispersing corrective, not a nourishing-life drug, and not securely this species. **(c) absent.**

**Sushruta & Ayurveda (SS).** Tamarisk (*jhau*) is placed by *kashaya* (astringent) *rasa* and *sheeta virya* — a therapeutic-energetic assignment that reduces kapha and pitta and, in excess, aggravates vata. It is not attested among the *rasayana* or *vajikarana* drugs and no *ojas*-related action is recorded for it; its whole action is drying and binding, which is corrective of a lax, leaking tissue rather than cultivational. The frame is present, the cultivation status is not. **(b) framed — classified energetically, no *rasayana* status attested.**

**Practitioner's note.** Tamarisk is a consolidation drug, used topically and in short internal courses to close a boundary that is leaking — bleeding gums, weeping skin, lax gut — and then stopped; its own tannin chemistry makes sustained high-dose internal use a hepatotoxic hazard, so long-term "tonic" use is contraindicated by the plant itself. The genuinely cultivational work it asks of a practitioner is not ingestion but discrimination: keeping its three look-alike products apart (astringent bark, sweet tamarisk honeydew, and the unrelated mannitol *manna* of *Fraxinus*), and refusing contaminated provenance in a species that concentrates whatever the polluted ground holds.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy and lactation — avoid.** No safety data. Astringent, and the anti-inflammatory rodent work used doses with no reproductive-tox screen.
- **Constipation, dry constitutions, tissue depletion — avoid.** The plant's entire action is drying and binding. It is contraindicated by its own indication.
- **Anaemia and mineral deficiency** — tannins chelate non-haem iron and reduce its absorption. Separate from iron-containing meals and supplements by at least two hours.
- **Chronic high-dose internal use — avoid.** Hydrolysable tannins in sustained high dose carry hepatotoxic potential. There is a real irony in a traditional "liver tonic" whose principal constituent class is, at dose, hepatotoxic. Keep internal courses short.
- **Diabetes medication** — the α-amylase/α-glucosidase inhibition is real in vitro; theoretical additive hypoglycaemia with acarbose or sulfonylureas. Untested in humans.
- **Contaminated provenance** — the plant accumulates heavy metals from polluted riparian soil. Provenance is a safety issue for this species specifically, not a purity nicety.
- **Species substitution** — do not use *T. gallica* for a *T. chinensis* (*cheng liu*) indication or the reverse.
- **Ecological contraindication** — do not introduce to non-native range.
