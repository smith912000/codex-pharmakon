# Adhatoda vasica — ba.sha.ka, Bhashaka, "Malabar Nut Tree" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Adhatoda vasica, the first of two binomials Clark 1995 prints for ba.sha.ka (with Veronica ciliata Fisch.), and the English "Malabar Nut Tree" he prints; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints "Justicia Adhatoda" in its scientific-name column* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts in this row.** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and the row's binomial is the first of two on Clark's line (the second belongs to a different family of plant). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 975).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads: "ba.sha.ka: Malabar Nut Tree: Adhatoda vasica: Veronica ciliata Fisch: [has twelve names. It grows in forested south-facing valleys, has a large trunk, thick leaves and bright pale yellow flowers, with bitter taste and cool power. Of the many types the white, red and blue ones are superior and a mediocre type is found in Tibet]. It cures blood fevers, [poisoning, painful infectious fevers, liver diseases and bile disorders]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". So the twelve names, the habitat and description, the bitter taste and cool power, the ranking of the white, red and blue types as "superior" and the Tibetan type as "mediocre", and the further indications (poisoning, painful infectious fevers, liver diseases, bile disorders) are the commentary layer. What stands outside the brackets is three words of action: the drug "cures blood fevers". The root-text layer, as Clark gives it, names the drug and one indication, and no taste or power.

Three more Clark lines name the drug. Line 768, in the exposition of tastes, reads: "The group of medicines having bitter taste includes neem, Indian gentian, Aconitum balfouri, Picrorhiza kurroa, bitter cucumber, Conessi (Kurchi), musk, (animal) bile, Indian barberry, Malabar nut tree, mineral exudate, Gentiana straminea, Corydalis edulis, etc." In chapter 21, "The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines" (heading at line 1204), line 1210 reads: "The class of medicines which cure blood disorders consists of Red Sandalwood, Caraganajubata, Catechu tree, Picrorhiza, Vasaka (Malabar Nut tree), Emblic Myrobalan, Corydalis edulis, Pterocephalus hookeri, Madder and Bastard Teak." Line 1383, a passage on supplementary ingredients for dual and triple humoral disorders, says "Adhatoda vasica and Picrorhiza kurroa for blood disorders". Clark's name index prints "Adhatoda vasica" (line 1554) and "Malabar Nut tree" (line 1611); the Tibetan-name index line that would hold *ba.sha.ka* (line 1722) does not print it in the lines read. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a bitter, cool-powered tree that cures blood fevers (Clark line 975; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3423), that is listed among the blood-disorder medicines (Clark line 1210; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3598) and, in Clark's commentary layer, also against poisoning, painful infectious fevers, liver diseases and bile disorders. These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. The botanical identity is uncertain between two binomials, and the Codex gives no protocol.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (inside the brackets of line 975) prints "bitter taste and cool power". The root-text list at line 768 and the Men-Tsee-Khang list at line 3213 place it among the bitter-tasting substances. No other quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes the tree by its taste and what it does: bitter, cool, curing blood fevers (outside the brackets, line 975), and it stands in the bitter-taste group (line 768) and the blood-disorder class (line 1210). The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this entry places ba.sha.ka in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. In the lines read, the essence-extraction mentions in the herb section are for other plants (for example at lines 971 and 1121), and ba.sha.ka is not named there. Its framing is therapeutic only. The ranking of types as "superior" and "mediocre" in line 975 is a ranking of quality of the drug inside Clark's commentary layer and not a grade of cultivation.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a blood-fever and blood-disorder drug in the Tibetan texts, and nothing more can be said from them. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. The botanical identity is uncertain (two binomials in Clark), so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Fevers and blood disorders need medical assessment. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
