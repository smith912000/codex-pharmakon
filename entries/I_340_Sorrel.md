# I-340: Rumex acetosa (Common Sorrel)

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary name:** Common sorrel  
**Botanical name:** *Rumex acetosa* L.  
**Family:** Polygonaceae  
**Synonyms:** Garden sorrel, bread-and-cheese plant (UK folk name), sour grass, green sauce  
**Traditional names:** Oseille (French); Sauerampfer (German — "sour dock"); Acedera (Spanish); Schav (Yiddish — for sorrel soup); Chukar (Hindi); Hyssop sorrel (obsolete English)

**Botanical description:** Perennial herb 30–100 cm; basal leaves arrow-shaped (sagittate), smooth, pale green, characteristic sour taste (oxalic acid); stem leaves sessile, clasping; flowers in whorled panicles, reddish-green, dioecious (separate male and female plants); fruit small, 3-angled achene. Deeply rooted perennial; returns every spring.

**Related pharmacobotanically important species:**
- *Rumex crispus* (Yellow dock): root highest anthraquinone content in genus; the principal medicinal Rumex
- *Rumex obtusifolius* (Broad-leaved dock): classic nettle sting remedy (applied fresh leaf); folk medicine widespread
- *Rumex hymenosepalus* (Canaigre): high tannin root; alternative tannin source; US Southwest indigenous use
- *Oxalis acetosella* (Wood sorrel, Oxalidaceae — different family): also oxalic acid-containing; similar sour taste; traditional fever remedy; NOT botanically related to Rumex

**Part used:** Leaves (culinary + minor medicinal); root (primarily *R. crispus* — yellow dock, highest anthraquinones)

## 3. Convergence Analysis

**Pattern:** The *Rumex* genus — docks and sorrels — appears across Eurasian traditions in a consistent pattern: sour, cooling, digestive, and mild laxative. The taste itself (oxalic acid) was recognized across traditions as a "cooling" quality (consistent with Galenic temperament theory and Ayurvedic virya classification), and the astringent-plus-laxative dual character of the root (tannins + anthraquinones in a lower ratio than rhubarb) generated consistent therapeutic applications.

Dioscorides' "Lapathon" encompasses the broader Rumex complex; the Islamic tradition's ḥummâḍ (sourness) as a cooling digestive aligns pharmacologically. The dock and sorrel family had a further cultural role as spring food across Northern Europe — providing vitamin C after winter scurvy-prone months (similar to nettle and dandelion). This food-medicine integration is particularly evident in French cuisine (oseille sorrel soup; crème vichyssoise with sorrel) and Ashkenazi Jewish cuisine (schav = cold sorrel soup with egg and sour cream).

**Yellow dock (*R. crispus*) distinction:** While common sorrel (*R. acetosa*) is predominantly culinary with minor medicinal use, yellow dock root contains higher concentrations of anthraquinone glycosides (emodin, chrysophanol, physcion) and iron-rich compounds. Yellow dock has been used specifically as an "alterative" in 19th-century American botanical medicine (Eclectic tradition) for skin diseases, liver conditions, and anemia (iron content + improved GI absorption via anthraquinone-stimulated colonic motility).

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

### Oxalic Acid — Primary Organoleptic and Toxicological Significance
- Produces sour, acidic taste
- Forms calcium oxalate crystals (insoluble) in colon → calcium malabsorption with HIGH sorrel intake
- At extreme doses: acute oxalate nephropathy (hypercalciuria, renal tubular obstruction)
- At culinary doses: safe; calcium oxalate forms in gut (not absorbed in significant amounts)

### Yellow Dock Root — Anthraquinone Mechanism
- Same stimulant laxative mechanism as rhubarb (I-339) but MILDER (lower anthraquinone %)
- Tannin:anthraquinone balance similar to rhubarb → similar biphasic dose response at different dose thresholds
- Anti-inflammatory (emodin, quercetin): NF-κB inhibition

### Iron in Yellow Dock — Nutritional Context
- Historical Eclectic botanical claim: yellow dock "builds the blood" — attributed to iron content
- Modern pharmacological analysis: iron in yellow dock is chelated with oxalic acid → poorly absorbed
- However: bile acid secretion-stimulating effects from anthraquinones may improve overall GI iron absorption environment
- CURRENT assessment: yellow dock is NOT a reliable iron supplement; anemia requires pharmaceutical iron + dietary optimization

## 6. Clinical Evidence

### Constipation — Level C (yellow dock root)

No RCTs specifically for *Rumex crispus*. Evidence extrapolated from:
- Anthraquinone class evidence (as per I-339 rhubarb; Level A for class)
- Yellow dock Commission E: NOT listed (insufficient evidence for approval)
- Traditional Eclectic botanical medicine: yellow dock for chronic constipation with "liver congestion" — consistent traditional use across North American botanical medicine systems

### Skin conditions — Level D

Historical "blood purifier" / "alterative" use for eczema, psoriasis, acne: no modern RCT evidence; possible anti-inflammatory mechanism (emodin + quercetin) provides pharmacological plausibility but unproven clinically.

### Nutritional food use — Level A (vitamin C, folate — class evidence)

Common sorrel as a spring green is a legitimate vitamin C source (50–120 mg/100g — comparable to orange juice). Historical scurvy prevention with spring greens including sorrel is established.

## 7. Phenomenological Record

### Schav — Ashkenazi Spring Soup
Cold sorrel soup (schav) is one of the most culturally distinctive spring foods in Ashkenazi Jewish cuisine — a bright green, sour cold soup made from sorrel, egg, sour cream, and broth, served cold. Its spring seasonality (sorrel first available in March/April) directly corresponded to post-winter vitamin C repletion in historical Eastern European Jewish communities. The tradition migrated to America with Eastern European Jewish immigration (1880s–1920s); schav was sold in glass bottles in early 20th century New York delis. The sour taste that renders it culturally distinctive is pharmacologically the oxalic acid providing the vitamin C vehicle.

### Rumex and the Dock Leaf Remedy
The folk remedy of applying fresh dock leaf (*R. obtusifolius*) to nettle stings is one of Britain's most widespread folk medicine practices. The mechanistic rationale for efficacy is unclear: dock leaf does NOT contain anti-histaminic compounds; the cooling moisture from the leaf and the rubbing motion may provide more relief than any specific chemical constituent. However, a 2009 study found no evidence of efficacy beyond rubbing water on the sting — yet the cultural practice persists because dock (Rumex) and nettle (Urtica dioica) grow in adjacent habitats (both prefer disturbed, nitrogen-rich soil), making dock immediately available when nettle stings occur. This is potentially the most widespread placebo-driven folk medicine practice in Britain.

### Oseille in French Haute Cuisine
Sorrel (oseille) holds a unique position in French cuisine: it is the primary sour flavoring agent before citrus became universally available. Classic French preparations include:
- *Sauce à l'oseille*: cream + butter + sorrel (reduced)
- *Soupe à l'oseille*: pureed sorrel soup
- *Truite à l'oseille*: trout with sorrel sauce (a Troisgros brothers signature dish of *nouvelle cuisine*)
The sorrel sauce for trout by Jean and Pierre Troisgros (1971) — featuring sorrel's acidity as a counterpoint to fatty fish — became one of the most emulated dishes of French nouvelle cuisine. The culinary-medicinal duality of sorrel (acid flavoring + digestive stimulant + vitamin C source) exemplifies the Codex's consistent theme of food and medicine as a continuous spectrum.

## 8. Practical Application

### Culinary Use (Common Sorrel)

**Optimal preparation:**
- Fresh young leaves: highest vitamin C, most tender
- Cooking: sorrel turns olive-brown on cooking (pH change + chlorophyll degradation); flavor becomes milder
- Blanching 30 seconds: reduces oxalic acid ~30%; retains vitamin C
- Avoid cooking in iron or aluminum pots: oxalic acid reacts with metal

**Contraindications for culinary use:**
- **Kidney stones (calcium oxalate type):** sorrel significantly increases urinary oxalate → AVOID or minimize
- **Inflammatory kidney disease:** AVOID high sorrel consumption
- **Gout:** purine content minimal; oxalic acid load is the concern

**Safe culinary amount:** 40–100g fresh leaf per serving; reasonable weekly quantity for most adults

### Yellow Dock Root (Medicinal Use)

**For mild/chronic constipation:**
- 4:1 standardized extract: 500–1000 mg per dose (adult)
- Dried root: 2–4 g TID
- Tincture (1:5): 2–4 mL TID

**For skin conditions (traditional, Level D):**
- Decoction for internal use; poultice topically; no proven clinical benefit beyond traditional

**Contraindications (yellow dock root):**
- Intestinal obstruction: ABSOLUTE
- Pregnancy: AVOID (anthraquinones — emmenagogue)
- Renal insufficiency: CAUTION — oxalate load
- Concurrent digoxin: monitor electrolytes (potassium depletion risk)

### Energetic Classification
**Primary:** ◉ Qi (functional) — digestive, blood-moving, cooling; the classical TCM description of sourness entering the Liver and cooling heat aligns with the mild hepatoprotective and anti-inflammatory properties  
**Secondary (yellow dock):** ◈ Jing (structural) — traditional "blood building" associations; hematopoietic nutritional support claims
