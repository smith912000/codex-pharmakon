# Lesser Calamint — Nepitella, Mountain Balm, Calamintha

## *Calamintha nepeta* (L.) Savi (syn. *Clinopodium nepeta* (L.) Kuntze) | Family: Lamiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Hot and dry in the second degree — an acrid, aromatic warming herb in the Galenic reckoning, opening obstruction and dispersing cold, moist humours.
**Training Phase:** Purification

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian (AT):** The aromatic mints of the Lamiaceae appear among the fragrant herbs of the Mesopotamian medical tablets, valued as carminative and digestive drugs and as fumigants — the cuneiform materia medica repeatedly reaches for pungent, mint-family aromatics to settle the belly and clear "wind." Lesser calamint belongs to this old Near-Eastern lineage of culinary-medicinal mints.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** Dioscorides describes *kalaminthe* in *De Materia Medica* as a hot, biting herb: taken in drink or applied, it was used to bring on sweat, expel worms, move the menses, and against bites and bruises. This is the source of the western reputation of calamint as a heating, opening, emmenagogue herb — and, importantly, the classical warning-sign that it was used to provoke menstruation.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM):** In the Greco-Arabic tradition calamint (*fudanj* / mountain mint group) is a hot, dry aromatic used as a stomachic and carminative, to cut phlegm, warm a cold stomach, and open obstructions — a diffusive warming digestive in the same family of uses Dioscorides recorded.

The very name preserves the reputation: *calamintha* derives from the Greek for "beautiful mint," and across the Mediterranean the plant kept a double life as kitchen herb and household remedy, the *nepitella* still married to mushrooms and artichokes in Tuscan cooking. That culinary familiarity is significant — it marks the dose range at which the herb has been safely eaten for millennia, and by contrast marks off the concentrated oil as a different and more hazardous thing.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Calamint is a *warming opener* — a small, intensely aromatic herb whose whole doctrinal identity is dispersal of cold, damp stagnation: settling the gut, cutting phlegm, provoking sweat and menses. The pharmacology matches the doctrine and also encodes its danger. The essential oil is dominated by the monoterpene ketone **pulegone** (and related menthone/piperitenone oxide), the same class of compound that makes pennyroyal both a traditional emmenagogue and a recognised hepatotoxin. The tradition's use of calamint "to bring on the menses" and its chemical kinship to pennyroyal are two faces of one fact: the property that was prized and the property that kills sit on the same molecule.

### Active Components

- **Pulegone** — the major essential-oil constituent (reported roughly 13–32% of the oil, season-dependent); an oxygenated monoterpene ketone and the key toxicological concern.
- **Menthone / isomenthone**, **piperitenone oxide**, **piperitone epoxide** — further monoterpenoids of the oil.
- **D-limonene**, **germacrene D** and other terpenes.
- **Rosmarinic acid and flavonoids** in the polar fraction, contributing antioxidant activity.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Lesser calamint sits in the **Purification** phase as a *warming aromatic carminative*. It is indicated for a cold, damp, sluggish digestive picture — bloating, trapped wind, poor appetite, phlegmy stagnation — where a diffusive, warming herb restores movement. Culinary doses (the herb as *nepitella* in Italian cooking) are the safe and traditional register. In Kenetica the herb is used as a food-grade aromatic and mild infusion, never as concentrated essential oil, precisely because the active principle carries the pennyroyal-type hazard (see Contraindications).

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed: Božović & Ragno (2017), *Molecules* — a review of *Calamintha nepeta* and its principal constituent pulegone documents antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-ulcer and insecticidal activities, consistent with the herb's traditional digestive and antiseptic uses. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules22020290). Vlachou et al. (2023), *Plants* — GC-MS analysis confirms pulegone as a dominant oil constituent (13.0–32.0%, varying with harvest season and cultivation), alongside piperitenone oxide, isomenthone and D-limonene. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants12244098).

The toxicological basis for caution is direct: Khojasteh-Bakht et al. (1999), *Drug Metabolism and Disposition* — human liver cytochrome P-450s (CYP2E1, CYP1A2, CYP2C19) oxidise pulegone to menthofuran and onward to a reactive furan epoxide, the pathway underlying pulegone's known hepatotoxicity; the paper notes pulegone's folkloric use as an abortifacient "despite its potential lethal effects." [PMID: 10220485](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10220485/) (no DOI registered in PubMed).

**Evidence grade:** Bioactivity is preclinical (in vitro/animal) with strong traditional culinary-medicinal support; the pulegone hepatotoxicity/metabolism pathway is well established mechanistically in human hepatic enzymes.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** hot and dry (≈2nd degree); acrid, diffusive, deobstruent, carminative, emmenagogue, diaphoretic.
- **Dioscoridean:** heating and biting — sudorific, anthelmintic, emmenagogue.
- **Ayurvedic correlate (by analogy):** pungent (*katu*) *rasa*, heating (*ushna*) *virya*, pungent *vipaka*; kapha- and vata-reducing, pitta-aggravating in excess.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** The Mesopotamian medical tablets reach repeatedly for pungent Lamiaceae aromatics as carminatives, digestives and fumigants, and lesser calamint belongs to that broad Near-Eastern lineage of culinary-medicinal mints. But the cuneiform corpus classifies illness by causal agency — the hand of a god, a ghost, a broken oath — so that a drug is placed partly by what agency it is set against rather than by any status it holds in a person's vital constitution; and the standing blocking warning is in force for every Assyrian substance identification, the more so here where the tablets name a class of fragrant mints rather than *Calamintha nepeta* specifically. No cultivation status can honestly be assigned. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *Kalaminthe* is given a plain therapeutic account in *De Materia Medica*: a hot, biting herb taken in drink or applied to raise sweat, expel worms, move the menses and treat bites and bruises. Dioscorides carries no subtle-body doctrine into which such a herb could be ranked, and manufacturing one for him would be a fabrication the tradition does not support. What he actually offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention — the recognition of the true heating, biting *kalaminthe*, the judgement of its acrid quality on the tongue, the season of its gathering, the guard against a milder look-alike. That is a real practice of provenance and sensory assay, not a vital-body cultivation status. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** As *fudanj* (the mountain-mint group), calamint is a hot, dry aromatic worked as a stomachic and carminative — to cut phlegm, warm a cold stomach, provoke sweat and open obstruction. The strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus is the conservation of the innate heat and radical moisture, the finite vital endowment whose slow depletion *is* ageing; but that is a longevity frame of the whole regimen, not a status the tradition fixes to a single heating deobstruent, and no *mufarrih* or cardiac-*ruh* role is claimed for calamint in the retrieved sources. Per the corpus discipline no *Canon* chapter is cited. Its standing here is therapeutic, a warming digestive, not cultivational. **(b) framed:** attested in the materia medica, no cultivation status sourced.

**Practitioner's note.** Calamint is a warming opener, not a substance to build or conserve. Across all three traditions it is worked for a defined, cold-damp, stagnant picture — trapped wind, phlegm, sluggish digestion — and its whole character is to disperse and provoke movement, including of the menses, which is exactly why it is withheld in pregnancy. The safe register is the kitchen: the leaf as *nepitella*, or a light infusion. Nothing in the sources makes the concentrated pulegone-rich oil a cultivation material; the property that was prized as an emmenagogue and the property that damages the liver sit on the same molecule. Use the food-grade herb for the defined complaint, and go no further.

### Contraindications

**Contraindicated in pregnancy.** Calamint's essential oil is rich in **pulegone**, the same hepatotoxic, abortifacient monoterpene that makes pennyroyal dangerous; it was historically used to provoke menstruation. Pulegone is metabolised by human liver CYPs to menthofuran and a reactive furan epoxide, the mechanism of its hepatotoxicity (Khojasteh-Bakht et al. 1999, [PMID: 10220485](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10220485/)). Avoid the concentrated essential oil internally at any time; avoid the herb medicinally in pregnancy, breastfeeding, in infants, and in anyone with liver disease. Culinary use of the leaf as a flavouring is the traditional safe exposure; do not escalate to oil or high-dose extracts.
