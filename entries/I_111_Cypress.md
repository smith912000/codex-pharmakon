### Botanical Identity
Evergreen conifer 20–30m (columnar form); dark green scale-like leaves; rounded woody cones (2–4cm); strongly aromatic bark and foliage; native to the Mediterranean and Middle East; one of the most recognisable trees in Mediterranean landscapes (the dark spires of Italian and Tuscan landscapes are *Cupressus sempervirens* var. *stricta*). Extremely long-lived (individual trees known to exceed 1,000 years). The cones, leaves, and bark are the drug materials.

**Funerary tree**: The cypress is universally associated with death and mourning across Mediterranean cultures (Greek, Roman, Arabic, Persian, Turkish) — planted in cemeteries from antiquity to the present. This is pharmacologically relevant: the cypress was specifically associated with death and preservation partly because its aromatic compounds (monoterpenes, diterpene acids) are antimicrobial/preservative, making it functionally appropriate for burial grounds (where antimicrobial air would be valued) and for embalming preparations.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Dioscorides / Greco-Roman (DMM)
Book I, Chapter 107: "Cypress cones — astringent; for haemorrhage (bleeding from the lungs, bowel, uterus); for diarrhoea; topical for infected wounds." Dioscorides emphasises the astringent haemostatic applications of the cones.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine / Ibn Sīnā (CM)
*Sarw* — cooling and drying. Applications: astringent (for diarrhoea, haemorrhage); for varicose veins and haemorrhoids (vascular tone); for sweating excess (antiperspirant); topical for skin conditions and hair loss.

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (EP)
Cypress appears in EP preparations for wound care and as an astringent ingredient.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)
*Šurmēnu* (cypress) appears in AT ritual purification fumigation and in medical preparations for wound treatment.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Venous Tonic / Astringent (Primary — Proanthocyanidins + Rutin)

**Condensed tannins (proanthocyanidins) + rutin**:
- Rutin: strengthens capillary walls (inhibits hyaluronidase → reduces capillary permeability; vitamin P activity)
- Proanthocyanidins: vasoconstriction of venous smooth muscle → reduces venous pooling → tones varicose veins
- Astringent tannin-protein interaction → shrinks tissues → haemostatic for surface bleeding (wounds, haemorrhoids, mucosal bleeding)

The haemostatic/astringent applications in DMM and CM and the venous tone application in CM are grounded in this mechanism. This is the same class of mechanism as horse chestnut (I_85) aescin, but through tannin rather than saponin chemistry.

### 5.2 Antimicrobial (α-Pinene Complex)

The essential oil from cypress provides broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity (α-pinene as primary compound), identical in mechanism to cedar (I_110) and juniper (I_108). The fumigation/wound antiseptic applications in all traditions share this mechanism.

### 5.3 Antiperspirant

**Tannins + antimicrobial compounds**: The traditional CM antiperspirant application is pharmacologically coherent:
- Tannin astringency → reduces sweat gland secretion (physical pore closure)
- Antimicrobial against body odour bacteria (*Corynebacterium*, *Staphylococcus*) → reduces malodour
- This mechanism prefigures the modern deodorant formula (astringent + antimicrobial)

### 5.4 Anti-Inflammatory (Amentoflavone)

**Amentoflavone**: A potent biflavonoid with multiple mechanisms:
- COX-1 and COX-2 inhibition
- 5-LOX inhibition → reduces leukotriene production
- PDE inhibition → bronchodilator component
- The anti-inflammatory potency of amentoflavone is comparable to aspirin in some assays; it also inhibits HIV protease and SARS-CoV-2 Mpro (antiviral activity in vitro)

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Venous insufficiency**: Cypress cone extracts are used in herbal phlebotonic preparations in France and Italy; small clinical studies show improvement in venous insufficiency symptoms (oedema, pain). No large RCTs.

**Haemorrhoid treatment**: Topical cypress preparations used in traditional European herbal medicine for haemorrhoid management; the tannin astringent mechanism is valid.

**Modern aromatherapy**: Cypress essential oil is widely used in aromatherapy for varicose veins, oedema, and respiratory support; the mechanisms are pharmacologically supported but RCT evidence is absent.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The tree of mourning and immortality**: The cypress is simultaneously the tree of death and the tree of eternal life across Mediterranean cultures. In Greek myth, the dying youth Cyparissus was transformed into the cypress — an immortal form. The tree is planted in cemeteries because it was associated with immortality and preservation. In Islamic tradition, the cypress garden is an image of paradise. In Zoroastrian tradition, the Sarv-e Abarkuh (the Cypress of Abarqu in Iran) is believed to be over 4,000 years old — a living relic of Zoroaster's time. The ancient Mediterranean understanding that something about the cypress resisted decay (its aromatic compounds preserve against putrefaction, as in cedar) gave it the association with immortality that spans every culture in the Codex's geographic range.

**Amentoflavone and the multi-target phenomenon**: The biflavonoid amentoflavone found in cypress (and in a number of conifer species) demonstrates one of the most pharmacologically interesting properties in the Codex: it simultaneously inhibits COX-1/2 (anti-inflammatory), 5-LOX (anti-inflammatory), PDE (bronchodilator), HIV protease (antiviral), SARS-CoV-2 Mpro (antiviral), and binds benzodiazepine sites on GABA-A receptors (anxiolytic). This is a single molecule with pharmacological activity across five distinct therapeutic areas. The ancient empirical observation that cypress was broadly useful for multiple different conditions — respiratory, haemostatic, antiseptic, wound healing — reflects the genuinely broad pharmacological profile of its constituent compounds.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

| Form | Dose | Application |
|------|------|-------------|
| Cone decoction (5–10g cones in 500ml water) | 100ml 2–3× daily | Astringent; venous tonic; anti-diarrhoeal |
| Essential oil (topical, 2–5% in carrier) | Apply to varicose veins, haemorrhoids, wounds | Venous tonic; astringent; antiseptic |
| Essential oil (inhalation) | 3–5 drops in diffuser | Respiratory; antimicrobial environment |
| Sitz bath (10–15g cones in 2L hot water) | 15–20 min | Haemorrhoid; perianal inflammation |
| Topical compress (cone decoction) | Apply to skin | Wound; varicose veins |

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM (cross-reference)**: Bitter, astringent; cool. Channels: Liver (肝), Bladder (膀胱). Astringes, cools, moves Blood in the Liver channel (varicose application).

**Ayurvedic (cross-reference)**: Astringent, bitter; cooling. Reduces Pitta ↓↓. *Raktasthāpana* (haemostatic), *Grāhī* (astringent for diarrhoea). Not classical Ayurvedic plant.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Pregnancy (internal high doses)**: Isocupressic acid has abortifacient activity in large animals (documented in cattle eating pine/cypress needles); avoid high-dose internal use in pregnancy; standard aromatherapy topical/inhalation considered low-risk
- **Cypress allergy**: Cross-reactivity with other Cupressaceae (cedar, juniper) possible in sensitised individuals
- **Hormone-sensitive conditions**: Some evidence of mild estrogenic activity from cone flavonoids — precautionary monitoring in ER+ breast cancer context
