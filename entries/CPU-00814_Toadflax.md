# Toadflax — Osuros (Dioscorides 4-143), glossed "Great Snapdragon, Dragon's Mouth, Dog's Mouth"; Antirrinon (4-133)

## *Linaria vulgaris* (spine botanical; the translator's gloss for Dioscorides 4-143 lists "Linaria vulgaris [in Sprague]" beside *Antirrhinum linaria* and *A. majus*, and for 4-133 "Linaria cymbalaria … Ivy-leaved Toadflax") | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity warning.** The English word "Toadflax" carries this row, and the Dioscorides identifications are weak. Chapter 4-143 *osuros* is credited to *Linaria vulgaris* only through Sprague in a list that also offers *Antirrhinum majus* and, "[other usage]", *Osyris alba* ("Poet's Cassia"). Chapter 4-133 *antirrinon* is glossed to other species, among them "Linaria cymbalaria, Cymbalaria muralis — Cymbalaria, Kenilworth Ivy, Ivy-leaved Toadflax", which is a different plant from *L. vulgaris*. The Avicenna tag has no locus. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Three chapters were read in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`.

*4-143 OSUROS* (text line 9288; gloss at lines 9289 and 9290: "Osyris, Linaria [Fuchs], Linaria vulgaris lutea flore majore [Bauhin], Antirrhinum linaria [Linnaeus], Linaria vulgaris [in Sprague], Antirrhinum majus, Orontium majus — Great Snapdragon, Dragon's Mouth, Dog's Mouth [other usage] Osyris alba — Poet's Cassia, Gardrobe"; the chapter carries the cross-reference "see 4-133"). The whole chapter: "Osyris is a little black shrub, with thin hard-to-break stems, and around each, three small leaves; there are also four or five or six of these, like hemp; they are black when they are beginning but later they become reddish. A decoction of this (taken as a drink) helps jaundice." This is the only chapter the translator links to *L. vulgaris*, and Dioscorides' description (a little black shrub with three to six hemp-like leaves) is not on its face a toadflax.

*4-133 ANTIRRINON (KUNOKEPHALON)* (line 9197; cross-reference "see 4-143"): "Antirrhinon is a herb similar to anagallis [2-209] in the leaves and stalk, but the flowers are purple, similar to leucoion [3-138] only smaller, so it was also called sylvestris lychnis. It bears a fruit like the nostrils of a calf, carnation-like in appearance. It is said that (used as a personal ornament) this opposes poisons, and that rubbed on with lily oil or cyprine [nutsedge], it makes one beautiful. It is also called anarrhinon, and some have called it lychnis agrestis." The flowers are purple, whereas *L. vulgaris* is generally known as a yellow-flowered plant, and the chapter is cited here only because the translator's list puts a toadflax beside it.

*4-40 ELATINE* (text line 8343): glossed "Antirrhinum elatine, Linaria elatine, Cymbalaria elatine — Elatine, Cancerwort, Pointed-leaved Toadflax". It is another species of toadflax (*L. elatine*), with leaves "applied with polenta" for "inflamed rheumatic eyes" and, boiled and sipped, to stop dysentery. It is not *L. vulgaris* and is not transferred to this entry. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The one use recorded for *osuros* is a decoction drunk for jaundice, and for *antirrinon* an amulet and a cosmetic rub, all as Dioscorides' own and none as a Codex protocol. Because the identification of both plants with *L. vulgaris* is weak, the Codex gives no protocol for this plant.

### Preparation & Dosing

Dioscorides gives, for *osuros*, "a decoction of this (taken as a drink)" (4-143, line 9288) and, for *antirrinon*, wearing it "as a personal ornament" and rubbing it on with lily oil or cyprine (4-133). No quantity is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent* as to cultivation status. Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system. What he offers a cultivator is attention: he records the colour change of the *osuros* leaves from black to reddish with age and the number of leaves at each node, and, for *antirrinon*, the flower colour and the calf-nostril fruit. The amuletic line in 4-133 ("used as a personal ornament this opposes poisons") is introduced by "It is said", which records a report and is not an endorsement.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent.* The tag rests on a MASTER_INDEX row, not on a list row or a text read. No cultivation framing, and no attestation of any kind, was found.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing to cultivate here in the Codex's sense. The Greek accounts are short and descriptive, they probably describe different plants from the spine's, and the Unani tag is unsupported.

### Contraindications

No safety data were read. Do not treat the jaundice use as advice, and do not use any unidentified wild plant as medicine on the strength of this entry. Seek ordinary medical care for jaundice, which is a sign of illness that needs examination.
