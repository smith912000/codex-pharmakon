# Rubia tinctorum — Eruthrodanon (Dioscorides 3-160); erythodanon, eruthrodanum, rubia passiva, teuthrion

## *Rubia tinctorum, the second Latin name on the first of the two groups of names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-160 ("Rubia sativa [Fuchs], Rubia tinctorum [Fuchs, Linnaeus], Rubia tinctorum sativa [Bauhin] — Dyer's Madder"); the line's second group gives "Rubia sylvestris [Fuchs], Rubia sylvestris laevis [Bauhin], Gallium molugo — Hedge Bedstraw [Mabberley]" and "[other usage] Rubia peregrina — Wild Madder"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (lines 7895 to 7896) is in two parts, which the chapter itself explains ("Some of it is wild, some sown"): the first part, with *Rubia tinctorum*, is the dyer's madder, and the second part, with *Rubia sylvestris* and *Gallium molugo*, is a hedge bedstraw and a wild madder. The row takes *Rubia tinctorum*. It was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-160. ERUTHRODANON*, heading at line 7894, SUGGESTED line at 7895 to 7896, text at line 7897. It stands in Book Three, in the part headed "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants", after *3-159. PHALARIS* and before *3-161. LONCHITIS* (heading at line 7898). The translators' index gives *Rubia tinctorum*, *Rubia sativa*, *Rubia sylvestris*, *Rubia sylvestris laevis* and *Rubia peregrina* at page 532, and *erythodanon* at page 532.

The whole chapter reads: "Some call eruthrodanum 'erythodanon'. The red root is suitable for dying. Some of it is wild, some sown — as in Thabana, Galilee and Ravenna, Italy and in Caria — sown among the olives, like in the fields. It is sown usefully because much gain is made of it. The stalks of it are four-cornered, long, rough, similar to those of aparine, but in every respect bigger and stronger, having the leaves at distances at every joint lying about like stars in a circle. The seed is round, at first green, afterwards red, after that it is ripe, black. The root is thin, long, red, and diuretic; as a result a decoction (taken as a drink with honey and water) helps jaundice, sciatica, and paralysis. It draws out quantities of thick urine, and sometimes also blood. Those who drink it must be washed every day and the difference of their voided excrement viewed. A decoction of the stalk with the leaves (taken as a drink) helps those bitten by venomous creatures, and a decoction (taken as a drink with vinegar and honey) reduces the spleen. The root (inserted as a pessary) is an abortifacient, and draws out the menstrual flow and afterbirth. Smeared on with vinegar it cures white vitiligines [form of leprosy]. The root some call dracons, some, cinnabar, the Romans, rubia passiva, the Etruscians, lappa minor, the Egyptians, sophobi, some ereuthodanum, and it is also called teuthrion."

The chapter is a dye plant first, a medicine second. The red root is "suitable for dying". It is wild or sown, and the chapter names places where it is sown (Thabana, Galilee, Ravenna, Italy and Caria), "among the olives, like in the fields", and says it is sown "because much gain is made of it". The description gives four-cornered, long, rough stalks "similar to those of aparine, but in every respect bigger and stronger", leaves "at distances at every joint lying about like stars in a circle", and a round seed that is green, then red, then, ripe, black. The root is "thin, long, red, and diuretic". The medicinal uses follow in this order: a root decoction with honey and water for jaundice, sciatica and paralysis; the root draws out "quantities of thick urine, and sometimes also blood"; a decoction of the stalk and leaves for those bitten by venomous creatures; a decoction with vinegar and honey that reduces the spleen; the root as a pessary, an abortifacient that draws out the menstrual flow and afterbirth; and the root smeared on with vinegar for white vitiligines (the translators gloss this as a form of leprosy). The chapter prints these further names: *dracons*, *cinnabar* ("some"), the Roman *rubia passiva*, the Etruscan *lappa minor*, the Egyptian *sophobi*, *ereuthodanum* and *teuthrion*.

**The discipline of watching.** The chapter carries one instruction to the user and not to the preparer: "Those who drink it must be washed every day and the difference of their voided excrement viewed." The text does not say what difference to look for. The sentence follows "It draws out quantities of thick urine, and sometimes also blood", which is where the chapter says blood may appear.

**Names and comparisons in other chapters.** The chapter compares its stalks to *aparine*, which is *3-104* (cleavers), the subject of entry I_906, whose own text compares the aparine leaves back to "rubia [dyer's madder]" (line 7477). *4-117. MUAGROS* (heading at line 9079) says the leaves of *myagros* are "pale ... similar [to] those of rubia [dyer's madder]". The name *cinnabar* is printed here only as a name that "some" give the root, and the Codex carries nothing from any other substance of that name. The two comparison chapters are separate plants.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter number, *Eruthrodanon*, *Rubia tinctorum* and *Dyer's Madder* found no existing entry on 3-160 itself. I_1158 (*Phalaris arundinacea*, 3-159) names 3-160 as the next chapter and gives its line as 7892; the heading is at line 7894. I_906 (Cleavers) and I_897 (Cow Wheat, 4-117) quote the comparison to *rubia* from the other side. I_254 (Indian Madder, *Rubia cordifolia*) names *Rubia tinctorum* in its Unani paragraph and its constituent notes, and I_505 (Alkanet) lists madder in a cross-reference; those are not an entry on the Dioscorides chapter, and the Codex does not merge them.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. It is a general, well-established fact that the root of dyer's madder (*Rubia tinctorum*) contains anthraquinones, notably alizarin and related red pigments, and that this is the basis of its use as a dye. The chapter mentions the dye but not the compounds, and the species is the translators' suggestion. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a dye root, a diuretic decoction for jaundice, sciatica and paralysis, a decoction for venomous bites and for the spleen, a pessary that is an abortifacient and draws out the menstrual flow and afterbirth, and a root smeared on with vinegar for vitiligo-like patches. These are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for any of them. The chapter's own warning that the drinker must watch the urine and stool, and its mention of blood, argue for caution, and a pessary to expel the afterbirth or end a pregnancy is not something the Codex describes or recommends. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations: a decoction of the root "taken as a drink with honey and water"; a decoction of the stalk with the leaves "taken as a drink"; a decoction "taken as a drink with vinegar and honey"; the root "inserted as a pessary"; and the root "smeared on with vinegar". No quantity, frequency or duration is given, apart from the instruction that those who drink it "must be washed every day". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The one well-established general fact is the one stated under Active Components. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one action quality: the root is "diuretic". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. It prints the root as "thin, long, red" and the seed as green, then red, then black when ripe.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use. What it offers is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: provenance (the places where it is sown, wild against sown), a plant told by its four-cornered rough stalks and leaves in circles like stars, the colour sequence of the seed, and, most distinctly, an instruction to watch the effect on the person: the drinker is to be washed daily and the difference of the excrement viewed. The plant is given no status beyond a dye and a list of remedies. The identification is the translators'.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It offers a dye, a diuretic, a pessary and a skin remedy, so it is history and not a practice, and the one piece of method that is portable, watching the body's output while taking a strong drug, is not a reason to take it. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning beyond its instruction to watch the urine and stool, and it says the root can draw out blood and acts as an abortifacient. It is a general, well-established fact that pregnancy must not be risked with such a preparation, that blood in the urine needs medical assessment, and that jaundice, sciatica, paralysis, venomous bites and skin patches all need medical care rather than a root decoction. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion with a wild and a sown form, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
