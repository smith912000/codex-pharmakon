# Hingu — Hiṅgu / Rāmaṭha / Sahasravedhī / Jatuka (Sanskrit); Hing (Hindi); Asafoetida, Devil's Dung, Food of the Gods (English); Anjudan (Persian)
## *Ferula asafoetida* L. | Family: Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)
**Tradition Tags:** SS (Suśruta Saṃhitā — great carminative / antispasmodic gum-resin), CS (Caraka — dīpanīya, vāta-anulomana), AH; also prominent in Unani (Ḥiltīt)
**Energetic Classification (Ayurvedic):** Kapha–Vāta hara, Pitta-vardhaka; heating (uṣṇa vīrya), sharp and penetrating (tīkṣṇa)
**Training Phase:** Digestive-priming / Activation phase; Nervine-settling (antispasmodic) adjunct; pre- and post-meal support

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda (Suśruta, Caraka, Vāgbhaṭa):** Hiṅgu is the oleo-gum-resin exuded from the root and rhizome of *Ferula asafoetida*, and it is one of Ayurveda's premier **dīpana–pācana–vāta-anulomana** drugs — it kindles digestion, burns toxin, and above all *moves Vāta downward*, relieving colic, distension, flatulence and cramping. The classical texts prize it for **śūla** (spasmodic abdominal pain), **ānāha** (constipation/obstruction), **gulma** (abdominal masses/spasm), **krimi** (worms), and for cold, spasmodic, wind-driven disorders. Its intensely penetrating (tīkṣṇa, sūkṣma) nature lets it reach and unlock stuck channels. It is a keystone of the digestive compound **Hiṅgvaṣṭaka cūrṇa** ("eight-ingredient asafoetida powder"). Notably, raw hiṅgu is traditionally **fried in ghee** before use — a processing step (śodhana) that both tames its harshness and unlocks its carminative virtue.

**Unani:** As Ḥiltīt / Anjudan, asafoetida is held in "great esteem among indigenous medicines, particularly in the Unani system, from the earliest times," used as a digestive aid, antispasmodic, and nervine — a convergence with the Ayurvedic reading. According to PubMed, a review documents this Unani standing alongside its use in hysteria, nervous conditions, bronchitis, asthma and whooping cough (Mahendra & Bisht, 2012, *Pharmacogn Rev*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4103/0973-7847.99948)).

**Western herbalism & historic medicine:** In 18th–20th-century Western practice asafoetida was a recognised antispasmodic and expectorant, employed for "hysteria," nervous complaints, flatulent colic, bronchitis and asthma — the volatile oil being eliminated via the lungs, which underlies its expectorant reputation (Mahendra & Bisht, 2012, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4103/0973-7847.99948)).

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Unbinder:** Hiṅgu's doctrinal signature in Kenetica is *release of the bound*. Its very name in some traditions ("thousand-piercer," Sahasravedhī) declares its function: it penetrates and dissolves knots — the cramp, the colic, the spasm, the stuck breath, the seized nerve. Where Cavya ignites and Sarṣapa draws, Hiṅgu **unbinds**. Its notoriously foul raw smell that transforms, on frying in ghee, into a savoury depth is itself the doctrine: the pungent, off-putting, "devil's dung" material becomes "food of the gods" through correct processing — a symbol of the *śodhana* principle that the raw and violent, properly refined, becomes the profoundly nourishing. In the training vocabulary it is the antispasmodic exhale, the release of clenched tension, the settling of a wind-scattered (Vāta-vitiated) nervous system. It belongs to the moment of *letting go* of what is gripped.

### Active Components

The active material is the oleo-gum-resin, comprising a **volatile sulphur-rich essential oil**, gum, and resin. The oil's organosulphur compounds (disulphides such as the characteristic 1-(1-methylthiopropyl)-1-propenyl disulphide) give the pungent odour and much of the pharmacology; the resin fraction contains coumarins and **ferulic acid / sesquiterpene coumarins** (umbelliferone-type, ferulic esters).

- **Volatile organosulphur compounds** (disulphides) — odour, antimicrobial, antispasmodic
- **Sesquiterpene coumarins & ferulic acid derivatives** — antioxidant, resinous fraction
- **Umbelliferone-type coumarins** — resin
- **Gum polysaccharides** — bulk/vehicle

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. **Antispasmodic / anti-colic** — the flagship use: relief of cramping, spasmodic abdominal pain, flatulent colic. Strongly supported both traditionally and mechanistically.
2. **Carminative digestive primer** — Hiṅgvaṣṭaka-style support before or with heavy meals to move Vāta and prevent distension. Digestive-priming phase.
3. **Nervine-settling adjunct** — the historic "antispasmodic for nervous/hysterical conditions" and its smooth-muscle-relaxant action position it as a settling agent for wind-scattered, cramped, tense states (traditional + preclinical mechanism).
4. **Expectorant** — for cold, spasmodic, productive respiratory complaints (traditional).

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, asafoetida has **documented preclinical antispasmodic and hypotensive activity that corroborates the traditional carminative reputation**. A controlled tissue-and-animal study showed the gum extract relaxed isolated guinea-pig ileum precontracted by acetylcholine, histamine and KCl in a concentration-dependent manner, and significantly lowered mean arterial blood pressure in anaesthetised rats — with the authors concluding the relaxant compounds interfere non-specifically with muscarinic, adrenergic and histaminic receptor activity or with calcium mobilisation in smooth muscle (Fatehi et al., 2004, *J Ethnopharmacol*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2004.01.002)). A review summarises the gum resin as antispasmodic, carminative, expectorant, laxative and sedative, with historic use in flatulent colic, asthma and nervous conditions (Mahendra & Bisht, 2012, *Pharmacogn Rev*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4103/0973-7847.99948)).

**Evidence grade:** The antispasmodic (smooth-muscle-relaxant) action has direct in-vitro/animal support and a plausible receptor mechanism — a good match to the flagship traditional use, though human clinical trials for the specific indications are limited. Sedative/nervine and expectorant claims rest on traditional and review-level evidence. The hypotensive effect is animal-level and also a caution (see below).

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Rasa (taste):** Kaṭu (pungent)
- **Guṇa (quality):** Tīkṣṇa (sharp/penetrating), Sūkṣma (subtle), Snigdha (unctuous, once ghee-fried), Laghu (light)
- **Vīrya (potency):** Uṣṇa (heating)
- **Vipāka (post-digestive):** Kaṭu (pungent)
- **Prabhāva (special effect):** Vāta-anulomana par excellence and antispasmodic "unbinding" — its penetrating action on gripped/knotted channels exceeds what its taste alone predicts
- **Doṣa action:** Kapha-Vāta hara (especially a premier Vāta-mover); Pitta-vardhaka
- **Karma (actions):** Dīpana (agni-kindling), Pācana (digests āma), Vāta-anulomana (relieves wind/downward-moves Vāta), Śūlaghna (antispasmodic/anticolic), Krimighna (anthelmintic), Kaphaghna/Śvāsahara (expectorant, anti-asthmatic), Hṛdya (heart-favourable in tradition), Medhya/nervine-settling (traditional)
- **Gaṇa membership (Suśruta/Caraka):** dīpanīya and vāta-anulomana groups; keystone of Hiṅgvaṣṭaka

### Contraindications

- **Pitta excess / hyperacidity:** heating pungent aggravates gastritis, hyperacidity, peptic ulcer and heat/bleeding states.
- **Hypotension / antihypertensive therapy:** animal data show a blood-pressure-lowering effect — use caution alongside antihypertensives and in already-low blood pressure `[USER: verify against clinical guidance]`.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** traditionally cautioned (emmenagogue reputation); avoid therapeutic doses without qualified supervision. Historic use in "infantile" colic does not license unsupervised infant dosing.
- **Bleeding / anticoagulation:** the review notes blood-thinning effect — caution around surgery and with anticoagulants.
- **Always process (ghee-fry) before internal use;** raw resin is harsh and can irritate.
