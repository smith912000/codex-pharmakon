# Sium angustifolium — Eleioselinon (Dioscorides 3-75)

## *Sium angustifolium [Linnaeus], "Water Parsnip", the last of the names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-75 ("Heleio selinon [Pliny], Apium palustre, Sii primum genus, Laver vulgo dicitur [Fuchs], Apium palustre, Sion, [Bauhin], Sium angustifolium [Linnaeus] — Water Parsnip")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (lines 7197 and 7198) prints several names with their namers, the last being Linnaeus, and the row takes the last. It was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-75. ELEIOSELINON*, heading at line 7196, SUGGESTED line at 7197 and 7198, text at line 7199. It stands in Book Three under the running head "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants", after *3-74. SELINON AGRION, SELINON KEPAION* (line 7180) and before *3-76. OREOSELINON* (line 7200), both different chapters. The Codex's own name extraction lists it (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 481); the translators' general index prints "Sium angustifolium 451" (line 17556).

The whole chapter reads: "Helioselinum grows in watery places. It is bigger than the cultivated and it has similar effects to the garden kind. Some call it campestre, others, water smallage, and the Romans, apium rusticum."

That is the whole of it: a place (watery places), a size (bigger than the cultivated plant), a use by reference ("similar effects to the garden kind") and three other names, *campestre*, "water smallage" and the Roman *apium rusticum*. The chapter gives no description of leaf, flower, seed or root, no quality, no preparation and no dose. The heading prints the Greek *eleioselinon* and the text spells it *helioselinum*; both are reproduced as printed.

**What "the garden kind" refers to.** The chapter does not give a chapter number. The chapter just before it, *3-74. SELINON AGRION, SELINON KEPAION* (line 7180), is the garden selinum, glossed by the translators as *Apium graveolens* (celery). Its text (line 7183) reads that it applied with bread or polenta "is good for the same things as coriander (as well as for inflammation of the eyes)", "soothes burning in the stomach", slacks "breasts swollen with clotted milk", "causes an urge to urinate" when eaten, and that a decoction "resists poisonous medicines [antidote] by causing vomiting" and "stops discharges of the bowels", the seed being "more urinary". That is a labelled cross-reference. It is the text of 3-74, not of 3-75. It is only an inference that "the garden kind" is that chapter, and the inference that the water plant has those uses is the reader's, since 3-75 itself says only "similar effects". The Codex does not credit those uses to this plant as attested. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A chapter-level search of the entries folder for 3-75 found only two files (I_857 and I_745), and the match in both is a page range inside a journal reference ("743-75"), not this chapter. No entry on 3-75 as its own plant was found. The parsley entry I_181 treats *Petroselinum*, a different plant (3-77), and was not read for this entry.

### Active Components

The chapter prints none. It is a general fact that *Sium* is a genus of the carrot family (Apiaceae) that grows in or near water, and that several water-side members of that family are poisonous. The composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing here says whether the plant of 3-75 is safe to eat. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter gives no use of its own; it says only that the plant "has similar effects to the garden kind". The Codex gives no protocol. Water-side plants of the carrot family include some of the most poisonous plants known, and the identity here is a translators' suggestion, so there is nothing in this entry to act on.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use. The part of the discipline of attention that is present is small: the plant is placed ("in watery places"), sized against its cultivated relative ("bigger than the cultivated"), and its other names are given, among them the Roman *apium rusticum*. Because the chapter says so little, the discipline it offers is chiefly that of comparison with a plant already known, and of keeping the wild and the garden kinds apart. The plant is given no status beyond that of a wild relative of the garden selinum. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It supports the observation that a wild water plant is not a garden plant, which is a reason for restraint. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter says nothing of harm. As a general matter, wild plants of the carrot family that grow in water include dangerous poisons, and several resemble edible ones; the identification here rests on a translators' suggestion, so no wild plant should be gathered, eaten or applied on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
