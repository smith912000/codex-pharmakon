# I-282: Apium graveolens (Celery / Smallage / Celeriac)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-282 | Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Apium graveolens* L.
**Family:** Apiaceae

**Varieties:**
- *A. graveolens* var. *dulce* — Garden celery (cultivated for stalk/petiole); lowest essential oil; primary culinary form
- *A. graveolens* var. *rapaceum* — Celeriac/Turnip-Rooted celery (cultivated for root); higher essential oil than stalk; intermediate medicinal
- *A. graveolens* var. *secalinum* — Cutting/Leaf celery (leaf celery); highest essential oil; most medicinal; closest to wild smallage

**Common Names:**
- English: Celery, Garden Celery, Smallage (wild form), Celeriac (root form)
- Arabic: *Karafs* (كرفس) — well documented in Islamic medicine
- Hindi: *Ajwain ka patta* / *Shalari*
- Sanskrit: *Ajamoda* (shared with parsley — creates ongoing confusion; both Apiaceae diuretic carminatives)
- Greek: *Selinon* (σέλινον) — ancient name; city of Selinunte in Sicily named for wild celery growing there; appears on Selinunte coins (~500 BCE)
- Mandarin: *Qíncài* (芹菜) — "celery vegetable"
- Japanese: *Serori* (セロリ) — Western celery; introduced

**Historical Etymology:**
The Greek *selinon* used by Homer (Iliad) and Theophrastus establishes celery's presence in ancient Greek culture. In the Iliad, Elysian Fields (paradise) were described as having meadows of *selinon*. Wreaths of wild celery (*selinon*) were given to winners of the Nemean Games (one of the four Panhellenic Games) — connecting celery to victory and divine favor.

**Morphology:**
Biennial; 30–100 cm cultivated (to 150 cm wild); pinnate compound leaves; white umbel flowers; small ribbed seeds (technically fruits); distinctive celery aroma from all parts (more intense in wild/leaf varieties).

**Parts Used:**
- **Stalks (dulce var.):** Primarily dietary; low essential oil
- **Seeds:** Highest essential oil and medicinal potency; diuretic; anti-inflammatory; antiarthritic
- **Leaves (secalinum var.):** Intermediate; culinary and medicinal
- **Root (rapaceum var.):** Diuretic; concentrated active compounds
- **Essential oil:** Concentrated pharmaceutical/supplement form

**Anti-Hypertensive Discovery — Chinese Celery:**
Chinese traditional medicine documented celery's blood pressure-lowering effects centuries before the pharmacological discovery of phthalides (celery's anti-hypertensive compounds). This anti-hypertensive indication represents a clinically validated traditional observation with specific modern mechanistic explanation (phthalide smooth muscle relaxation → vasodilation).

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domains:**
1. **Diuretic / renal / kidney stone** — DMM, CM, SS, EP, AT, FMT: primary and universal
2. **Anti-arthritic / anti-inflammatory** — CM, SS, AT, European folk: gout; joint inflammation; rheumatism; validated by phthalide + 3nB mechanisms
3. **Anti-hypertensive** — Chinese TCM, European folk: blood pressure; validated by phthalide
4. **Emmenagogue** — DMM, CM, European folk: promotes menstrual flow; less emphasized than in parsley
5. **Carminative / digestive** — All traditions: anti-flatulent; digestive stimulant

**Unique Pharmacological Compound — Phthalides:**
Celery contains phthalides (including 3-n-butylphthalide, 3nB) that are not found in other Apiaceae carminatives at significant concentration. These compounds are responsible for:
- Anti-hypertensive (calcium channel antagonist in vascular smooth muscle)
- Anti-platelet
- Anxiolytic / sedative (potentially)
- Neuroprotective

**Energetic Classification:**
- ◉ Qi (functional): Primary — diuretic; anti-hypertensive vascular Qi; carminative
- ◈ Jing (structural): Secondary — anti-arthritic (joint structure); kidney structural support

**Primary Tier: ◉ Qi** with meaningful ◈ Jing anti-arthritic component

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Phthalides (Primary Unique Active Class)

**1. 3-n-Butylphthalide (3nB) — 50–75% of celery seed oil**
- The most pharmacologically significant celery compound
- **Vascular smooth muscle Ca²⁺ channel antagonist:** relaxes vascular smooth muscle → vasodilation → blood pressure reduction
- **Anti-platelet:** TXA₂ inhibition; independent mechanism
- **Neuroprotective:** reduces oxidative stress in neurons; reduces β-amyloid formation in Alzheimer's models
- **Anxiolytic:** mild sedative via GABA modulation (proposed)

**2. Sedanolide** — phthalide; anti-inflammatory; analgesic
**3. Neocnidilide** — phthalide; vasodilatory
**4. Butylidene phthalide** — aromatic; anti-spasmodic

### Essential Oil (Terpenes)

**5. Limonene — 70–80% of non-phthalide seed oil fraction**
- Carminative; anti-GERD; antimicrobial (same as in dill, caraway)

**6. β-Selinene, α-Selinene** — sesquiterpenes; anti-inflammatory; the "celery" sesquiterpene note

**7. Phellandrene** — antimicrobial

### Flavonoids

**8. Apigenin** — GABA-A PAM; anxiolytic; anti-inflammatory; apigenin is the dominant celery flavonoid
**9. Luteolin** — anti-inflammatory; anti-proliferative

### Coumarins (CAUTION)

**10. Bergapten (5-methoxypsoralen)** — furocoumarin; significant phototoxic risk
**11. Xanthotoxin (8-methoxypsoralen)** — potent phototoxin; **celery is a major occupational phototoxicity plant**
**12. Psoralen** — furocoumarin

**Celery Furocoumarin CRITICAL NOTE:**
Celery (especially wild celery, diseased celery with Botrytis infection, and celery root) contains furanocoumarin concentrations high enough to cause significant phototoxic dermatitis:
- Celery processing workers (field harvest, food processing) are an occupational at-risk group for phytodermatitis
- Diseased celery has 10–100× higher furocoumarin (fungal infection stimulates plant production as defense)
- **Celery dermatitis (phytodermatitis):** Burns, blistering after celery contact + sun exposure

### Vitamins and Minerals (Leaf)

**13. Vitamin K₁ — 29.3 μg/100g** (lower than parsley but still significant)
**14. Vitamin C — 3.1 mg/100g** (low in commercial stalk celery)
**15. Potassium — 260 mg/100g** (relevant for diuretic K⁺ balance)

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 1. Anti-Hypertensive (3nB — Primary Unique Mechanism)
- **3nB Ca²⁺ channel blockade (L-type):** reduces Ca²⁺ influx into vascular smooth muscle cells → smooth muscle relaxation → arterial vasodilation → blood pressure reduction
- This is the same mechanism class as calcium channel blocker drugs (amlodipine, nifedipine) — a pharmaceutical drug class mechanism found naturally in celery
- **Dose-dependent:** 8 stalks/day (or seed extract) produces measurable BP reduction in clinical studies
- Traditional Chinese observation validated: eating celery regularly reduces blood pressure; now understood mechanistically

### 2. Diuretic (Phthalide + Apigenin + Flavonoid)
- Phthalides: increase glomerular filtration rate; natriuretic (Na⁺ excretion)
- Apigenin: mild aldosterone antagonism → K⁺-sparing diuresis (similar to dandelion)
- Combined: significant diuresis at seed extract doses

### 3. Anti-Arthritic / Anti-Gout (Multiple Mechanisms)
- **Uric acid reduction:** Celery seed extract inhibits xanthine oxidase (XO) → reduced uric acid production; same enzyme as allopurinol (pharmaceutical gout drug) though weaker per dose
- **Anti-inflammatory (3nB, sedanolide):** COX inhibition; 5-LOX inhibition → reduced prostaglandin and leukotriene production → reduced joint inflammation
- Validates traditional "anti-rheumatic" use in Islamic, European, and Indian traditions

### 4. Neuroprotective (3nB)
- Reduces β-amyloid aggregation in vitro
- Reduces oxidative stress in hippocampal neurons
- Anti-neuroinflammatory: NF-κB inhibition in microglial cells
- **Alzheimer's research interest:** 3nB pharmacology is being explored as a therapeutic target; early-stage; not yet clinically validated for Alzheimer's

### 5. Anxiolytic / Sedative (Apigenin + 3nB)
- Apigenin: GABA-A PAM (same as chamomile, parsley)
- 3nB: mild GABA modulation (proposed)
- Traditional "nerve tonic" application has pharmacological basis

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Anti-Hypertensive:**
- Human observational study: Chinese physicians (University of Chicago; case report series n=73) — 4 stalks celery/day for 1 week → mean 14.4 mmHg systolic reduction, 11.6 mmHg diastolic reduction
- **Mechanism studies:** Multiple animal model confirmations; 3nB mechanism in vivo well validated
- **Evidence Grade: Level C** (human observational + strong mechanistic)

**Anti-Arthritic / Gout:**
- Celery seed extract (150 mg standardized phthalide) in pilot study (n=70): significant reduction in serum uric acid + improved arthritis pain scores
- **Evidence Grade: Level C** (pilot human; XO inhibition mechanism validated)

**Diuretic:**
- Traditional and in vitro/animal data; no dedicated human RCT
- **Evidence Grade: Level C**

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Overlooked Medicinal Vegetable:**
Celery occupies an unusual cultural position in modern Western food culture: widely used, frequently praised for low calorie content, but rarely valued for pharmacological action. The pharmacological reality (calcium channel antagonist, xanthine oxidase inhibitor, diuretic, anxiolytic via apigenin) contrasts with the dietary framing of celery as "negative calorie food" or "diet food" — a mechanistically shallow framing of a pharmacologically complex plant.

**Taste Profile:**
- Commercial stalk (dulce var.): mild, slightly bitter-green-watery; cool; crisp; characteristic but not intense
- Leaf celery (secalinum): much more aromatic, bitter, intense; essential oil-rich; medicinal character
- Seeds: concentrated, intense celery character; slightly bitter-earthy; distinctly medicinal
- Celeriac (root): more concentrated flavor than stalk; earthy-celery; complex; traditional European medicinal root

**Blood Pressure Experience:**
Traditional Chinese patients and herbalists who use celery consistently for hypertension report: over 1–2 weeks of daily high celery intake, measurable reduction in blood pressure (confirmed by home monitoring in some modern case reports); reduced sensations of head fullness, tinnitus, and flushing characteristic of hypertension in TCM "Liver Yang rising" pattern; generally attributed to the "calming" (降 *jiàng* — lowering/descending) nature of celery in Chinese dietary medicine.

**Occupational Phytoderm Context:**
Agricultural workers and food processing workers who handle large volumes of celery (especially in high UV environments — harvesting on sunny days) report blistering burns on forearms, hands, and faces where celery juice contacted skin followed by sun exposure. This occupational phototoxicity is serious and underrecognized. The UV-activated furocoumarin (bergapten + xanthotoxin) creates psoralen photoadducts with DNA — the same mechanism used therapeutically in PUVA (Psoralen + UVA) treatment for psoriasis, but undesired when accidental.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Fresh stalk (dietary — anti-hypertensive):**
- 4–8 stalks/day: sufficient phthalide delivery for blood pressure support
- Combined with dietary change; not a replacement for pharmaceutical anti-hypertensives in severe cases
- Chinese dietary medicine: daily stalk celery or fresh celery juice (200 mL/day)

**Celery seed extract (supplement):**
- Standardized to 85% 3nB content: 150–300 mg/day
- Gout: 150 mg 3× daily for uric acid reduction; 4–8 week course
- Anti-hypertensive: 150 mg 2× daily
- Diuretic: 150 mg 2× daily

**Celery seed tea:**
- 1–2 teaspoons crushed seeds in 250 mL boiling water; steep covered 10 minutes; 2 cups/day
- Traditional preparation for kidney stones, gout, urinary complaints

**Celeriac (root) preparation:**
- Soup or decoction: stronger than stalk; traditional European diuretic and anti-arthritic
- Grated raw in salads: traditional French *céleri rémoulade*; dietary delivery

### Safety Profile

**Generally safe at dietary doses. Specific concerns with concentrated preparations and particular populations.**

**Drug Interactions:**
- **Anti-hypertensive medications:** Additive blood pressure-lowering (3nB Ca²⁺ channel antagonism + pharmaceutical anti-hypertensives); monitor BP; may reduce medication requirements over time — adjust with prescriber
- **Warfarin:** Vitamin K₁ (29 μg/100g) lower than parsley; minor; monitor with large dietary changes
- **Diuretic drugs:** Additive; electrolyte monitoring
- **Allopurinol:** Additive XO inhibition (celery seed + allopurinol); may enhance gout control; monitor uric acid

**CRITICAL — Furocoumarin Phototoxicity:**
- Contact with celery juice (especially leaf celery, diseased celery, or root) + UV exposure → phototoxic burns
- **Topical celery applications** (celery juice as skin tonic, etc.): **AVOID in sun-exposed areas without UV protection**
- Celery seed essential oil: high furocoumarin; avoid topical without dilution and UV avoidance

**Contraindications:**
- **Pregnancy:** Celery seed/root — emmenagogue; uterotonic; **AVOID seed and root medicinal preparations in pregnancy**; culinary stalk amounts safe
- **Kidney disease (acute):** Avoid seed extract; oxalate content; nephrotoxic potential of high-dose phthalides
- **Apiaceae allergy** (cross-reactive with carrot, parsley, fennel, coriander — celery is among the most common Apiaceae allergens; anaphylaxis possible)
