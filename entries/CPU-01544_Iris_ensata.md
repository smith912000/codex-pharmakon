# Iris ensata — dres.ma, Dremai, Dre dru (rGyud-bzhi); "Iris"

## *Iris ensata Thumb, the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for dres.ma (with Iris decora and Iris lactea Pall.), and the one the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints in its scientific-name column, glossed "Iris"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts in this row.** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and the row's binomial is the first of three on Clark's line. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1048).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads: "dres.ma: Iris ensata Thumb: Iris decora: Iris lactea Pall.: [is of three types; the male type has two names, the female has three, and the neuter type has ten. The male type has thick linear leaves and a reddish-brown flower. The wild type has smaller fruit. The female type has a blue flower, a flexible leaf and is used to make ropes. The neuter type has an unattractive grey fruit and a reddish-blue flower]. Its petals are anthelmintic, relieve cramps, [neutralize poisons and are a general purgative]." The square brackets are as printed.

Clark's preface (line 82) says "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". So the part of the entry outside the brackets, that its petals are anthelmintic and relieve cramps, is the root-text layer as Clark gives it. The three types and their descriptions, and the claims that the plant neutralises poisons and is a general purgative, are in the commentary layer. The drug is therefore of three kinds, male, female and neuter, with 2, 3 and 10 names. The female kind is "used to make ropes" in the description. The text prints no taste and no power.

A second Clark locus names the seed. In chapter 21, "The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines" (heading at line 1205), line 1221 reads: "The category of anthelmintics contains Musk, Asafoetida, Garlic, Bengal Kino tree, Datura, Henbane, Embelia, seed of Iris ensata, Snail shell, Artemesia(nestita) ash, Daphne tangutica (tibetanum), Arisaema, Zanthoxylum and Heracleum (candicans)." There the plant is named as *Iris ensata* in English, with the part the seed, and not by its Tibetan name. Clark's name index (line 1705) lists *dres.ma* with no further information.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (Explanatory Tantra, glossary).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate blocks, and the pairing of a Tibetan name with "Iris ensata" is not printed in one row. The scientific name "Iris ensata" is at line 5254, the English "Iris" at line 5256, and the neighbouring row "Iris nepalensis Thlaspi arvense L." at line 5253. In the phonetic column the names run "Dre dru" (line 5193), "Drega" (5194), "Dremai" (5195); in the Tibetan column "dres 'bru" (5211), "bre ga" (5212). The sequences do not settle which phonetic name belongs to which scientific name, because the columns hold different numbers of entries. The Codex therefore does not state that the glossary pairs *dres.ma* with *Iris ensata*. [USER: verify]

The Men-Tsee-Khang text uses the names in two places. At line 3438 (the materia medica summary of the Explanatory Tantra) it reads: "Dremai gesar destroys ein and relieves abdominal cramps." At line 3638 it names "Dre dru" in a list ending "are the group of medicines that cures cm disorders", where the same list names Thangtrom and Langthangtse (datura and henbane in Clark's entries). "Ein" is one of the technical terms the translation leaves untranslated (line 686), and "cm" is probably an OCR error for it, since the same two names are said at line 3438 to cure disorders caused by "ein". Neither term is resolved further here, and "gesar" and "dru" are not translated in the text read. The index (lines 7348 and 7349) gives "Dre dru 222", "Dremai 208, 305" and "Dremai gesar 208". That "Dremai" and "Dre dru" are forms of *dres.ma* is a reading from the sound of the names and from the agreement on cramps and anthelmintic use, and is not stated in the text. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *dres.ma*, *Iris ensata*, *Dremai* and *Dre dru* found no existing entry on this drug. The only hit is I_973 (*Zanthoxylum armatum*), which mentions the Tibetan *gyer.ma* and is not on this drug. Clark's *ma.nu.pa.tra* (line 957) and *dur.byid* (line 1133) also print other *Iris* species (*I. germanica*, *I. dichotoma*), under different Tibetan names. They are different drugs and are not read as this one.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that the genus *Iris* is large, that its rhizomes are the part used in many traditions, and that fresh iris rhizomes are generally known to be irritant. Which part and species the Tibetan drug is (petals, in Clark; seed, in the compounding chapter) is only as stated above. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the Tibetan drug. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a plant, in three kinds, whose petals are anthelmintic and relieve cramps (with a commentary claim of poison-neutralising and general purgative action), whose seed is in the anthelmintic class, and, in the Men-Tsee-Khang text, a *gesar* that destroys *ein* and relieves abdominal cramps. These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for worms, cramps or poisoning, and the botanical identity is uncertain.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation and no quantity for *dres.ma*. Clark (line 1048) gives the part used (the petals) and the uses, and line 1221 the seed as an anthelmintic. The Men-Tsee-Khang text (lines 3438 and 3638) gives only the effect and the group. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark line 1048 prints no taste and no power for *dres.ma*. The Men-Tsee-Khang text places *Dremai gesar* as destroying *ein* (line 3438) and *Dre dru* in a group of medicines for what is printed as "cm disorders" (line 3638). No other quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes the plant by what it does and by its three kinds: anthelmintic petals that relieve cramps, with a commentary claim of poison-neutralising and purgative action. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. Nothing read places *dres.ma* in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. In the lines read, the only mention of essence extraction in the neighbourhood of this entry is for other plants, and *dres.ma* is not named there. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is an anthelmintic and cramp-relieving drug in the Tibetan texts and nothing more can be said from them. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. A purgative and a poison-neutralising claim, in the commentary layer, are reason for caution. Fresh iris plants can irritate, and worms, cramps and poisoning need medical care. The botanical identity is uncertain (three species in Clark), so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
