# Dngul.zil — "Mineral Sulphate of Calcium: Carbonate of Lime" (Tibetan dngul.zil, "silver zil")
## *A greenish, needle-breaking stone softer than silver ore; Clark (1995) glosses it as a calcium sulphate and as a carbonate of lime, and the two glosses are different compounds* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan Tradition (FMT).** The locus is the Explanatory Tantra materia medica in Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 832; name-index row `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` line 13; list row `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 140, "Ngulzil, , mineral, ... id_uncertain yes"). The entry reads: "dngul.zil: Mineral Sulphate of Calcium: Carbonate of Lime: [varies in shape and is softer than silver ore. It has a greenish hue and breaks into needle-like pieces. All three types of zil] remove discoloration of the bone."

**Reading the entry.** (1) The action, "remove discoloration of the bone", is stated of "all three types of *zil*". The three are the entries just before it: *stang.zil* (line 830, "Massicotite ... a black stone which breaks into sparkling needlelike pieces"), *gser.zil* (line 831, "Vermiculite: Litharge: Mineral Oxide of Lead ... a soft stone which breaks into long, sparkling needle-like pieces") and *dngul.zil* itself (line 832). The action is therefore a group action of the three *zil* stones and is not specific to the *dngul.zil*. (2) The description of *dngul.zil* is "varies in shape", "softer than silver ore" (the *dngul.rdo* of line 829), "greenish hue", "breaks into needle-like pieces". (3) The name *dngul* is the Tibetan word that appears as silver in *dngul.rdo* ("Silver Ore", line 829) and in the names of the stone's neighbours; the gloss "silver zil" is the form of the name in the headword, and Clark does not translate it. No dose and no preparation are printed.

**Position in the text.** The entry stands in "(2) The Powers of Stone Medicines" (heading at line 816), between *gser.zil* and *gru.bzhi* ("square stone", a type of pyrite, line 833). The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text was searched for "dngul", "ngul", "gypsum" and "calcium sulph" and returned no entry for this drug. The index of Tibetan terms lists it (Clark line 1685, "dngul.rdo. dngul.zil."). [USER: verify]

**Identity.** The gloss carries two compounds: "Mineral Sulphate of Calcium" (gypsum or anhydrite) and "Carbonate of Lime" (calcite or limestone). They are not the same, the file does not choose between them, and the neighbouring *zil* are glossed as lead oxides (massicot, litharge). The Codex's name-index row classes the item "plant" although it sits among the stone medicines. Dngul.zil is treated here as a mineral of uncertain identity, not as a calcium sulphate. ### Active Components

Stated generally. If the stone were a calcium sulphate it would be gypsum (hydrated) or anhydrite; if a carbonate of lime it would be calcite or limestone (CaCO3). Gypsum is a soft mineral that can be scratched with a fingernail, and calcite is harder; Clark's "softer than silver ore" does not decide between them. Needle-like fracture is a feature of fibrous gypsum (satin spar) among minerals, among others. The text does not say which mineral is meant, and no composition is asserted. No figures were read in the sources retrieved.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The only use printed is the group action of the three *zil* stones on "discoloration of the bone". The identity is unsettled, the neighbouring *zil* are lead oxides, and no preparation or dose is given. The Codex proposes no use.

### Contraindications

- **Unidentified mineral:** an unidentified stone should not be ingested.
- **Neighbouring lead stones:** the *zil* group includes lead oxides (massicot, litharge; lines 830-831); if the group is prepared as one, lead poisoning is a hazard, and the identity of *dngul.zil* does not remove it.
- **Calcium compounds:** large oral doses of calcium salts can cause constipation and raise blood calcium, especially in kidney disease; this is a general fact about the compounds, not about *dngul.zil*.
- **Dust:** powdered gypsum, limestone and the other minerals are respiratory irritants.
