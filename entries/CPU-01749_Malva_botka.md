# Malva botka — Champa, lcam pa, and Nyiga, nyi dga' (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Malva botka, the binomial as the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints it, set by column position against Champa (English name "Blue mallow") and again against Nyiga (English name "Marsh mallow"); Clark 1995 prints Malva verticillata Linn, a type of Mallow, and Dalbergia lanceolaria for lcham.pa* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and on a binomial that only one of the two editions prints.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk for *botka* found nothing. The genus *Malva* occurs in those texts only in other species or in a translator's gloss (Dioscorides, for example, lines 5753 and 7919, naming *Malva hortensis*, *Malva sylvestris pumila* and *Malva alcea*; Yang's "Semen Abutilonis Seu Malvae" for *gu huo*, `Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`, line 3758). None is read as a mention of this drug. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain, and it cannot confirm "botka" as an epithet from the sources read: it is kept as printed. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 248) the key *Malva botka* is taken from the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line 5143 with the note "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". It is the only binomial the glossary prints at that place, so it is neither the first nor a later one of several. The glossary prints *Malva botka* **twice**: here against Champa (line 5143) and again in the "N" block against Nyiga (line 5564), described below; the row's locus names only the first. It is not printed by Clark, whose binomial for the same Tibetan drug, *Malva verticillata* Linn, is the first binomial of Clark's line and has its own row (names.csv line 182, Clark line 1092; the subject of I_1314). The pairing with a Tibetan name is the Codex's reading by position, set out below.

**Identity doubt.** The two editions give two different names for the drug: *Malva botka* (glossary) and *Malva verticillata* (Clark, with *Dalbergia lanceolaria* as Clark's third name, a genus of legume trees as the Codex understands it, not checked against a flora). Both editions say "mallow" in English ("Blue mallow" in the glossary; "a type of Mallow" in Clark), so the two agree in kind. The Codex cannot say whether the glossary's *Malva botka* is a misprint, a rare name or a separate species. The entry stands for the Tibetan drug. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "C" block, lines 5128 to 5160).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. Eight phonetic names are printed (lines 5130 to 5133): "Chaga", "Chaagtik", "Champa", "Chang tsher", "Chawa", "Che tsha", "Chhuma tsi", "Chhurugpa". Eight transliterations follow (lines 5135 to 5140): "bca'sga", "lcags tig", "learn pa" (line 5136, the OCR's reading), "spyang tsher", "lea ba", "Ice tsha", "chu ma rtsi", "chu rug pa". The scientific names (lines 5142 to 5146) are eight: "Zingiber officinalis", "Gentianopsus paludosa" (both on 5142), "Malva botka" (5143), "Cryptothladia polyphylla", "Angelica glauca", "Ranunculus tanguticus" (5144), "Rheum nobile" (5145) and "Helerpestes cymbblar" (5146). The English names (lines 5158 to 5160) include "Ginger Iron coloured gentian", "Blue mallow Thistel" and "Buttercup Himalayan rhubarb". Counted in order, "Champa" is the third phonetic name and the third transliteration, and *Malva botka* is the third scientific name, with "Blue mallow" the third English name. The count can be checked on neighbours: "Chaga" is first against *Zingiber officinalis* and "Ginger", "Chaagtik" second against *Gentianopsus paludosa* and "Iron coloured gentian", "Chawa" fifth against *Angelica glauca*, and "Chhuma tsi" seventh against *Rheum nobile* and "Himalayan rhubarb", as Clark's materia medica allows a check (Clark prints *chu.ma.rtsi* and *lcha.wa*, both within the same run of entries). The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary, the second place (the "N" block, lines 5539 to 5570).* The same binomial is printed again. Nine phonetic names are printed (lines 5539 to 5541): "Ngonbu", "Nheu", "Nimpa", "Nyalo", "Nyangtse trae", "Nye shing", "Nyiga", "Nying shhosha", "Nyiwa". The transliterations (lines 5543 to 5546) include "nyi dga'" (line 5546), which is Clark's *nyi.dgah*, the name Clark gives to the neuter type under *lcham.pa* (line 1092; Clark's name index, line 1696). The scientific names (lines 5548 to 5570) are nine: "Cyananthus lobatus", "Chenopodium album", "Azadirachta indica", "Aconogonom campanalatum", "Coptis teetoides", "Asparagus adscendens" (5563), "Malva botka" (5564), "Choerospondias axillaris" and "Codonopsis convolvulacea". Counted in order, "Nyiga" is the seventh phonetic name and *Malva botka* the seventh scientific name, with the English "Marsh mallow" (line 5566) set beside "Wild asparagus" for the sixth and seventh. The count can be checked on neighbours: "Nimpa" is third against *Azadirachta indica* and "Neem", and "Nyangtse trae" (Clark's *myang.rtsi.spras*, Gold Thread) fifth against *Coptis teetoides*, (Clark prints *myang.rtsi.spras* as Gold Thread: *Coptis teetoides* at line 1072, and the glossary's English name there is "Gold thread", line 5561); I_1236 and I_1306 count the same block. The pairing is by position, not printed in one row. [USER: verify] The Men-Tsee-Khang running text names "Nyiga" once more: "Gya tsha, Gyam tsha, Sergi Jema, Digsin, Sugmel and Nyiga are the group of medicines that are diuretics" (line 3652, page 222; the index lists "Nyiga 222", line 7454). So the glossary sets one binomial against two Tibetan names, Champa and Nyiga, and Clark's commentary calls *nyi.dgah* a type (the neuter) of the one drug *lcham.pa*; the Codex does not say whether the two glossary names are two types of one plant or two plants, and treats this entry as covering both places because the binomial is the same. *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text on "Champa" (line 3456).* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428). The sentence reads: "Champa clears obstruction of urine, relieves thirst and stops diarrhea." The index lists "Champa 170, 210" (line 7290). The neighbouring sentence before it (line 3456) names "Shhu khen, Tsoe and Tsod" and the one after it "Drak cham" and "Tamik". The text gives no Latin name, no taste and no part used.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, a second place (page 170, line 3046).* In the passage on diet and the cooking of leaf vegetables, the text reads: "Cooked Champa and Baekhur generate heat and stop diarrhea." The index points to page 170 for "Champa". The Codex does not know whether the cooked vegetable and the drug are the same plant; the name is the same. Clark's diet material has a similar sentence without Tibetan names, "Stew of mallow and Plantago depressa increases digestive heat and stops diarrhoea" (Clark line 698), which uses an English word for the plant; the Codex records the likeness of the wording and does not set the two names equal.

*Clark 1995.* Clark's line for *lcham.pa* (line 1092, in chapter 20) is the other Tibetan source for this drug and is quoted in full in I_1314. Its root-text layer gives "cures urinary retention, diarrhea" and "thirst", which agrees in kind with Men-Tsee-Khang's "clears obstruction of urine, relieves thirst and stops diarrhea". The Codex reads Champa and *lcham.pa* as the same drug from the spelling, the three agreeing actions and the glossary's English name. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Malva botka*, "Champa" and "lcham.pa" found no entry on this drug under this binomial. I_1314 (*Malva verticillata*, tag FMT) treats the same Tibetan drug and the same Men-Tsee-Khang sentence under Clark's first binomial; the two entries are one drug under two binomials, the glossary's and Clark's, and they are not merged. I_1231 (*Angelica glauca*) and I_1294 (*Gentianopsis paludosa*) quote the same glossary block ("C") and print "Malva botka" as a neighbouring scientific name; I_1236 (*Asparagus adscendens*) and I_1306 (*Lactuca dissecta*) quote the "N" block and print it likewise. None gives the drug its own entry. I_460 (Common mallow, *Malva sylvestris*, tags EP, DMM, CM, TCM and SS) and I_703 (Dwarf mallow, *Malva neglecta*, tags EP, AT, DMM and CM) are *Malva* entries of other species from other traditions and do not cite the Tibetan texts. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Malva* is a genus of the mallow family (Malvaceae), whose leaves and flowers are characteristically mucilaginous. Which plant the Tibetan texts mean, which part is used, and whether the glossary's *Malva botka* is a mallow of that kind, are not settled by the lines read, so no statement about this drug's constituents is made. Clark's commentary (line 1092, quoted in I_1314) gives the tastes "sweet and astringent" for the drug.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "clears obstruction of urine, relieves thirst and stops diarrhea" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3456), and a name, Nyiga, listed in a group of diuretics (line 3652) and, as a cooked vegetable, "generates heat and stops diarrhea" (line 3046). These are the texts' claims in their own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. Urinary obstruction and diarrhea can be serious and need medical assessment.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give one preparation for the name: it is cooked ("Cooked Champa and Baekhur", Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3046), with no quantity, frequency or duration. The part used, in the drug sentence (line 3456), is not stated. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Men-Tsee-Khang prints no taste or power for "Champa" in the lines read. The one quality printed is that the cooked vegetable is said to "generate heat" (line 3046). Clark's commentary for *lcham.pa* (line 1092) prints "sweet and astringent tastes" and no power. The actions printed are clearing obstruction of urine, relieving thirst and stopping diarrhea (Men-Tsee-Khang), and, for the cooked vegetable, generating heat and stopping diarrhea.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Tibetan texts give this drug by what it does: it clears urinary obstruction, relieves thirst and stops diarrhea, and it appears once as a cooked leaf in the diet material. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes, and not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23 in both editions); no line read places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic and dietary only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name, not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a urine, thirst and diarrhea drug, and once a cooked vegetable, in the Tibetan texts, with no dose, and the binomial behind the Tibetan name is uncertain. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Urinary obstruction and diarrhea need medical assessment; no dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the identity is uncertain, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
