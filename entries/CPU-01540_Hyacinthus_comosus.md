# Hyacinthus comosus — Uakinthos (Dioscorides 4-63, "Hyacinthus"); "Tassel Hyacinth"

## *Hyacinthus comosus [Linnaeus] (now written Muscari comosum), the first of three identifications on the translators' SUGGESTED line for chapter 4-63 (with Hyacinthus botyroides, "Grape Hyacinth", and, under ALSO, Scilla bifolia, "Squill"), glossed "Tassel Hyacinth"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant under this name. The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The SUGGESTED line is the least settled of this batch: it lists the tassel hyacinth, the grape hyacinth (a different species, with its own Renaissance labels from Fuchs and Bauhin) and, after "ALSO", the squill *Scilla bifolia*. The Codex does not state the identity as certain, and the entry takes the first Linnaean binomial printed. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-63. UAKINTHOS* at line 8551, SUGGESTED lines at 8553 to 8557, text at line 8558. The chapter stands in Book Four, "Other Herbs & Roots", after 4-62 *ASTRAGALOS* and before 4-64 *MEKON ROIAS*, both different chapters. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 643.

The whole chapter reads: "Hyacinthus has leaves similar to a bulbus [2-200]; a smooth green stalk twenty centimetres long, thinner than a little finger; a curled calyx lying on it full of flowers of a purple colour, and the root similar to bulbus. Smeared with white wine on boys this is thought to keep them hairless; it also is therapeutic for the bowels. A decoction (taken as a drink) induces urine, and helps those bitten by harvest spiders. The seed is more astringent and is put in treacles. A decoction (taken as a drink with wine) cleanses jaundice. It is also called helonias, or porphyranthes; the Romans call it vaccinium, and some, ulcinum." The square brackets are the translators'.

So the chapter gives a description for recognition (leaves and root like *bulbus*, the bulb of chapter 2-200 *BOLBOS EDODIMOS*; a smooth green stalk twenty centimetres long and thinner than a little finger; a curled calyx full of purple flowers), five uses and three other names. The uses are: the root smeared on with white wine, "thought to keep" boys hairless; "therapeutic for the bowels"; a decoction as a drink to induce urine and to help those bitten by harvest spiders; the seed, "more astringent", put in treacles; and a decoction in wine that "cleanses jaundice". The hairless claim is introduced by "is thought" and is reported and not asserted. The other names are *helonias*, *porphyranthes*, and the Roman *vaccinium* and *ulcinum*. The chapter says nothing of the Hyacinthus of the Greek myth, and nothing is added from it here.

**Overlap.** A chapter-level search of the entries folder for 4-63 and for *Hyacinthus*, *Muscari* and *Uakinthos* found no existing entry on this chapter. The hits for 4-63 in the entries folder (I_689, I_772) are not on this chapter. The chapter's SUGGESTED line names *Scilla bifolia*, a squill, under ALSO. That name is a translators' alternative for this Greek chapter and does not make this entry a squill entry. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is a general fact that *Muscari* (the grape hyacinths) are bulbous plants of the asparagus family (Asparagaceae, in older systems Liliaceae or Hyacinthaceae), and that *Scilla* is a different genus of bulbs. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the plant of this chapter, which could be any of the translators' three. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a wine drink for jaundice, a decoction as a diuretic and for spider bites, a bowel remedy, an astringent seed in treacles, and a topical belief about hairlessness in boys. These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for jaundice or spider bite, and the identity is a suggestion among three plants that are not the same.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations and no quantities: the root "smeared with white wine"; "a decoction (taken as a drink)"; "a decoction (taken as a drink with wine)"; the seed "put in treacles". It prints no dose, frequency or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identity is too uncertain for a modern study of one bulb to bear on the Dioscorides drug. It is a general, well-established fact that jaundice has many causes and needs medical assessment.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 4-63 prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality. The one quality printed is that the seed is "more astringent" than the rest of the plant. The text prints no taste.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a description for recognition (the leaves and root against a bulb the reader is expected to know from chapter 2-200, a measured stalk, a curled calyx of purple flowers) and a note that different parts differ in quality (the seed is the more astringent). The hair claim is recorded with "is thought". The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is that the seed and the root are judged separately. The text offers nothing more, and the abstentive answer is the right one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 4-63 prints no warning. The identification is only the translators' suggestion among three different plants, so no bulb should be gathered or eaten or used on the strength of this entry. Some bulbs of this kind are toxic. Jaundice and spider bites need medical care. The text gives no dose, and no safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
