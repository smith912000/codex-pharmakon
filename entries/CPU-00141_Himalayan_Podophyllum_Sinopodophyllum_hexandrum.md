### Origin & Tradition

*Sinopodophyllum hexandrum* — long known as *Podophyllum hexandrum* or *Podophyllum emodi*, the Himalayan or Indian mayapple — is a perennial herb of the Berberidaceae found in the alpine and sub-alpine forests and meadows of the Himalaya, from Afghanistan and northern India through Nepal and Bhutan into southwestern China. A single stem carries a pair of deeply lobed leaves and a solitary nodding pink-white flower, ripening to a fleshy red fruit; beneath the soil runs a knotted rhizome and root that is the source of the drug. In the Himalayan folk pharmacopoeia it is **bankakri** (and many local cognates), gathered for its powerful purgative and caustic root.

In the Ayurvedic and broader Himalayan-Tibetan traditions the rhizome was valued as a drastic purgative and as an agent against skin growths, warts and stubborn lesions, and in cleansing therapies — always with respect for its toxicity. It belongs to the class of strong, near-poisonous purging roots used sparingly. The related North-American species, *Podophyllum peltatum* (American mayapple), played the same role in Eclectic and folk Western medicine, where its dried resin, **podophyllin**, became a recognised caustic for venereal warts. The two podophyllums — Old World and New World — converge on the same active chemistry.

That chemistry is **podophyllotoxin**, an aryltetralin lignan that arrests cell division by binding tubulin and blocking microtubule assembly — an antimitotic "spindle poison" mechanism. The Himalayan species is notably rich in it, which is why *Sinopodophyllum hexandrum* became the principal commercial source of podophyllotoxin and drove the plant toward over-harvest and protected status. As a topical agent, podophyllin/podophyllotoxin remains in use against genital warts; ingested, the resin is a dangerous gastrointestinal and neurological poison.

The plant's greatest significance is as a chemical ancestor. Semi-synthetic modification of podophyllotoxin yielded the **topoisomerase-II-targeting anticancer drugs etoposide and teniposide**, which are widely used in modern chemotherapy. Thus a nodding alpine herb of the Himalaya, a drastic folk purgative, became the starting material for a major class of cancer medicines. In the Codex it stands as the paramount example of a toxic traditional purgative whose active lignan crossed from folk caustic to clinical antineoplastic — and as a conservation cautionary tale, the wild source depleted by the very value its chemistry created.

### Contraindications

- **Internal crude-drug use — absolute prohibition outside expert supervision.** A drastic purgative and systemic poison.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding — absolute.** Antimitotic; teratogenic risk; podophyllin is specifically avoided in pregnancy.
- **Broken or extensive skin (topical).** Avoid application to large areas, mucosa or broken skin — systemic absorption causes poisoning.
- **Children, the frail, the depleted — avoid.** A draining, toxic drug inappropriate for deficiency states.
- **Bone-marrow suppression / cytotoxic interactions.** Systemic exposure suppresses marrow; the antineoplastic derivatives carry the full cytotoxic-drug contraindication profile (oncology supervision only).
- **Neurological risk.** Systemic podophyllotoxin can cause peripheral neuropathy and central toxicity.
- **General:** suspected ingestion or topical overdose is a medical emergency.
