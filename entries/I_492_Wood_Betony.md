# I-492 — Wood Betony
## *Betonica officinalis* L. (= *Stachys betonica* Benth.)

**Family:** Lamiaceae | **Volume:** I — Plant-Based | **Entry:** I-492

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted Name:** *Betonica officinalis* L. (1753); also accepted as *Stachys betonica* Benth. and *Stachys officinalis* (L.) Trevis.
**Common Names:** Wood betony, bishop's wort, woundwort, betony, purple betony, bishopswort
**Synonyms:** *Stachys betonica* Benth. (1834); *Stachys officinalis* (L.) Trevisan (1842); *Betonica alopecuros* L.; multiple regional varieties

**Etymology:** *Betonica* (also written *Vettonica*) derives from the Vettones, an ancient Iberian Celtic people of the western Iberian Peninsula (modern Portugal/western Spain), through whose territory the plant was traded and from whom Roman physicians first learned of its medicinal properties; *officinalis* = of the apothecary, indicating official medicinal status; "wood" from its preferred habitat (woodland edges and clearings); "bishop's wort" reflects the plant's ecclesiastical significance in medieval monastery gardens where bishops maintained betony as a standard medicinal herb

**Botanical Description:** Erect perennial, 15–60 cm; stem square (Lamiaceae); leaves opposite, oblong to ovate, deeply crenate-serrate, rugose (strongly wrinkled/textured), densely hairy; basal leaves long-petiolate; stem leaves sessile; flowers bright reddish-purple (occasionally white) in a dense, interrupted terminal spike; calyx with 5 teeth; bloom June–August; rhizomatous, spreading slowly to form clumps; attractive ornamental with characteristic rugose leaves and vivid flower spikes

**Distribution:** Native to Europe (Britain south through Mediterranean, north to Scandinavia, east to European Russia); preferred habitats: woodland edges, clearings, dry grasslands, heathland, hedgerows; calcifugous (avoids chalk/limestone substrates); declining in Northern European localities due to grassland and woodland habitat loss

**Plant Part Used:** Aerial parts (leaves, flowering tops); leaves harvested before flowering for maximum alkaloid/phenolic content; whole herb at flowering for Commission E-style preparations

**Adulterants/Substitutions:** *Stachys sylvatica* (hedge woundwort; similar appearance, different chemistry — lacks betonicine alkaloid profile); *Stachys palustris* (marsh woundwort); visual distinction from betony by absence of betony's long-petiolate rugose basal leaves in related *Stachys* species

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

### The Greatest Reputation in European Herbalism

Wood betony holds a unique position in the history of European medicine: it was, by contemporary consensus of ancient and medieval physicians, the most highly regarded medicinal plant in the Roman and Anglo-Saxon pharmacopoeia. This reputation did not arise from a single dramatic application — as pennyroyal's reputation for emmenagogue activity (I-489) or hyssop's for the biblical purification ritual (I-487) — but from a perceived breadth of application that made it the universal corrective of its era.

Antonius Musa, physician to the Emperor Augustus (63 BCE – 14 CE), wrote a treatise specifically devoted to betony (*De Herba Vettonica*) listing 47 separate conditions for which betony was indicated — from headache, liver disease, and nervous complaints to snakebite protection and memory improvement. This treatise was cited throughout the medieval European materia medica and reached its popular crystallization in the proverb: *"Vende pallium tuum et eme betonicam"* — "Sell your coat and buy betony." The proverb (surviving in multiple European languages: Spanish *"Vende tu manto y compra betónica"*; Italian *"Vendi il mantello e compra betonica"*) encapsulates the idea that betony's medicinal value exceeded even the need for warm clothing.

Dioscorides describes *kestron/bettonikē* in *De Materia Medica* III.12, noting applications for headache, liver conditions, digestive complaints, and as a remedy for injuries from venomous animals. His account is more measured than Musa's but confirms the plant's prominence in first-century CE Mediterranean medicine.

### Reading Musa's 47 Conditions Through Pharmacological Mechanism

The list of 47 conditions attributed to betony by Antonius Musa represents not medical fantasy but the classical approach to therapeutic classification — each "condition" is a symptom description rather than a modern disease category. When read through a pharmacological lens, the majority cluster around three mechanism groups: (1) nervous system complaints (headache, melancholy, trembling, disturbed sleep — GABA-modulatory flavones + rosmarinic acid), (2) digestive complaints (stomach pain, poor digestion, liver congestion, nausea — bitter tonic caffeic acids + tannins + astringent action), and (3) inflammatory conditions (wounds, swellings, joint pain — verbascoside + rosmarinic acid anti-inflammatory). Musa's 47 conditions, when aggregated by underlying mechanism, collapse to approximately 3 pharmacological categories — matching the plant's actual activity profile precisely.

### The Decline and Partial Rehabilitation

By the 18th and 19th centuries, betony had largely dropped from the European pharmacopoeia. Enlightenment rational empiricism, which required reproducible therapeutic effects beyond humoral theory's explanatory framework, found betony's universal reputation difficult to validate systematically. Contemporary phytochemistry has partially rehabilitated it: the alkaloid, phenylethanoid glycoside, and caffeic acid derivative profile confers genuine anti-inflammatory, mild astringent, and nervine activity — not the 47-condition polypharmacy claimed by Musa, but pharmacologically coherent activity for nervous headache, digestive toning, and mild anxiety.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

### Alkaloids (Distinctive Betony Fraction)

**Betonicine** (betaine of L-stachydrine): 0.3–1.1% dry weight; betaine alkaloid; hypotensive in animal models; mild smooth muscle activity; the same stachydrine documented for cardiac effects in motherwort (*Leonurus cardiaca* I-477) — in betony, present at lower concentrations with correspondingly milder cardiovascular action; betaine alkaloids constitute one of the key pharmacological differentiators of betony within Lamiaceae

**Stachydrine** (L-proline betaine): 0.1–0.5% dry weight; related betaine; mild cardiovascular and mild central nervous system activity in animal models; also found in citrus fruit juice (lemon, orange) — dietary exposure context

**Trigonelline:** betaine alkaloid; found in fenugreek and coffee; mild cholinergic modulation in animal models

### Phenylethanoid Glycosides
**Verbascoside (Acteoside):** 1–3% dry weight; the same compound documented in mullein (*V. thapsus* I-473) and several other entries; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; antiviral; mild antihypertensive in animal models; among the most widely distributed phenylethanoid glycosides in medicinal plants

**Forsythoside B:** phenylethanoid glycoside; anti-inflammatory

**Echinacoside:** minor phenylethanoid glycoside; immunomodulatory; shared with *Echinacea* spp.

### Phenolic Acids
**Rosmarinic acid:** standard Lamiaceae constituent; anti-inflammatory; GABA-T inhibitor; in betony, concentration is moderate — not at the exceptional 6% dry weight of self-heal (I-491) or high concentrations of lemon balm (I-478), but pharmacologically meaningful

**Caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid:** hydroxycinnamic acid antioxidants; bitter tonic activity (vagal stimulation of gastric secretion)

**Lithospermic acid:** caffeic acid dimer; anti-inflammatory; minor antiandrogenic activity

### Flavonoids
**Apigenin:** flavone; GABA-A positive allosteric modulation at benzodiazepine binding site (same mechanism as chamomile); contributes to mild sedative/nervine effect

**Luteolin:** flavone; NF-κB inhibition; NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition; anti-inflammatory

**Scutellarein (6-hydroxyapigenin):** flavone; same structural class as baicalein in skullcap (I-476) but distinct compound; mild GABA-A modulatory activity; 6-hydroxyl group increases potency vs apigenin

**Quercetin, kaempferol glycosides:** standard flavonol fraction; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; capillary-protective

### Iridoids
**Harpagide, 8-acetylharpagide:** iridoid glycosides; mild anti-inflammatory; also found in devil's claw (*Harpagophytum procumbens*) — the genus name commemorates the compound's first structural characterization in that plant, though harpagide occurs broadly across Lamiaceae

### Tannins
**Condensed tannins (procyanidins):** 2–5% dry weight; astringent; anti-inflammatory; wound-hemostatic; relevant to throat, wound, and digestive astringent applications

### Essential Oil (Minimal)
Betony has minimal EO content (<0.1%) — a critical pharmacological distinction from most pharmacologically active Lamiaceae in preceding entries (thyme, oregano, lavender, peppermint all 0.5–4% EO). Betony's activity is primarily alkaloid- and phenolic-based, not EO-based. This explains both its mild aroma and the failure to find the same dramatic antibacterial and antifungal activity characteristic of high-EO Lamiaceae.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Nervine / Mild Sedative Mechanism

**Rosmarinic acid → GABA-T inhibition:**
Standard Lamiaceae nervine mechanism; elevates synaptic GABA through inhibition of GABA transaminase; mild anxiolytic effect; present in betony as part of its phenolic fraction; not at the concentrations of lemon balm (I-478) or skullcap (I-476), contributing to betony's characterization as a gentle "nervine tonic" rather than a primary anxiolytic

**Apigenin + scutellarein → GABA-A positive allosteric modulation:**
Flavone-mediated PAM at the benzodiazepine binding site of GABA-A receptor; apigenin the primary driver (well-documented); scutellarein (6-hydroxyapigenin) provides additional betony-specific modulation; combined flavone content produces mild sedative effect — appropriate for tension headache and nervous exhaustion; insufficient for acute anxiety management

**Combined nervine mechanism:** Betony achieves mild but genuine nervine activity through two complementary GABA-enhancing pathways (GABA-T inhibition + GABA-A PAM), the same dual mechanism that makes lemon balm and skullcap more potent — in betony, at lower constituent concentrations

### 5.2 Headache Mechanism

**Rosmarinic acid → anti-inflammatory + antispasmodic:**
COX/LOX inhibition relevant to tension headache's neurogenic inflammatory component; mild smooth muscle relaxation relevant to vascular tension headache's vasospasm component

**Betonicine → mild hypotensive + vasodilatory:**
Betaine alkaloids reduce peripheral vascular resistance at higher doses; potential relevance to vascular and tension headache components; mechanism similar to (but weaker than) stachydrine's documented cardiovascular effects in *Leonurus cardiaca* (I-477)

**Flavone GABA-modulation → muscle relaxation:**
Cervical muscle tension contributes significantly to tension-type headache; GABA-mediated muscle relaxation (central mechanism) may reduce this contributor

**Combined headache mechanism:** Anti-inflammatory (rosmarinic acid) + mild vasodilation (betonicine) + anxiolytic/muscle-relaxant (apigenin/scutellarein) = polypharmacological profile for tension-type headache that is mechanistically coherent, supporting the most prominent traditional indication even without RCT validation

### 5.3 Digestive/Tonic Mechanism

**Caffeic acid + chlorogenic acid → bitter tonic:**
Mild digestive bitter activity via vagal stimulation of gastric acid and enzyme secretion; less pronounced than true bitter herbs (gentian, wormwood I-467) but sufficient for mild digestive atony and hypochlorhydria

**Tannin + rosmarinic acid → astringent/anti-inflammatory (GI mucosa):**
Procyanidins coat and tone GI mucosa; anti-inflammatory activity relevant to mild gastritis or irritable bowel presentations; traditional use for "stomach weakness" reflects this toning/normalizing action on mucosa

### 5.4 Wound Healing (Topical)

**Verbascoside + rosmarinic acid → fibroblast stimulation + anti-inflammatory:**
Same wound-healing mechanism as mullein (I-473) verbascoside and self-heal (I-491) rosmarinic acid; caffeic acid derivatives promote collagen synthesis; relevant to traditional poultice application

**Tannins → astringent hemostasis:**
Procyanidin tannin content (2–5%) provides meaningful astringent activity at wound margins and mucosal surfaces

### 5.5 Mild Antihypertensive Mechanism

**Verbascoside → ACE-inhibitory-like + calcium channel modulation:**
Verbascoside demonstrates mild ACE-inhibitory activity in vitro and calcium channel modulation at smooth muscle; mild antihypertensive effect in animal studies; clinical significance in humans at herb-tea doses likely modest but consistent with the traditional "calming" effects of betony on the cardiovascular system in nervous states

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 Headache / Nervine

**Evidence Level: C (traditional evidence) + D (pre-clinical mechanistic)**

- No RCTs for any betony indication
- Weiss RF 1988 (*Herbal Medicine*, English translation from *Lehrbuch der Phytotherapie*): endorses betony as "the most widely prescribed European nervine" for tension headache, mild anxiety, and nervous exhaustion; experience-based clinical recommendation from extensive practitioner record
- Sticher O & Lahloub MF 1982 (*Planta Medica* 45(3):159-163): phytochemical characterization; establishes the alkaloid and flavone profile supporting the nervine mechanism
- Mills S & Bone K 2000 (*Principles and Practice of Phytotherapy*): Level C evidence for tension headache; nervine tonic; digestive support

### 6.2 Digestive

**Evidence Level: C (traditional evidence)**

- Commission E: NOT monographed
- No RCTs; traditional use well-documented across multiple European herbal traditions for stomach weakness, digestive atony, mild gastritis, nervous dyspepsia
- British Herbal Pharmacopoeia 1983: includes betony as official herbal preparation; indications include anxiety-related digestive complaints

### 6.3 Antihypertensive

**Evidence Level: D (pre-clinical animal data only)**

- Stachydrine/betonicine: animal studies demonstrate mild hypotensive effect via reduced peripheral vascular resistance
- No clinical trials for blood pressure; not recommended as primary antihypertensive; may contribute to betony's nervine-cardiovascular combined calming effect

### 6.4 Regulatory Status

- **Commission E:** NOT monographed
- **ESCOP:** NOT monographed
- **EMA:** No herbal monograph
- **British Herbal Pharmacopoeia (BHP) 1983:** Includes betony as official herbal preparation; indications: anxiety, headaches, nervous debility — the only major pharmacopoeia to include betony in the 20th century
- **British Herbal Compendium:** Level C evidence for nervine tonic applications

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

### 7.1 "Sell Your Coat and Buy Betony"

The proverb *"Vende pallium tuum et eme betonicam"* encapsulates the peak of betony's cultural authority. The hyperbole is deliberate: stating that betony is worth more than adequate clothing reflects not irrational superstition but the seriousness with which medical emergencies were treated in pre-modern medicine, when effective pharmacological intervention was rare and precious. Betony, in this context, represented one of the most reliable broad-spectrum therapeutic agents available — not because it cured 47 specific diseases but because its anti-inflammatory, nervine, and digestive-tonic properties ameliorated symptoms across an enormous range of presentations that converged on the same underlying mechanisms.

The proverb's persistence across multiple European languages for over a millennium itself documents genuine therapeutic utility: folk proverbs encoding specific therapeutic endorsements rarely survive this long without some experiential basis that validates them in successive generations of users.

### 7.2 The Monastery as Pharmacological Research Institution

Wood betony was a standard constituent of European monastery herb gardens from at least the 9th century through the 17th century. The monastic herb garden system — a network of standardized pharmacological collections maintained across hundreds of sites over eight centuries — represents one of history's longest continuous phytomedicinal research programs. Plants preserved in these gardens were not selected arbitrarily; they were maintained because they produced reproducible results under the clinical conditions that monasteries experienced (continuous care of the sick, injured, elderly, and dying). Betony's persistence in this system across 800 years of continuous monastic medicine constitutes a form of longitudinal evidence that cannot be dismissed — though it cannot substitute for controlled trials.

### 7.3 The Anglo-Saxon Supernatural Framework for a Neurological Herb

In Anglo-Saxon England, betony was among the most important herbs in the monastic medical tradition. The *Lacnunga* (~10th century) and *Bald's Leechbook* (~9th century) reference betony for protection against supernatural evil, nighttime terrors, and disturbed dreaming. In a pre-scientific framework, these applications mapped precisely to the same symptom clusters that betony's mild nervine activity actually addresses: anxiety-induced sleep disturbance, nervous exhaustion, and the somatic manifestations of psychological distress. The supernatural interpretive framework provided a cultural container for a genuinely beneficial herb. What Anglo-Saxon physicians called "protection against nighttime demons" and what modern phytotherapy calls "nervine support for anxiety-related sleep disturbance" are, at the pharmacological level, the same therapeutic application.

### 7.4 The Vettones and the Etymology of Medical Authority

The naming of betony after the Vettones — an ancient Iberian Celtic people — encodes a geopolitical and cultural history of pharmacological knowledge transmission. Roman physicians encountering the Iberian Peninsula during conquest learned local materia medica from indigenous populations, of which betony was evidently the most prominent therapeutic herb. Antonius Musa's decision to name his monograph for the people from whom Romans had learned of the plant — the *Vettones* → *Vettonica* → *Betonica* etymology — is an unusual acknowledgment of indigenous pharmaceutical knowledge in a classical Latin medical text. The survival of this attribution for 2,000 years in the plant's Linnaean binomial constitutes the longest-surviving pharmacological attribution to a pre-Roman European people.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Indications (Evidence-Based, Within Safety Parameters)

**Tension Headache (Nervine Support):**
Herb tea; traditional use; Level C + mechanistic support (anti-inflammatory, mild anxiolytic, mild vasodilatory); appropriate as supportive measure for mild to moderate tension headache; not a primary therapy for severe, frequent, or atypical headache patterns requiring medical evaluation

**Mild Anxiety / Nervous Exhaustion:**
Herb tea; mild GABA-modulatory flavone content + rosmarinic acid GABA-T inhibition; Level C evidence; appropriate for mild nervous tension and long-term nervine tonic use; not a primary anxiolytic — *Melissa officinalis* (I-478) and *Scutellaria lateriflora* (I-476) have superior clinical evidence for anxiety; betony for tonic/trophorestorative use rather than acute anxiolysis

**Digestive Tonic:**
Herb tea before or after meals; bitter tonic + astringent action; Level C evidence; appropriate for dyspepsia, digestive atony, mild irritable bowel, nervous dyspepsia

**Topical Wound Healing / Oral Care:**
Fresh herb poultice or strong tea compress; traditional use; Level B mechanistic support (verbascoside + tannin + rosmarinic acid); appropriate for minor cuts, bruises, oral ulcers (gargle), pharyngitis (gargle)

### 8.2 Preparations and Dosing

**Herb Tea (Standard):**
- 2–4 g dried aerial parts in 250 mL boiling water; steep 10–15 minutes; cover during steeping
- 3 cups daily; up to 4–6 weeks; suitable for long-term use as nervine tonic; excellent safety profile supports extended use

**Tea for Headache:**
- 4–6 g dried herb (stronger preparation); drink warm at onset or early prodrome of tension headache; may combine with *Lavandula angustifolia* (I-483) 2 g or *Valeriana officinalis* 2 g for enhanced nervine effect

**Tincture:**
- 1:5 in 45% ethanol; 2–4 mL three times daily; equivalent to 2–3 cups of tea per day in phenolic content

**Topical:**
- Fresh herb poultice: crush leaves/stems, apply to wound or bruise; secure with clean cloth; change every 4–6 hours
- Strong tea compress: 6–8 g herb in 200 mL water; steep 20 minutes; apply when cooled to body temperature; hold 15 minutes; repeat as needed
- Oral gargle: strong tea cooled; gargle 30–60 seconds; do not swallow large quantities

**Culinary:**
- Young leaves (before flowering): edible as salad addition or tisane; mild astringent flavor; no safety concern at food quantities; traditional salad herb in parts of Southern Europe

### 8.3 Contraindications and Safety

**CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- **Pregnancy:** Historical emmenagogue reputation in some sources; insufficient safety data; avoid during pregnancy as precautionary measure
- **Hypotension:** Betonicine's mild hypotensive effect; avoid in pre-existing hypotension or concurrent antihypertensive medication use until interaction data available

**SAFETY PROFILE:** Excellent; no documented serious adverse effects in historical or contemporary records; centuries of continuous use across European medical traditions without serious safety signals; one of the safest medicinal herbs in the Codex; the 2,000-year use record with no toxicity documentation is itself meaningful pharmacovigilance data

**DRUG INTERACTIONS:**
- **Antihypertensives:** Theoretical mild additive hypotensive effect from betonicine; clinical significance unclear; monitor if used regularly alongside antihypertensive medications
- **CNS depressants:** Mild additive effect from GABA-modulatory flavones; clinical significance low at typical herb-tea doses; standard precaution with sedative medications

**NOTES ON LONG-TERM USE:** The remarkably clean safety record of betony over 2,000 years of use, combined with its genuinely mild-but-real pharmacological activity, makes it an archetype "nervine tonic" herb — appropriate for long-term daily use in stress management and headache prevention without the tolerance concerns of benzodiazepines or the gastric risk of NSAIDs. This safety + tolerability profile explains why it was the most widely used herb in both monastery gardens and domestic Anglo-Saxon medicine.

### 8.4 Energetics

**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi + ☿ Shen (mild)
**Thermal Quality:** Warm, mildly drying (Galenic: warm, dry — the classic nervine temperament)
**Directional Action:** Upward-resolving (headache — directing energy downward from the head per classical interpretation); resolving (digestive stagnation); astringent (wounds/mucosa)
**Classical Taste:** Bitter-astringent (tannins + alkaloids + caffeic acids)
**Organ Affinity (TCM-influenced):** Liver (headache, tension); stomach (digestive); nervous system (nervine)

### 8.5 Quality and Authentication

**Botanical Authentication:** Visual identification reliable for experienced botanist; the strongly rugose (deeply wrinkled) long-petiolate basal leaves + dense terminal spike of bright reddish-purple flowers in interrupted whorls is essentially characteristic; GC-MS not the primary authentication tool (minimal EO); HPLC preferred for verbascoside (>0.5% good quality), rosmarinic acid, stachydrine/betonicine alkaloid content; tannin content by gravimetric protein precipitation

**Harvest and Preparation:**
- Leaves: before flowering for maximum alkaloid and phenolic content
- Whole herb: at full flower (June–August) for maximum flavone content
- Drying: 35–40°C; avoid high-temperature drying that degrades alkaloids and volatile phenolics; store dried herb ≤12 months in sealed dark container; whole dried herb retains potency longer than powdered material

**Commercial Availability:**
Betony was historically widespread commercially but has declined in modern herb trade; may require sourcing from specialist botanical herbalists or UK/European suppliers; *Stachys betonica* and *Betonica officinalis* both acceptable; verify species on CoA — *Stachys sylvatica* (hedge woundwort) is a common botanical confusion that lacks betony's alkaloid profile
