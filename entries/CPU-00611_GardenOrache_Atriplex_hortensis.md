# Garden Orache — Mountain Spinach, Red Orach, French Spinach

## *Atriplex hortensis* L. | Family: Amaranthaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM
**Energetic Classification:** Cool and moist (first to second degree) — a Galenic cooling, emollient, softening pot-herb
**Training Phase:** Foundation

---

### Origin & Tradition

Garden orache is one of the oldest cultivated leaf vegetables of Europe and the Near East, grown as a pot-herb long before spinach displaced it. A tall, quick-growing annual of the goosefoot family (now placed in Amaranthaceae, formerly Chenopodiaceae), it comes in green and deep-red ("rubra") forms, the red owing its colour to betalain pigments rather than anthocyanins. Its culinary and medicinal roles overlap completely: it was eaten as a cooling, moistening green and used as a gentle domestic remedy for exactly the states a cooling green was thought to correct.

**Assyrian (AT):** The Mesopotamian medical and agricultural record includes cultivated leafy potherbs of the goosefoot type used as food and as soft, cooling poultice material. Orache-type greens belong to this class of mild, moistening vegetable-drugs applied to hot, dry, or inflamed conditions and eaten to soften the stool. As with most tablet plant-names, precise binomial identification is uncertain, so *Atriplex* is placed here as a member of the broad Assyrian potherb tradition rather than a securely named single entry.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** Dioscorides describes orache (*atraphaxis*) in *De Materia Medica* as an edible garden herb that is emollient and loosening to the belly — a cooling, moistening green taken to soften and move the bowel and applied externally to hot swellings. This is the founding Western statement of orache's gentle laxative-emollient character, carried forward through the medieval and Renaissance herbals.

**Avicenna (CM):** In Unani medicine following Avicenna's *Qanun*, orache (*qataf*) is classed as cold and moist, a *mulattif*/emollient and mild laxative food-drug. It was recommended to cool hot temperaments, moisten dryness, ease constipation, and, as a poultice, to soften indurations and calm hot inflammations. Its status as a food-medicine — nourishing and gently corrective rather than potent — is characteristic.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Orache's soft, water-laden, quick-growing leaf was read directly as its virtue: a plant that is itself cool, moist, and yielding was held to impart coolness, moisture, and yielding softness to a body that was hot, dry, and bound. The doctrine here tracks real physiology — a high-water, mucilage-and-fibre green genuinely softens stool and soothes irritated tissue. The red "rubra" form, rich in blood-coloured betalain pigments, drew additional symbolic association with the blood, and modern work confirms those very pigments carry the plant's antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity.

### Active Components

Garden orache combines the profile of a leafy vegetable with several distinctive bioactives. Leaf and seed contain **betalain pigments** — the red *Atriplex hortensis* var. rubra is a notable source of **amaranthin-type betacyanins** (amaranthin, celosianin) responsible for its colour and much of its antioxidant/anti-inflammatory action. The plant supplies **mucilage and dietary fibre** (the basis of its emollient, laxative use), vitamins and minerals typical of a green, and — importantly — **oxalates**, which it accumulates as a member of the goosefoot group. The seeds also contain **type-1 ribosome-inactivating proteins** ("hortensins"), plant defence proteins of pharmacological interest.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Orache belongs in the **Foundation** phase as a nourishing, cooling, moistening food-herb — a base-layer green rather than an active corrective. Its role is to build and soothe: to cool a hot, dry constitution, moisten and loosen a bound bowel, and supply antioxidant pigment and fibre as ordinary food. In practice this means orache is used at culinary scale, cooked like spinach, as part of the dietary bedrock of a training phase — its value is nutritional and gently emollient, not pharmacological intensity. The betalain content gives it a modest functional-food edge over a plain leaf green.

### Scientific Correlation

Modern evidence for *Atriplex hortensis* is preclinical but genuine, and it converges on the red-pigment fraction. According to PubMed, Kumorkiewicz-Jamro et al. (2024), *Food Chemistry* studied *A. hortensis* var. 'rubra' leaf and seed extracts and purified amaranthin-type pigments (amaranthin, celosianin) in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages: extracts and pigments inhibited prostaglandin E and nitric oxide production by up to ~90% and ~70% respectively, downregulated Il6, Il1b, Nos2 and Cox2, and showed potent dose-dependent radical-scavenging activity without cytotoxicity. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2024.140920). This substantiates the traditional "cooling, calms hot inflammation" reputation at the cellular level.

Separately, according to PubMed, Ragucci et al. (2025), *International Journal of Biological Macromolecules* characterised **hortensin 4**, the main type-1 ribosome-inactivating protein from *A. hortensis* seeds, reporting antifungal and antiviral (tobacco mosaic virus RNA N-glycosylase) activity and cytotoxicity toward glioblastoma cells enhanced by temozolomide. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2025.142085). This flags that the *seed* proteins are biologically potent — a reason to keep food use to the leaf.

**Evidence grade: Preclinical (in vitro / cell-model).** Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity is well demonstrated in cell models; there are no human clinical trials. Traditional emollient/laxative use is long-standing but not clinically quantified.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Cold and moist, first to second degree. Emollient (*mulattif*), mild laxative, cooling to hot temperaments; poultice for hot swellings.
- **TCM (by analogy):** Cool, sweet, moistening — a "clear heat / moisten dryness / lubricate the intestines" leaf-food in character, not a classical Chinese herb.
- **Ayurvedic (by analogy):** *Rasa* sweet (madhura) with astringent note; *virya* cooling (shita); *vipaka* sweet; pacifies Pitta, moistens Vata dryness, may increase Kapha and — via oxalate — is used cautiously.

### Contraindications

- **Oxalate content:** as a goosefoot-family green, orache accumulates oxalates. This is the primary caution — avoid large or raw quantities in anyone with a history of **calcium-oxalate kidney stones, hyperoxaluria, gout, or impaired renal function**. Cooking and discarding the water reduces oxalate load; keep raw intake modest.
- **Mineral binding:** high oxalate can reduce calcium/iron absorption if orache is a dietary staple.
- **Seed material:** the seeds contain ribosome-inactivating proteins (hortensins) — food use should be the leaf, not concentrated seed preparations.
- **Not a drug substitute:** evidence is preclinical; do not present as a treatment for any condition.
