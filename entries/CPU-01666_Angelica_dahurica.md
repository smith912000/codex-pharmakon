# Angelica dahurica — Bai Zhi 白芷 (root) (Shennong Bencao Jing)

## *Angelica dahurica, as the pharmaceutical Latin "Radix Angelicae Dahuricae" that Yang 1998 prints for the entry Bai Zhi; the same Romanised name Bai Zhi is also printed for two other entries in the same text* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Shennong text (SBJ).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The entry is in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`). The Latin name is the translator's pharmaceutical Latin and was matched to the binomial by stem in the extraction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, row 124). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Chinese characters 白芷 are those of the Codex's own list of Shennong names (`_sources/shennong.csv`, line 162, which prints the characters alone), not of Yang's English text. [USER: verify]

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *Bai Zhi, PDF page 76.* The page marker `[[p76]]` is at line 2833 and the entry is at line 2835. No class heading is printed on this page. The governing heading is "Herbs: Middle Class" at line 2801 on the page marked `[[p75]]` (line 2800), and the next heading, "Herbs: Middle Class" again, is at line 2873 on `[[p77]]` (line 2872). The entry therefore stands under the middle-class heading by position (thirty-four lines and one page break below it), not directly beneath it. The text reads (lines 2835 to 2840):

"Bai Zhi (Radix Angelicae Dahuricae)167 is acrid and warm. It mainly treats leaking, red and white [vaginal discharge], blood block, and swollen genitals in females, and cold and heat, head wind, and [wind] invading the eyes causing tearing. It promotes the growth of the muscles and skin and moistens and makes [the skin] shiny. It can be used to make a face cream. Its other name is Fang Kiang (Fragrance). It grows in rivers and valleys."

"Fang Kiang" is printed so in the text file; the Codex does not correct it. The entry prints no toxicity statement.

Yang's note 167 (lines 2853 to 2862) is the translator's modern commentary and not part of the classic: "Angelica Dahurica dissipates wind, dispels dampness, frees the orifices, and promotes perspiration. It is often used as an usher to the hand and foot yang ming and jue yin and hand tai yin. The yang ming travels the face and head. When there is headache and blurred vision, one may prescribe Angelica Dahurica with Ligusticum Wallichium. ..." The note goes on to toothache, nasal congestion, itching of the eyes, tearing, wind stroke, leaking, blood block and genital swelling. It puts these in the present tense, and it does not say they are the classic's.

*Two other entries share the name.* The Romanised name "Bai Zhi" is printed for a different entry earlier in the same text: "Bai Zhi (Ganoderma Alba)" at line 1424, in the Superior Class (PDF page 37), treating cough and counterflow qi ascent. That is a fungus entry, and it is the subject of I_110 (*Ganoderma lucidum*). It is not this plant. A third use is as an other name: the entry for Gou Ji (Rhizoma Cibotii Barometsis, line 2777 onward) reads "Its other name is Bai Zhi (Hundred Branches)" at line 2780. The three are different entries, with different Latin names and different glosses, and the Codex keeps them apart.

*A translator's cross-mention.* In the note on another entry (note 145, on Mi Wu, lines 2405 to 2418), Yang writes that a medicinal "should be combined with Bai Zhi (Radix Angelicae Dahuricae)" for yang ming headache (line 2415). It is the translator's note to a different entry, and is given here only as a cross-reference. It is not a statement of the classic.

*The class headings.* Yang's table of contents prints "Herbs: Middle Class" (line 716). The preface (lines 385 to 390) says of the middle class: "They mainly nurture personality and correspond to humanity. They may or may not be toxic, and [therefore,] one should weigh and ponder before putting them to their appropriate use. If one intends to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion, one should base [one's efforts] on the middle class." Yang's commentary (lines 358 to 362) says that middle-class medicinals "are able to cultivate personality or modify temperament" and that "Since this class of medicinals may be toxic, their prescription requires care."

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Angelica dahurica* and *Bai Zhi* found no entry on this plant. I_067 (*Ligusticum chuanxiong*) names *Angelica dahurica* (as Bái Zhǐ, 6 g) in a modern formula in its prose (line 70). I_011 and I_010 are on *Angelica sinensis*, and I_313 on *Notopterygium incisum*; they are different plants. I_110 (*Ganoderma lucidum*) contains the other Shennong "Bai Zhi" (the fungus, line 1424), as stated above. None is an entry on this plant.

### Active Components

General composition, stated without numbers: *Angelica dahurica* is a plant of the carrot family (Apiaceae), and the roots of plants of this family are generally known to contain coumarins, including furanocoumarins. This is a general statement about the family. The composition of the root as the Shennong text meant it was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Shennong text lists the entry's actions in its own terms (leaking, red and white discharge, blood block, swollen genitals in females, cold and heat, head wind, tearing, promoting the growth of the muscles and skin, moistening and making the skin shiny, and use in a face cream). These are not modern diagnoses, and they are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: the middle class (中品), by position.* The class is assigned by the printed heading "Herbs: Middle Class" (line 2801, `[[p75]]`), repeated at the top of the next page (line 2873, `[[p77]]`). The entry on `[[p76]]` has no heading directly above it, so the grade rests on its position between the two. This is a printed class, and not a promise of cultivation use. In the preface (lines 385 to 390) the middle class mainly "nurture[s] personality", may or may not be toxic, and is the class on which one bases the effort "to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion". It is not the class the preface ties to making the body light and prolonging life (lines 352 to 355, 383 to 384), which is the superior class.

The entry's own words fit that frame and go no further: it names women's complaints, head and eye complaints, and the skin ("promotes the growth of the muscles and skin and moistens and makes [the skin] shiny"). It says nothing of protracted taking, of making the body light or of prolonging life. (The next entry, Du Ruo, does say "Protracted taking may boost the essence, brighten the eyes, and make the body light" at lines 2844 to 2845; that sentence belongs to Du Ruo and not to Bai Zhi.) The entry asserts no *jing*, *shen* or *neidan* action, and none is added here.

**Practitioner's note.** A middle-class entry in the Chinese scheme: for complaints of women, the head, the eyes and the skin, weighed before use, and not the class of long-term life-nourishing drugs. The entry promises no cultivation outcome, and it shares its name with two other entries, so nobody should take the name alone as the plant. Nothing here supports taking this root as a cultivation practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The Shennong entry prints no warning. The preface says of the middle class generally that it "may or may not be toxic" and calls for weighing before use. The identity rests on a translator's Latin name, and the name Bai Zhi is shared with two other entries, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Vaginal discharge, genital swelling and eye complaints need medical care. Plants of the carrot family that contain furanocoumarins can sensitise skin to sunlight, a general fact about the family and not a finding about this plant. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
