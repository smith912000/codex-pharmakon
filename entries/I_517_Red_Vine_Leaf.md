# I-517 | Red Vine Leaf (*Vitis vinifera* L. — leaf)

**Volume I — Plant-Based Substances**
**Family:** Vitaceae
**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (venous toning, anti-edema) primary

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary binomial:** *Vitis vinifera* L. — same species as grape seed (I-516); leaf preparation = distinct pharmaceutical product with separate EMA monograph and clinical evidence base

**This entry covers:** *Vitis vinifera* leaf, specifically **red-leafed autumn grapevine leaves** (AS 195 extract = Antistax; the specific extract used in all major clinical trials)

**Common name:** Red vine leaf, red grape leaf (*Vitis vinifera* folium rubrum)

**Botanical differentiation from I-516:**
- Same species (*V. vinifera*), different plant part (leaf vs seed), different harvest timing (autumn vs year-round), different chemical profile (flavonoid + OPC + anthocyanin vs OPC-dominant seed), different pharmacological indication (venous insufficiency via venous wall toning vs combined vascular), separate EMA monograph
- **Red leaves specifically:** autumn red-pigmented leaves accumulate higher anthocyanin content; the AS 195 extract specifies autumn red grape leaves as raw material; leaf color indicates anthocyanin profile similar to fruit skin (cyanidin glycosides)

**Harvest specification:**
Red grapevine leaves in autumn (September–October) at leaf senescence/coloration stage; primarily from *V. vinifera* variety "Cabernet Franc" in the Rheingau region (Germany) for AS 195; other European wine regions for other commercial preparations.

The traditional uses (wound healing, anti-inflammatory, astringent, anti-hemorrhagic) form a different cluster than the modern venous insufficiency indication — the classical convergent indication is topical wound and inflammation management, while the modern pharmaceutical CVI indication has been developed specifically from the autumn red-leaf extract standardized preparation.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

The grape leaf's culinary tradition (dolmas/stuffed vine leaves across Middle Eastern, Greek, Turkish, and Balkan cuisines) documents continuous whole-population exposure to leaf polyphenols in the diet — a potential contributor to the Mediterranean dietary cardiovascular protection hypothesis alongside olive oil, red wine, and fish. The leaf preparation in cuisine uses young green spring leaves (different chemistry from the autumn red pharmaceutical leaves), but the overlap is significant enough to suggest a pharmacological dietary connection.

The AS 195 extract (Antistax) was developed from the observation that autumn red vine leaves contain the highest concentrations of both flavonoids (especially isoquercitrin/quercetin-3-glucuronide) and anthocyanins — the combination providing a dual anti-edema mechanism (flavonoid capillary tone + anthocyanin endothelial protection). This is a different pharmacological approach from grape seed OPCs (I-516) or horse chestnut aescin (I-507) for CVI — reflecting the diversity of available CVI botanical approaches with distinct mechanisms.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Flavonoids (primary in autumn red leaves):**
- **Isoquercitrin** (quercetin-3-glucoside): 2–4% DW in autumn red leaves; primary CVI-active flavonoid
- **Quercetin-3-glucuronide**: 1–3% DW; co-primary
- **Kaempferol-3-glucoside**: 0.5–2% DW
- **Myricetin-3-glucoside**: 0.3–1.5% DW
- **Quercetin**: aglycone, trace
- PhEur *Vitis vinifera* folium rubrum — ≥6.0% total flavonoids expressed as isoquercitrin; this standardization is the quality marker distinguishing pharmaceutical-grade autumn red leaf from standard grape leaf

**Anthocyanins (autumn red pigmentation):**
- **Cyanidin-3-glucoside**: 1–4% DW in red leaves; primary anthocyanin
- **Cyanidin-3-glucoside-5-glucoside**: minor
- **Malvidin-3-glucoside**: present (same as in red wine)
- Lower than fruit/skin anthocyanin but higher than green leaves

**OPCs (catechin-based):**
- Procyanidin B₁, B₂: 0.5–2% DW; lower OPC content than seed
- Catechin, epicatechin: 0.5–2% DW

**Phenolic acids:**
- **Trans-caftaric acid** (caffeic acid + tartaric acid conjugate): characteristic grape compound; 0.5–2% DW; anti-inflammatory; antioxidant
- **Trans-coutaric acid**: caffeic acid/p-coumaric acid-tartaric acid conjugate; characteristic
- **Chlorogenic acid**: present

**Tartaric acid:**
- High tartaric acid content in all grape tissue (distinguishing characteristic of *Vitis* spp.); present in leaves; mildly antioxidant contribution

**AS 195 extract standardization:**
- Aqueous-ethanolic extract of autumn red vine leaves
- Standardized to ≥6% total flavonoids (as isoquercitrin)
- One Antistax tablet = 360 mg extract = minimum 21.6 mg total flavonoids
- Clinical trials use 360–720 mg extract/day = 2–4 tablets AS 195

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Venous endothelial toning (isoquercitrin primary):**
Isoquercitrin (quercetin-3-glucoside) and quercetin-3-glucuronide:
1. **eNOS activation:** quercetin glycosides activate endothelial NO synthase via Akt/PKB phosphorylation → increased NO → venous smooth muscle relaxation + vasodilation
2. **Prostaglandin I₂ (prostacyclin) modulation:** flavonoids increase vascular prostacyclin synthesis → vasodilatory; anti-platelet aggregation
3. **Reduced expression of adhesion molecules** (VCAM-1, ICAM-1, E-selectin): reduce leukocyte-endothelial interaction → reduce venous wall inflammation
4. **Reduced venous wall permeability:** isoquercitrin cross-links basement membrane glycoproteins (similar to OPC collagen-binding but flavonoid-mediated) → reduced plasma protein leakage → reduced edema

**Anthocyanin contribution (cyanidin-3-glucoside):**
- Inhibits catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) → increased tissue catecholamines → mild venous constriction tone
- Capillary permeability reduction (same mechanism as bilberry I-513 anthocyanins)
- LDL oxidation protection

**Anti-inflammatory:**
- Quercetin: COX-2 inhibition; 5-LOX inhibition; NF-κB suppression
- Caftaric acid: antioxidant; NF-κB contribution
- Relevant to venous wall inflammatory component of CVI

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) — Level A:**

1. **Rabe E et al. 2011** (*International Angiology* 30:461-468) — n=242 CVI women; AS 195 (360 mg/day) vs placebo, 12 weeks; significant reduction in ankle volume (+6.0 mL vs -17.5 mL placebo; p<0.001); reduced heaviness, pain, pruritus, fatigue; Level A

2. **Kiesewetter H et al. 2000** (*International Angiology* 19:220-225) — n=101; AS 195 360 mg/day vs placebo, 12 weeks; significant ankle swelling reduction; Level A

3. **Kalus U et al. 2004** (*Arzneimittelforschung* 54:245-252) — n=67; AS 195 720 mg/day (higher dose); significant reduction in lower leg volume vs placebo; Level A; dose-dependent edema reduction confirmed

4. **Belcaro G et al. 2011** (*Angiology* 62:553-563) — comparison of several botanical CVI preparations; AS 195 included; consistent with positive outcome vs no-treatment; Level B (open-label comparison)

**Commission E (1994):** APPROVED for chronic venous insufficiency, heavy legs, feelings of heaviness and tension in the legs (red vine leaf dry extract, aqueous-ethanolic; 360–720 mg/day).

**EMA well-established use (2016):** *Vitis vinifera* L., folium rubrum (red vine leaf) — well-established use for symptomatic relief of discomfort and heaviness of legs related to minor venous insufficiency; also traditional use for same indication.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Stuffed vine leaves and the culinary-medical boundary:**
The dolma tradition (Turkish/Arabic *dolma* = "filled thing") — young grape leaves stuffed with rice, meat, pine nuts, herbs, and wrapped into cigar shapes — represents one of the most ancient documented culinary preparations in the archaeological record (Ottoman period documentation, but Mesopotamian origin probable given AT tablet documentation of vine leaf preparations). The young spring leaves used for dolmas are chemically different from the autumn red leaves of pharmaceutical interest (lower anthocyanin, higher chlorophyll, more tartaric acid), but the culinary tradition represents sustained multi-millennial exposure of Mediterranean and Middle Eastern populations to grape leaf phytochemicals.

**Grape leaf in wound management (Ebers Papyrus tradition):**
Egyptian medical documents consistently prescribe grape (juice, wine, leaves) for wound management — the Ebers Papyrus records grape leaf poultices for infected wounds and eye inflammation. The mechanism is the tannin-OPC astringent wound contraction + anthocyanin antimicrobial + anti-inflammatory combination. The oldest documented wound management preparation in the Codex that includes grape may be from Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE), making grape leaf one of the pharmacologically oldest entries in the entire volume.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Standard doses:**
- **AS 195 extract (360 mg/tablet):** 2 tablets (720 mg) in the morning; or 1 tablet twice daily; standard clinical dose
- **Dried leaf tea:** 5 g per 250 mL water, 10 min steep; 3× daily; traditional preparation; lower standardized flavonoid dose than extract
- **Duration:** 4–8 weeks minimum; reassess at 12 weeks; long-term use acceptable

**Commission E approved dose:** 360–720 mg standardized red vine leaf dry extract per day (standardized to ≥6% flavonoids as isoquercitrin).

**CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- Pregnancy: insufficient safety data; avoid
- Hypersensitivity to *Vitis vinifera*

**Drug interactions:**
- Anticoagulants: mild antiplatelet contribution (quercetin); monitor INR in warfarin patients
- Antihypertensives: additive vasodilatory effect; monitor blood pressure

**Positioning among CVI botanicals:**
| Botanical | Primary mechanism | Evidence tier | Cochrane? |
|-----------|-------------------|---------------|-----------|
| Horse chestnut (I-507) | Aescin capillary pore sealing | Cochrane A | Yes |
| Witch hazel (I-506) | Hamamelitannin venous wall toning | Level A | No |
| Grape seed (I-516) | OPC-B collagen cross-link | Level A | No |
| Red vine leaf (I-517) | Isoquercitrin/anthocyanin endothelial | Level A | No |

All four approaches have Level A clinical evidence; horse chestnut has the strongest regulatory recognition and only Cochrane review; the others are Commission E/EMA well-established use. Combination strategies are used clinically but lack specific combination RCT evidence.
