# Tibetan antelope blood — *gtsod* (Hodgson's antelope) blood, "the blood of Hodgson's antelope" (Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20)
## *The blood of the animal the Tibetan text calls* gtsod*; Clark prints "Hodgson's Antelope" with the Latin name* Pantholops hodgsoni *(printed* Pantholopshodgsoni *at the horn entry), the animal English calls the Tibetan antelope* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is the Tibetan *Four Tantras*. In Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; "Clark" below) the animal medicines close the materia medica of chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra with the sentence "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Blood is the fourth of the thirteen. The blood paragraph (lines 1173-1177) opens with deer blood and goat blood, and the sentence for this entry is at line 1173: "The blood of wild yak and of Hodgson's antelope (*gtsod*): halts diarrhoea."

The animal is named at the horn entry, line 1155: "*gtsod*: Hodgson's Antelope: Pantholopshodgsoni: [has seven names. It heals wounds and is oxytocic (facilitates childbirth)]." Clark's introduction (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets are taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, and those in parentheses from the oral instructions of Dr. Yeshe Donden and Dr. Pema Dorje. So "has seven names" and the horn's actions are in the commentary layer. In the blood sentence at line 1173 the claim itself is unbracketed, and only the Tibetan name "(*gtsod*)" is a parenthetical interpolation from the oral instructions; that is, the naming of the antelope by its Tibetan word in this sentence is Clark's addition, and the blood claim is a plain one-line statement, shared with the blood of the wild yak.

The same animal's blood returns in a second place. In Clark's list of medicines by the disorder they treat, "the class of anti-diarrhoeals consists of both wild and cultivated types of Bael fruit, *Schizandra sphaerandra*, Acorn, powdered root (of, for example, old pine tree), Plantain (*Plantago depressa*), *Plantago lanceolata*, Bastard Teak, the blood of Hodgson's antelope and Larkspur" (line 1222). That puts the blood in a named class with plant medicines, as a member of the anti-diarrhoeal group.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`; "MTK" below) gives the same claim in its own wording. At line 3540: "The blood of wild yaks and antelopes stop diarrhea." And in its list of groups of medicines (line 3645): "...antelope's blood and Jhakang are the group of medicines that cure diarrhea". The MTK glossary on animals (line 7064, with the Latin at line 7066) gives "Antelope: *Pantholops hodgsoni* Abel", agreeing with Clark's identification of the *gtsod* animal. *Pantholops hodgsonii* is, in English, the Tibetan antelope or chiru; that is a general fact and not a statement of the Tibetan text.

`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` has no row for *gtsod* (a search for the string returned nothing). The spine's locus is the Clark line 1173 given above. No row for this blood was found in `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv`. No other tradition's text on disk was found to name it; the Ebers list names the gazelle and antelope dung and fat, which are different animals and parts, and are not folded in here.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, blood is a fluid tissue of water, plasma proteins (including albumin, globulins and fibrinogen), red cells carrying haemoglobin, white cells, platelets, salts and dissolved nutrients. It clots when shed. Dried or processed blood of a wild animal may also carry its pathogens. The Tibetan text gives no mechanism for the anti-diarrhoeal action claimed.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The record is one line in a list of animal blood medicines: this blood, with that of the wild yak, halts diarrhoea. The Codex does not recommend taking blood from a wild animal for any purpose. The animal is a protected species, the blood of a wild animal is a hygiene hazard, and diarrhoea has safer remedies, the first of which is replacing lost fluid. The material is also bound up with conservation. No protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither text gives a preparation (fresh, dried, in a compound), a quantity or a dose for this blood. The only expression of its use is as one member of the anti-diarrhoeal class in Clark's list (line 1222). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] Clark remarks that "nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57), a remark that applies to the whole animal section.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Diarrhoea can cause dehydration and loss of salts, and the main treatment is oral rehydration; persistent or bloody diarrhoea, or diarrhoea in infants, the elderly or the ill, needs medical assessment. Animal blood carries a risk of bacterial and viral infection and of parasites, and is not a safe medicine. The Tibetan antelope is protected by law and its trade is prohibited under international agreement (CITES Appendix I). No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi.** No taste, potency or post-digestive taste is printed for this blood in the passages read (Clark lines 1173 and 1222; MTK lines 3540 and 3645). The only quality attached to it is the action "halts diarrhoea". The Tibetan system's three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) are not named in connection with this blood in those passages, so no humoral action is stated.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The frame is the thirteen-part animal section of the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica (Clark line 1148), and in it the blood is a drug for a stated action, halting diarrhoea (line 1173), and a member of the anti-diarrhoeal class (line 1222). Nothing in the passages read places it in any practice of essence extraction (*bcud len*), and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, so none is asserted here. The tradition's wider frame is that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching the root; this entry adds nothing to that.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports cultivation use. The blood appears in the sources as one line in a list of animal bloods, with a single action, in a part of the materia medica that Clark says is little used today. A cultivator has nothing to take from this entry; the honest practice is abstention, which also spares a protected animal.

### Contraindications

- **Law and conservation:** the Tibetan antelope is a protected species and trade in its products is prohibited in most countries; taking its blood is not lawful or ethical, and a product sold as such cannot be trusted to be what the text means.
- **Hygiene and infection:** the blood of a wild animal can carry bacteria, viruses and parasites; it should not be eaten, drunk or applied.
- **Diarrhoea:** needs fluid replacement and, if severe, bloody or prolonged, or in infants and the frail, medical care; do not delay it for an animal remedy.
- **Identification:** the animal is taken from Clark's gloss and the MTK glossary, not from any independent check; this entry does not say that any product on sale is the *gtsod* of the text.
