# Bat flesh — bya.wang.sha, "Bat flesh" (Clark 1995, rGyud-bzhi Explanatory Tantra ch.20)
## *The flesh of the animal Clark renders "bat"; the Men-Tsee-Khang translation reads the corresponding item as pheasant, so the identification is disputed between the two translations* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

This entry is the Tibetan "bat flesh" of Clark's translation. The Egyptian bat of the Ebers Papyrus is a separate spine row, `III_43_Bat.md`; the two entries cross-refer and nothing in either text links them.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The locus is Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20, "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin", under the sub-heading "Meat" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, heading at line 1147, sub-heading at line 1161, the item at line 1170). The entry, with its neighbours in the same run: "Wolf's stomach: [has twelve names] It increases digestive heat and digests undigested food, [especially meat]. Its tongue removes swellings of the tongue. Pig tongue: removes bone warts. Dog tongue: [has ten names and] heals all types of wounds. Donkey tongue: [has fourteen names]. It halts diarrhoea. ... Lungs of fox (wa) and of khug.rta: Cuculus melanoleucus: Riparia riparia: Hirundo rustica: (a type of swallow): heal punctured lungs. bya.wang.sha: Bat flesh: [has thirteen names]. It is anti-emetic [and dispels spirits]. mchhil.pa: Sparrow: Passer montanus: [has seven names]. nas.zan bird (lit. 'barley feeder'): Asian sparrow: [has ten names]. rtsangs.pa: a type of Gecko: Agama himalayana sacra: [has eleven names, for example sder.chhags. The flesh of] these three creatures increases semen, [is aphrodisiac, tonic and anodyne, and drains pus.]" In Clark's convention the square brackets enclose material from the commentary, so "anti-emetic" is the root-text claim and "[and dispels spirits]" is commentary. The Tibetan name is printed by Clark as *bya.wang.sha*; the Tibetan itself was not checked. The index of the same file lists "Bat flesh" (line 1562). Neither `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` nor `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` has a bat row; the spine row rests on the Clark line.

**The Men-Tsee-Khang reading.** The 2011 translation of the same passage (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 3536) reads in the same slot: "The lungs of foxes and red-rumped swallows stop haemoptysis. The meat of pheasants stops vomiting. The meat of sparrows and mountain-dwelling lizards increase semen." The position of the "pheasant" item (after the fox and swallow lungs, before the sparrows and lizards) is the position of Clark's "bat flesh", and "stops vomiting" is Clark's "anti-emetic". The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary on animals lists a pheasant (*Crossoptilon crassoptilon*, line 6914) and, among its Tibetan bird names, "Jhawang" (nearby in the OCR column, not reliably paired). On that evidence the two translations disagree about which animal the item is. Whether the Tibetan means a bat, a pheasant or another animal is not settled by the English texts. [USER: verify]

**A second bat-glossed item.** Clark also lists, in the section on hair and feathers (line 1195), "bya.ma.byi: Fruit Bat: Vespertilio superans: [has sixteen names]. Its [tail] feathers expel womb disorders." A bat has no feathers, so the translator's gloss for this item is doubtful; the item is recorded here because it carries the same gloss, and it is not the flesh. [USER: verify] Clark's list of the edible meats (lines 672-673, nine sections) does not name a bat, and a text search of the Men-Tsee-Khang file for the word "bat" returned nothing.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, if the animal is a bat, its flesh is muscle protein with little fat, and bats are reservoir hosts of several zoonotic viruses (rabies and related lyssaviruses, coronaviruses, Nipah and Hendra viruses). If the item is a pheasant, it is a lean game bird meat.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text records one use for the item, as an anti-emetic (and, in the commentary, against spirits). Given that the identification of the animal is disputed between the two translations, and that bats carry dangerous viruses, the Codex gives no protocol and recommends no use of bat flesh.

### Preparation & Dosing

None is given. Clark's entries in this run give the property of the meat and no preparation, and the Men-Tsee-Khang line gives none either. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Bats are reservoirs of lyssaviruses and of several emerging zoonotic viruses; contact with a bat's tissues and eating bat meat are recognised routes of human exposure. There is no established medical use of bat flesh as an anti-emetic. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* The item sits in the Explanatory Tantra's section on medicines of animal origin, under "Meat", as a therapeutic substance with one stated action (anti-emetic), and in the commentary one more (dispelling spirits). No cultivation status is attributed to it. The Tibetan frame is that pathology is at root a spiritual condition and that medicine is one of four treatment branches, supporting practice but not reaching the root; "dispels spirits" is a commentary gloss on a therapeutic action and is not read as a cultivation status. No *bcud len* use was found. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted. The identity of the animal is itself disputed between the two translations.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. The sourced claim is a single therapeutic line, the animal is uncertain, and a bat is a recognised carrier of lethal viruses. A cultivator takes no practice from this entry. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Infection:** bat flesh and handling bats risk rabies and other zoonotic viruses; contact with a bat calls for medical advice.
- **Identity:** the two translations disagree about the animal; nothing labelled "bat flesh" or "pheasant" can be assumed to match the source.
- **Conservation and law:** many bat species are protected and may not be killed or traded in many jurisdictions. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** killing a protected animal for a remedy of no demonstrated benefit cannot be defended.
- **Vomiting:** persistent vomiting needs medical assessment, not a traditional meat.
