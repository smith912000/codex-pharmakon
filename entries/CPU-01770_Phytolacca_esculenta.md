# Phytolacca esculenta — dpab.wo, Pawo karpo, Pawo serpo (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Phytolacca esculenta, printed by Clark 1995 as the first of three binomials for dpab.wo and tied there to the "white type" ("Phytolacca esculenta (white type)"); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints no esculenta and sets Phytolacca acinosa against both "Pawo karpo" and "Pawo serpo"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and under this binomial on Clark's edition alone.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Ebers, Assyrian, Sushruta and Shennong text on disk for *Phytolacca* found only the Shennong text (Yang 1998), which has *Shang Lu* (*Radix Phytolaccae Acinosae*, line 3734, with a translator's note at line 3743). That is another species and another tradition's entry (see I_621), and it is not read as this drug. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 99) the row is keyed by the Tibetan name *dpab.wo*, with the identification "Phytolacca esculenta (white type); Scutellaria baicalensis; Phytolacca acinosa (yellow type)", the locus "Clark 1995 (line 964)" and the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The spine row (CPU-01770) took the **first** of the three binomials.

**Identity doubt.** Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" and (line 68) that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect". Here the one Tibetan name is given a pokeweed for the white type, a skullcap (a different family) in the middle, and a second pokeweed for the yellow type, and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints the second pokeweed for both colours. The two editions therefore disagree on which species is the white type. The Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 964).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792). The entry stands after the heading "(5) Resins" (line 879) and before "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997); the file prints no "(6)" heading, although the opening list of sections (line 802) names "(6) plants". It follows *hkhyung.sder* (*Uncaria*, line 963) and precedes *bong.nga.dkar.po* (white aconite). It reads:

"dpab.wo: Phytolacca esculenta (white type): Scutellaria baicalensis: Phytolacca acinosa (yellow type): [For example, the yellow type grows in soft ground, has small yellow flower and a hollow yellow root. It has bitter taste, cool power and neutralizes poison. This yellow type is distinct only by virtue of its color and is beneficial in horse and mule diseases. The wild type resembles the white one (but has roots shaped like potatoes with tendrils), and is known as nar.ma.nur.mar. All three types] cure fever caused by poisoning."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The whole description, the taste and power, and the veterinary remark are inside the brackets; the commentary describes **the yellow type** in detail and gives the white type only by comparison ("The wild type resembles the white one"). "Cure fever caused by poisoning" is outside the brackets and is the root-text layer as Clark's marks stand. Clark's Latin index lists *Phytolacca esculenta* and *Phytolacca acinosa* (line 1626), and his index of Tibetan names prints "dpah.wo" (line 1715), which the Codex reads as an OCR variant of *dpab.wo*. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, chapter 21 (line 1212).* In Clark's chapter 21 ("The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines", heading at lines 1205 and 1206), the class of poison-neutralizing medicines reads, in part: "Musk, Red, Yellow and White Aconites, White and Brown Uncaria, (white) Phytolacca esculenta (var. Houtte), (a type of Pokeberry), (yellow) Scutellaria baicalensis, Pleurospermum hookeri, Senecio dianthus, Scutellaria, Oxytropis chiliophylla, ...". Here the white type is again *Phytolacca esculenta* (with "var. Houtte" as printed), but the yellow type is *Scutellaria baicalensis*, and "a type of Pokeberry" stands between them without a colour. At line 964 the yellow type is *Phytolacca acinosa*. Clark's two lines therefore give the yellow type different binomials. The Codex records the difference and does not resolve it.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (read through the name, not the binomial).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. Chapter 20, within the run of efficacies that precedes the herbal run (the sentence runs across lines 3420 and 3421): "White and brown Khyungdher cures hot disorders associated with poisoning. Pawo karpo, Pawo serpo and Pagoe have the same potency as Khyungdher." Chapter 21, line 3604: "La tsi, Bongnga karpo, Bongnga serpo, Bongnga marpo, Khungdher karpo and mukpo, Pawo karpo and serpo, Tsad, Nampar Guthup, Tak- / sha, ... are the group of medicines that cure poisoning." The glossary's "P" block prints one line, "Pawo karpo dpa' bo dkar po Phytolacca acinosa" (line 5600), then "Pawo serpo dpa' bo ser po" (line 5602) and "Phytolacca acinosa" (line 5604), with the English "Pokeberry" printed once, in a later column (line 5659). The index lists "Pawo karpo 205, 220" and "Pawo serpo 205" (line 7459). "Karpo" and "serpo" are Tibetan colour words ("white" and "yellow") in the names, and the glossary does not translate them. The Codex reads *Pawo karpo* as Clark's white type and *Pawo serpo* as his yellow type from the colour words and from Clark's white and yellow types in line 964; neither text says so in terms. The sentence "same potency as Khyungdher" names a drug that the Codex reads as Clark's *hkhyung.sder* (*Uncaria*, line 963, "cure [all kinds of] fever [caused by] poisoning") from the spelling (I_1261 records the glossary's positional pairing of "Uncaria" with it); the Codex records the sentence and does not turn it into a quality.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Phytolacca esculenta*, *dpab.wo*, *Pawo karpo* and *Pawo serpo* found no entry on this plant. I_621 (*Phytolacca acinosa*, tags CM, DMM, SBJ, SS) is the pokeweed entry of other traditions and carries no FMT tag; it is not merged, because it is another species in the Codex's reading and another set of texts. I_1261 (*Curcuma zedoaria*, "Pagoe"), I_1288 (*Gentiana jatamansi*) and I_1114 (*Lancea tibetica*, which follows *Pawo serpo* in the glossary run) quote the same Men-Tsee-Khang lines. I_1322 (*Myricaria elegans*) and I_1330 (*Peucedanum praeruptorum*) quote the neighbouring Clark list at line 1212. I_324 (*Scutellaria baicalensis*, tag SBJ) is the skullcap entry of the Shennong text. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Phytolacca* is the pokeweed genus (Phytolaccaceae), that pokeweeds are generally regarded as toxic when eaten raw, the root and the unripe berry most of all, and that they are generally reported to contain saponins. Which compounds the Tibetan drug holds, and which of its candidate plants it is, is not shown by the lines read, so no statement about its composition is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that cures "fever caused by poisoning" (Clark, line 964), whose yellow type "neutralizes poison" (commentary layer) and which belongs to the poison-neutralizing and poison-curing groups of both editions (Clark, line 1212; Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3604). These are the texts' claims in their own terms, attached to this binomial only through the name, and they are not Codex protocols. Pokeweed roots are toxic, and the Codex gives no protocol and no dose. A poisoning needs medical care, and no plant should be used as an antidote on the strength of this entry.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used (beyond the commentary's remark that the yellow type has a "hollow yellow root"), no quantity, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 964 prints, inside the brackets and **for the yellow type only**, "bitter taste, cool power". No taste or power is printed for the white type, to which this binomial is tied, and the Codex does not carry the yellow type's qualities over to it. The Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3420, 3604, 5600) print none; line 3421 says only that the *Pawo* drugs have "the same potency as Khyungdher" and prints no potency for *Khyungdher*.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The materia medica classes this drug by what it does: it cures fever caused by poisoning and belongs to the poison group. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, lines 3900 to 3950; Clark's chapter 23 is headed "Normal health", lines 1262 to 1276), and Clark's materia medica names other drugs as essence-extraction ingredients (for example lines 853, 971 and 1144), none of them this one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name and not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is an antidote-class drug of uncertain identity, from a genus generally regarded as toxic, with no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Pokeweeds are generally regarded as poisonous when eaten raw, and the plant behind the Tibetan name is uncertain; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Poisoning needs medical care. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
