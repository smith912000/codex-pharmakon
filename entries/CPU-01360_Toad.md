# Toad — Belpa (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary phonetic); "Frog" (Men-Tsee-Khang common name); Bufo bufo gargarizans Cantor (Men-Tsee-Khang scientific name)
## *The animal the project lists as "toad" on the strength of one Latin name in the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary on animals. The English name in that position is "Frog", neither text prints the word "toad", and neither text gives a medicinal use for it* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is the Tibetan *Four Tantras*, and the attestation is a glossary row. The locus is the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`; "MTK" below), Glossary on Animals, line 7191 (the project's name extract, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, takes "Bufo bufo" from this line and notes that "Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)").

The glossary has an "Amphibians" table (lines 7173-7193) whose columns the OCR has separated. The phonetic column reads "Belpa, Chhu ein, Daji, Dikpa, Dikcin, Sam" (lines 7179-7183); the common-name column reads "Frog, Crocodile, Snow Frog, Scorpion, Crab, Otter" (lines 7185-7189); and the scientific-name column reads "Bufo bufo gargarizans Cantor Crocodylus L. Batrachuporus Butnus martensi kansc pinchonii Charybdis japonica Lutra luta Linnaeus" (lines 7191-7193). Read in order, the first phonetic name, "Belpa", the first common name, "Frog", and the first scientific name, *Bufo bufo gargarizans* Cantor, belong together; the same order holds for the crocodile, crab and otter at the end of the three columns. The pairing is by order, not by a printed link. [USER: verify]

The English word "toad" does not occur in either of the Tibetan texts on disk. A search of MTK and of Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; "Clark" below) found "Bufo" only at this line. *Bufo bufo* is the Latin name of the common toad, and *B. b. gargarizans* a subspecies, and this is the only basis for calling the entry "toad". The glossary itself calls the animal "Frog".

What the texts say of "frog" in general is little:

- **Classification.** The frog is listed among the burrowing animals: "The marmot, porcupine, frog, snake, badger, lizard, mountain-dwelling lizard and scorpion are the group of animals that live in burrows" (MTK line 2918); Clark's group (h) reads "Burrowing animals include the marmot (Marmota bobak), porcupine, frog, snake, Tibetan badger, lizard (Eremias argus), gecko, and scorpion" (line 672). MTK's index lists "frog 162, 230" (line 7366): p.162 matches that classification, and p.230 is presumably the instrument below (the page number of line 3825 was not confirmed).
- **No medicinal use.** Neither the materia medica of Clark's chapter 20 (line 1148 on) nor MTK's equivalent passage (lines 3500-3575) gives an action for a frog or toad as such. The only amphibian medicine in those passages is the snow frog (*da.pyid*; Clark line 1169; MTK line 3534), which has its own entry.
- **Things named for the frog.** Clark prints two stones named for it, *smug.po.sbal.rgyab* ("the brown frog's back stone", hematite) and *dkar.po.sbal.rgyab* ("the white frog's back stone", lines 817-818), and a plant with leaves "shaped like frogs' feet" (line 1032). MTK names a surgical instrument, *balgo* ("lit. frog head"), used to "vent off accumulated pressure" and "to withdraw hepatic and pericardial effusion" (line 3825). These are stones, a plant and an instrument, and not the animal.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, many toads carry toxins in glands of the skin and behind the head, which in some species act on the heart; the flesh of any amphibian is otherwise mainly water and protein. Which, if any, of this applies to the animal in the glossary is not known from the sources. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts on disk list the frog among the burrowing animals and give no medicinal use for it, so the entry records a name and a classification only. Handling or taking toad or frog material is a toxicity and hygiene risk, and no preparation is sourced. The Codex does not recommend it.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation and no quantity are given for the frog or toad in the texts read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Many toads secrete skin toxins, including compounds that affect the heart, and wild amphibians can carry bacteria and parasites. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** No taste, potency or post-digestive taste is printed for the frog or toad in either text. The meat statements (Clark line 673; MTK line 2923) apply to the nine groups of meats in general. Neither text says which of the burrowing animals are dry-land, wet-land or amphibious, so no class is assigned to this animal.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* The attestation is a glossary row for an animal that the same glossary calls "Frog", and the texts give no medicinal use for it, so there is nothing for a cultivation frame to attach to. Nothing in the passages read places it in any practice of essence extraction (*bcud len*), and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, so none is asserted. The wider frame is that the three humors are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration (Clark line 51), so disease is at root a spiritual condition, and that food, behaviour, medicine and accessory therapy are the four supports of treatment (line 1299), of which medicine is the third. The chapter on routine behaviour asks the practitioner to refrain from the ten negative actions, the first of which is "taking life" (line 616).

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The sources name the animal in a glossary and a classification and give no use for it. The Codex does not recommend it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Toxicity:** many toads carry skin toxins that can be dangerous when handled or ingested; the animal's species is not securely established from the sources.
- **Identity:** the sources call it a frog and give a toad's Latin name; do not substitute one for the other, or either for the snow frog.
- **Legal and conservation status:** amphibians are declining and are protected in many regions; the status of the animal here is unknown.
- **Ethics:** the Codex does not recommend taking an animal's life for these uses.
