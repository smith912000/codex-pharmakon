# I-250: Origanum vulgare (Oregano / Wild Marjoram)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-250 | Lamiaceae (Labiatae)**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Origanum vulgare* L.
**Family:** Lamiaceae (Labiatae)

**Common Names:**
- English: Oregano, Wild Marjoram, Common Marjoram
- Greek: *Origanon* (ὀρίγανον) — from *oros* (mountain) + *ganos* (joy/brightness) = "joy of the mountain"
- Arabic: *Za'tar* (زعتر) — NOTE: Za'tar refers to a complex of Mediterranean aromatic herbs (Thymus, Origanum, Satureja) and is NOT species-specific; context determines which genus
- Persian: *Marzanjosh*
- Turkish: *Kekik* (also applied to thyme)
- Hebrew: *Ezov* (some authorities identify Biblical hyssop as O. syriacum, not O. vulgare)
- Latin: *Origanum* (direct from Greek); *vulgare* = common

**Taxonomy — Critical Disambiguation:**
The genus *Origanum* contains ~40 species; medically relevant:
- *O. vulgare* — Common Oregano (this entry)
- *O. syriacum* — Syrian Oregano / Za'tar (Middle Eastern primary medicinal; higher carvacrol/thymol)
- *O. marjorana* — Sweet Marjoram (botanically distinct; different aromatic profile; separate entry I-251)
- *O. compactum* — Moroccan Oregano (high carvacrol; strong antimicrobial)
- *Thymbra spicata* — Spiked Thyme / Za'tar (confusion species)

**Chemotype variation in O. vulgare:**
European oregano has highly variable chemotype distribution:
1. **Carvacrol chemotype** (Mediterranean; highest antimicrobial): 40–86% carvacrol — the commercially preferred "oil of oregano"
2. **Thymol chemotype**: 30–60% thymol (pharmacologically parallel to thyme, I-247)
3. **Sabinene hydrate chemotype** (northern European): Mild; low carvacrol/thymol; culinary but less antimicrobial
4. **Linalool/terpinen-4-ol chemotype**: Gentle; mild antimicrobial

Commercial "oil of oregano" is predominantly carvacrol chemotype from Mediterranean O. vulgare or O. compactum — this distinction from northern European O. vulgare (low carvacrol) is clinically critical.

**Morphology:** Aromatic perennial herb; 20–80 cm; rounded opposite leaves; purple-pink flowers in dense clusters; highly aromatic (carvacrol/thymol-dominant in Mediterranean varieties). Native to Mediterranean and Asian mountains; now cultivated globally.

**Part Used:** Aerial parts (leaves, flowering tops); essential oil; hydroalcoholic extracts.

- **Ancient Greek symbol of happiness:** Couples wore oregano crowns at weddings; planted on graves to ensure happiness in afterlife; the etymology (*joy of the mountain*) reflects cultural integration

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Essential Oil (0.5–4.5%; carvacrol chemotype)

**1. Carvacrol (5-isopropyl-2-methylphenol) — 40–86% of oil**
- Monoterpene phenol; positional isomer of thymol
- **Primary bioactive — antimicrobial mechanism:**
  - Intercalates in phospholipid bilayer → disrupts bacterial cell membrane integrity
  - Reduces membrane potential → ion leakage → bacterial cell death
  - Inhibits ATP synthase → energy depletion
  - Disrupts biofilm formation at sub-MIC concentrations
- **MIC values:**
  - *S. aureus* (MRSA): 0.031–0.25 mg/mL
  - *E. coli*: 0.125–0.5 mg/mL
  - *Listeria monocytogenes*: 0.031 mg/mL
  - *Candida albicans*: 0.0625–0.25 mg/mL
  - *Helicobacter pylori*: 0.0078 mg/mL (one of lowest MIC values for natural compound)
- **Anti-inflammatory:** NF-κB inhibition; PPAR-γ activation → TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 reduction
- **Anti-parasitic:** Direct antiparasitic effect on Giardia, Cryptosporidium (in vitro)
- **Anti-tumor:** Induces apoptosis in multiple cancer cell lines; Bcl-2 downregulation; caspase-3 activation; research active

**2. Thymol — 0–15% (lower than thyme; carvacrol dominant in Mediterranean chemotypes)**
- Parallel antimicrobial mechanism; synergistic with carvacrol

**3. p-Cymene — 5–20%**
- Biosynthetic precursor; membrane fluidizer → synergistic with carvacrol/thymol for antimicrobial activity

**4. γ-Terpinene, β-Ocimene, β-Caryophyllene — minor terpenes**
- β-Caryophyllene: CB2 agonist; anti-inflammatory; present 2–8%

**5. Linalool — Variable (high in northern European/linalool chemotypes)**

### Non-Volatile Constituents

**6. Rosmarinic Acid — 2–5% (among highest botanical sources)**
- Anti-inflammatory; antiviral; antioxidant; GABA-T inhibition
- Oregano is one of the highest rosmarinic acid sources in the plant kingdom

**7. Luteolin, Apigenin, Naringenin, Quercetin, Kaempferol — flavonoids**

**8. Salvianolic acid B — antioxidant phenolic**

**9. Oleanolic acid, Ursolic acid — triterpene acids**
- Anti-inflammatory; anti-tumor; hepatoprotective

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 1. Broad-Spectrum Antimicrobial (Carvacrol + Thymol — Primary)
Identical mechanism to thyme (I-247) with carvacrol as primary active rather than thymol:
- Membrane disruption; ATP depletion; ion leakage; biofilm inhibition
- Notably: H. pylori MIC extraordinarily low (0.0078 mg/mL) — relevant to gastric infection

**Anti-MRSA synergism:** Carvacrol combined with certain β-lactam antibiotics shows enhanced activity against MRSA strains in vitro — research area but no clinical trials yet.

### 2. NF-κB and PPAR-γ Anti-inflammatory Pathway
- Carvacrol + rosmarinic acid: NF-κB inhibition → reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines
- Carvacrol: PPAR-γ agonism → anti-inflammatory nuclear receptor activation; adipogenesis regulation (metabolic effects)
- Clinical relevance: Anti-inflammatory applicable in GI inflammation (IBD in vitro data)

### 3. Anti-parasitic Mechanism
- Carvacrol: Disrupts protozoan cell membranes (Giardia, Cryptosporidium); direct membrane toxicity
- In vitro Giardia data: Carvacrol at 0.5 mg/mL reduces Giardia trophozoite viability >90%
- Traditional vermifuge use validated biochemically

### 4. Antioxidant Defense
- Rosmarinic acid + luteolin + quercetin: Superoxide and peroxyl radical scavenging
- ORAC value of oregano among highest of all herbs tested (Zheng & Wang 2001: oregano ORAC 200,129 μmol TE/100g — extraordinary; compare blueberries at ~9,621)
- Net: Strong free radical protection; relevant to oxidative stress-related disease prevention

### 5. Estrogenic Activity (Mild)
- Luteolin, naringenin: Weak ER-β agonism; phytoestrogenic; clinical significance at culinary doses minimal

### 6. Anti-tumor (Research-Stage)
- Carvacrol: Multiple cancer cell lines (breast, colon, prostate, leukemia); apoptosis via caspase-3/9 activation; Bcl-2/Bax ratio shift; anti-metastatic (reduces MMP expression)
- Rosmarinic acid: Anti-proliferative; anti-angiogenic
- Ursolic acid: CDK inhibition; cell cycle arrest
- All Level D (in vitro); no clinical trials in cancer

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

### H. pylori Eradication

**Sivropoulou et al. 1996 (Journal of Agricultural Food Chemistry); Stamatis et al. 1999:**
- In vitro: Oregano oil MIC against H. pylori 0.0078 mg/mL (extremely low; competitive with metronidazole)
- In vivo human data: Limited; Borrelli et al. 2007 pilot (N=7 uncontrolled); partial H. pylori suppression with commercial oregano oil; not adequate for clinical recommendation
- Clinical evidence: Level D (in vitro; case series only)

### Anti-fungal / Vaginal Candidiasis

**Manohar et al. 2001:** Carvacrol equivalent to amphotericin B against systemic Candida in mouse model; Level D.

**Stiles et al. 1995:** Oregano oil inhibits Candida growth in vitro at concentrations achievable with topical preparations; Level D.

Clinical RCTs in vaginal candidiasis: Not yet performed; Level D.

### Anti-parasitic (Giardia)

**Force et al. 2000 (Phytotherapy Research):**
- N=14 patients; positive stool test for intestinal parasites (Blastocystis, Entamoeba, Giardia-type); oregano oil 600 mg/day × 6 weeks
- Complete elimination of parasites in 8/14 (57%); improvement in 3 additional
- Uncontrolled; small N; Level C

### Upper Respiratory Infection

**Oregano syrup (O. vulgare) combinations:** Traditional use; Level C from European Commission E traditional authorization; no standalone RCT.

### Antioxidant / General

**Standardized data (Zheng & Wang 2001; USDA ORAC database):** Oregano has highest measured antioxidant capacity among common culinary herbs; not a clinical endpoint but suggests preventive potential. Level D.

**Overall Clinical Evidence Grade: Level C–D for most indications; Level D anti-infective (strong in vitro, limited clinical).**

Oregano is a case where in vitro pharmacological evidence is compelling and traditional use is 2,000+ years robust, but controlled clinical trials are sparse. The anti-H. pylori and anti-parasitic data are the most promising for clinical development.

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

Oregano's phenomenological signature is dominated by its immediate sensory impact — the warm, pungent, slightly bitter aroma and taste of carvacrol that is among the most culturally familiar across Mediterranean and Latin American food cultures. Its medicinal phenomenology is essentially continuous with its culinary experience.

**Oral (oil of oregano / concentrated extract):**
- Immediate: Intense burning/warming sensation in mouth and throat (carvacrol TRPV1 activation at high concentration); distinctly hotter than culinary exposure
- 15–30 minutes: GI warmth spreading through digestive tract; antispasmodic effect (reduced bloating, cramping)
- 1–2 hours (anti-parasitic dose): Warm GI sensation throughout; some users report initial GI upset if starting at high dose (carvacrol's membrane effects on gut lining at antimicrobial doses)
- **Oil of oregano "burn" protocol:** Traditional high-dose use requires emulsification in honey or olive oil to reduce mucosal irritation

**Aromatherapy:**
- Warm, spicy, penetrating aroma; immediate olfactory association with Italian/Mediterranean cuisine
- Mentally: Activating, appetite-stimulating (culinary association); less cognitively clarifying than rosemary
- Antimicrobial via inhalation: Carvacrol vapor reduces airborne pathogen levels; used in traditional Mediterranean sick-room ventilation (windows onto oregano-covered hillsides)

**Culinary-medicinal continuum:**
Oregano is perhaps the most complete example of the culinary-medicinal continuum in Mediterranean herbalism. The quantities in traditional Mediterranean cooking (heavy oregano on grilled meats, in stews, on bread) provide meaningful carvacrol intake. The Mediterranean diet's disease-protective properties include this ongoing low-dose aromatic antimicrobial exposure as one plausible contributing factor.

**Za'tar cultural context:**
Za'tar (the spice blend of Origanum, Thymus, Sumac, and sesame) is consumed daily as bread dip across Lebanon, Syria, Jordan, and Palestine. Traditional belief: Za'tar "strengthens the mind" and "cleanses the blood." The antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant pharmacology of the constituent herbs provides mechanistic basis for these traditional beliefs. The daily consumption of Za'tar is effectively low-dose carvacrol + thymol + rosmarinic acid + ellagic acid (from sumac) administered as a culturally embedded preventive health practice.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Antimicrobial / anti-parasitic (oil of oregano):**
- Carvacrol-standardized oregano oil capsules (50–80% carvacrol): 100–200 mg carvacrol 3× daily × 4–6 weeks
- Alternatively: 4–6 drops oil of oregano in honey or olive oil 3× daily (liquid form)
- Critical: Use carvacrol chemotype (Mediterranean O. vulgare or O. compactum); northern European "oregano" tea has insufficient carvacrol
- **Start low** (1 drop) to assess GI tolerance; increase over 3–5 days

**H. pylori adjunct (experimental):**
- As above; combine with standard triple therapy (not replace); limited evidence
- Not recommended as monotherapy for confirmed H. pylori until clinical trial evidence available

**General anti-inflammatory / antioxidant:**
- Standardized extract (5% rosmarinic acid): 400–800 mg/day
- Tea: 1–2 g dried Mediterranean oregano per cup; 2–3 cups/day (modest carvacrol; higher rosmarinic acid)

**Topical antimicrobial:**
- Oregano oil 1% in carrier (antimicrobial wound/skin): Apply to minor wounds, fungal skin infections
- Never apply undiluted EO to skin or mucous membranes

**Za'tar protocol (preventive/culinary):**
- Daily Za'tar consumption (1–2 tablespoons spice blend on bread, in salads): provides continuous low-dose carvacrol + thymol + sumac polyphenols

### Safety Profile

**Generally safe** at culinary and standard medicinal doses. Essential oil requires caution.

**Adverse effects:**
- **GI irritation (most common with oil of oregano):** Burning, nausea, heartburn from high carvacrol; always dilute; take with food; enteric-coated capsule reduces GI exposure
- **Mucous membrane irritation:** Undiluted essential oil causes chemical burns; always dilute
- **Iron absorption interference:** Phenolic content reduces non-heme iron absorption (separate from iron supplementation by 2 hours)

**Drug interactions:**
- **Anticoagulants (warfarin):** Rosmarinic acid + ursolic acid additive anticoagulant; monitor INR
- **Antidiabetics:** Carnosic acid/carvacrol mild insulin sensitization; additive hypoglycemia possible; monitor glucose
- **CYP interactions:** In vitro CYP3A4 inhibition by carvacrol; clinical significance at therapeutic doses uncertain; transplant patients: monitor
- **Iron:** Reduces absorption; separate doses

**Contraindications:**
- Bleeding disorders (high-dose rosmarinic acid + ursolic acid anticoagulant)
- Pregnancy (high medicinal dose): Anti-parasitic/high-carvacrol preparations avoided; culinary use safe
- Lamiaceae allergy (contact dermatitis risk)

**Za'tar / culinary use:** Essentially no contraindications at culinary amounts; the therapeutic concerns apply to concentrated oil of oregano, not food seasoning.
