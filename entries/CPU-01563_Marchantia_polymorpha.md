# Marchantia polymorpha — Leichen (Dioscorides 4-53, "Lichen", also "bryon"); a moss that "sticks to moist rocks"

## *Marchantia polymorpha [Linnaeus], the last of five names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for chapter 4-53 (Lichen, Hepatica [Fuchs], Lichen petraeus latifolius, Hepatica fontana [Bauhin]); a second block, "[other usage]", adds three lichens: Lecanora esculenta "Manna Lichen", Alectoria jubata "Rock Hair Moss, Horse-hair Lichen", and Parmelia saxatilis "Lichen"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant under this name. The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The SUGGESTED block offers a long list of names for the one chapter, and the last name on the first line, *Marchantia polymorpha* [Linnaeus], is the one the consolidation took as the row key. The block then lists three different lichens as "other usage". The Codex does not state the identity as certain. *Marchantia polymorpha* is, in general botany, a liverwort and not a lichen, and the chapter itself calls the thing "a moss". The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The heading *4-53. LEICHEN* is at line 8471, the SUGGESTED block at lines 8473 to 8475 and the chapter text at line 8476. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 633. The chapter stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), after 4-52 *SCHOINOS* (the rushes, line 8454) and before 4-54 *PARONUCHIA* (line 8477).

The whole chapter reads: "Lichen grows on rocks and is also called bryon. It is a moss sticking to moist rocks. This is applied to stop discharges of blood, lessen inflammation, and heal lichen [papular skin disease], and applied with honey it helps jaundice. It also helps the fluids of the mouth and tongue [saliva]."

So the chapter says where it grows (on rocks, "sticking to moist rocks"), what kind of thing it is ("a moss"), and gives its other name, *bryon*. It gives four uses: it is "applied" to stop discharges of blood, to lessen inflammation and to heal the skin disease called lichen (the translators' bracket reads "papular skin disease"); applied with honey it "helps jaundice"; and it "helps the fluids of the mouth and tongue", where the translators' bracket reads "saliva". The chapter gives no description of the form, no colour, no season and no part used. The translators' indexes list *Marchantia polymorpha*, *Hepatica fontana*, *Lecanora esculenta*, *Parmelia saxatilis*, *Alectoria jubata*, the Greek *LEICHEN* and *Manna Lichen* all at page 595 (lines 16449 to 16450, 16055, 16285, 16919, 14562, 12244 and 16427).

**The word does several jobs.** The word *lichen* is a plant here and also names a skin disease inside the same sentence, and the same word is used for the disease in other chapters of the text. For one example, in 1-138 *ELAIA* (the olive, line 4543) the moisture from the burnt green wood "heals dandruff, parasitic skin diseases and lichen [papular skin disease]". The word is also the title of 2-45 *LEICHENES HIPPON* (line 5117), which concerns a bony excrescence on a horse's legs, and that chapter is covered by an animal entry, III_109. The three senses are different things, and only the plant of 4-53 belongs to this entry. [USER: verify]

**The other name.** The name *bryon* is also used in the text for other plants: 2-169 *KAUKALIS* (line 5995; the name appears in the list at line 5998, "Democritus calls it bryon") and the sea-moss chapter 4-99 (see Overlap). The Codex does not carry any of those chapters over to this one.

**Overlap.** The chapter 4-53 is not the subject of any existing entry (chapter-level search for 4-53, *Leichen*, *Marchantia* and *Hepatica fontana*). It is mentioned once, as "another passage near the name", in I_872 (Sea moss, *Corallina*, chapters 4-99 and 4-100), whose own subject is a different chapter, and the sea moss of I_872 is a seaweed. The chapter-number search also matched the string 4-53 inside the line ranges of III_191, III_224 and III_226 (for example "5344-5347" and "534-536"), which are not references to this chapter. III_109 (Horse chestnut, "spavin, ergot of the horse leg", 2-45) shares only the Greek word *leichen*. None is merged. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. As a general fact, *Marchantia polymorpha* is a liverwort, a small non-flowering plant that grows as a flat green ribbon (a thallus) on wet ground and rocks, while a lichen is a partnership of a fungus and an alga or cyanobacterium. They are different kinds of organism. Dioscorides' "moss" and the translators' mixed list of liverwort and lichens do not settle which organism, or whether more than one, the chapter means. Nothing is stated here about its composition. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a moss from wet rocks, applied for bleeding, inflammation and a papular skin disease, and with honey for jaundice. These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol. Bleeding and jaundice need medical assessment, and an unidentified moss from the rocks should not be applied to broken skin.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives only "This is applied", and "applied with honey" for jaundice. The text does not say how the moss is prepared, in what amount, to which part of the body (for jaundice) or how often. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identity is uncertain between a liverwort and several lichens, which are different kinds of organism, so a modern study of one would not bear directly on the Dioscorides drug.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this material. It is a short note on a moss from wet rocks with four external or honeyed uses, so the honest answer is that there is nothing here to take up.

### Contraindications

Chapter 4-53 prints no warning. Bleeding that does not stop and jaundice need medical care and must not be left to a poultice of moss. The identification is only the translators' suggestion between a liverwort and several lichens, and a wild moss gathered from rocks can carry dirt and microbes, so no plant should be gathered or applied on the strength of this entry. The chapter gives no dose, and no safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
