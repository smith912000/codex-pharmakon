# Pinwheel Flower / Crape Jasmine / Tagar

## *Tabernaemontana divaricata* (L.) R.Br. ex Roem. & Schult. | Family: Apocynaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Cooling / bitter-astringent (Ayurvedic attribution; inferred from traditional use — see note below)
**Training Phase:** Foundation — Signature Study

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Tabernaemontana divaricata* is an evergreen ornamental shrub of the dogbane family (Apocynaceae), native to South and mainland South-East Asia and widely planted across the tropics for its pinwheel-shaped white flowers — hence the common names "Pinwheel Flower" and "Crape Jasmine." Like most Apocynaceae, every part of the plant exudes a milky white latex when cut. It reaches two to three metres, favours warm, humid, partially shaded ground, and is propagated readily from cuttings, which is one reason it has spread so far as a temple and garden plant. In cultivation it is grown for ornament first and medicine second; the medicinal parts traditionally used are the **root, leaf, and latex**.

**A naming caution before anything else.** In Sanskrit-derived nomenclature this plant carries names such as *Tagar* and *Nandivardhana*. These are dangerously close to **"Tagara,"** which in the classical Ayurvedic materia medica denotes **Valerian (*Valeriana* spp.)** — an entirely different plant, from a different family, with a sedative valepotriate/valerenic-acid chemistry that has nothing to do with the indole alkaloids of *Tabernaemontana*. A cultivator or compounder who reads "Tagar/Tagara" in an old text and reaches for the pinwheel shrub when Valerian was meant (or vice versa) will get the wrong pharmacology entirely. Kenetica flags this as a **hard substitution hazard**: confirm the botanical, never the vernacular.

**Named-tradition attestations.** The plant sits within the broad Unani (**CM** — Avicenna's *Canon of Medicine* stream, as transmitted through South-Asian Unani practice) and Ayurvedic (**SS** — the Sushruta/Ayurveda stream) usage of the Indian subcontinent, where *T. divaricata* root and latex appear in folk and classical practice for skin complaints, eye disorders, and pain. Modern ethnopharmacological papers repeatedly describe it as "a Thai medicinal herb… used in traditional rejuvenation remedies as for preventing forgetfulness and improving the memory" and "as an analgesic, sedative, or a cough syrup" (Nakdook et al., 2010). The specific attribution of individual formulae to Avicenna's *Canon* versus the Sushruta Samhita is not something I can pin to chapter and verse from the retrieved literature — **[USER: verify]** the exact classical citations before printing them as doctrine. What is solidly attested in the modern record is the South/South-East Asian folk and traditional-rejuvenation use for memory, pain, and skin/eye complaints.

### Active Components

*T. divaricata* is, chemically, a **monoterpene indole alkaloid factory**. Phytochemical surveys of the leaves, roots, and aerial parts have isolated dozens of these alkaloids. The pharmacologically interesting classes include:

- **Bisindole (vobasinyl–iboga type) alkaloids** — e.g. 19,20-dihydrotabernamine, 19,20-dihydroervahanine A, 3′-R/S-hydroxyvoacamine, voacamine-type dimers. These are the potent cholinesterase inhibitors (see below).
- **Conophylline** and its congeners **conofoline, conophyllinine** — bisindole alkaloids studied for insulin/β-cell and autophagy effects.
- **Monomeric indole alkaloids** of the aspidosperma/iboga/voacanga families (voafinine, taberhanine, voacangine-, coronaridine-, tabersonine-type skeletons are characteristic of the genus).

Kam, Pang & Lim's phytochemical study of the double-flowered leaf variety alone reported 23 alkaloids, including several new ones, and explicitly tied the bisindoles to "stimulating insulin expression" (Kam et al., 2003). Note that alkaloid content varies by plant part, variety (single vs double flower), and provenance — a cultivator cannot assume a fixed profile.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In the Kenetica framework, *T. divaricata* is filed as a **Signature Study** plant, not a therapeutic staple, because its most interesting activities are **preclinical only** — demonstrated in enzyme assays, cell lines, and rodent models, never validated in human trials. Its "signature" is cholinergic: it slows the breakdown of acetylcholine, which is the same mechanistic lever pulled by the licensed anti-Alzheimer drugs (galantamine, donepezil). Two research threads define its interest:

1. **Cholinesterase inhibition / cognition (preclinical).** Extracts and isolated bisindole alkaloids inhibit acetylcholinesterase (AChE) in vitro and in rodents, and improve memory measures in amyloid-β rodent models.
2. **Conophylline — β-cell/insulin, autophagy, cytotoxicity (preclinical).** The bisindole conophylline induces insulin-producing-cell differentiation, lowers blood glucose in diabetic rats given oral extract, and is cytotoxic to cancer cell lines; its molecular target in autophagy induction has been mapped to GPX4.

Use case, honestly stated: this is a plant of **pharmacological interest and study**, a source organism for lead compounds. It is **not** a self-administered nootropic or antidiabetic remedy, and Kenetica does not present it as one.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Parameter | Note |
|---|---|
| Parts used (traditional) | Root, leaf, latex |
| Traditional forms | Aqueous/ethanolic extract; latex applied topically; folk cough/analgesic preparations |
| Studied preparations | Alkaloid-enriched stem/root extracts; isolated alkaloids (research only) |
| Human dose | **None established.** No validated human therapeutic dose exists. |
| Rodent research doses | Extracts studied at 250–1000 mg/kg in mice/rats (Nakdook 2010; Chattipakorn 2006) — **not** human-translatable |

**Safety caveats:** Apocynaceae alkaloids can be cardio- and neuro-active; the latex is a mechanical/chemical irritant. No purified-dose human safety window has been defined. Kenetica lists **no human dose** deliberately — inventing one would be fabrication. Preclinical-only.

### Scientific Correlation

Grading each claim by the strength of retrieved evidence:

- **AChE inhibition (in vitro + in vivo rodent): SUPPORTED (preclinical).** Multiple independent groups. Root bisindoles 19,20-dihydrotabernamine and 19,20-dihydroervahanine A inhibit AChE *more potently than galantamine* in the Ellman assay (Ingkaninan et al., 2006); 3′-R/S-hydroxyvoacamine is a non-competitive inhibitor with IC₅₀ ≈ 7.0 µM (Chaiyana et al., 2013); whole extract reversibly inhibits cortical and circulating AChE in rats (Chattipakorn et al., 2006) and modulates hippocampal synaptic transmission like galantamine (Pratchayasakul et al., 2010).
- **Memory improvement in Alzheimer's models: SUPPORTED (preclinical, rodent).** Root extract attenuated amyloid-β(25–35)-induced cognitive deficits and brain AChE elevation in mice (Nakdook et al., 2010).
- **Human anti-Alzheimer efficacy: NULL.** No human trial retrieved. Grade: unproven in humans.
- **Conophylline antidiabetic (β-cell/insulin): SUPPORTED (preclinical, rodent).** Oral conophylline-containing *T. divaricata* extract lowered fasting blood glucose and raised plasma insulin in streptozotocin-diabetic and Goto-Kakizaki rats (Fujii et al., 2009). Grade: preclinical; "functional food" language is the authors', not a human-efficacy claim.
- **Conophylline anticancer/cytotoxic: SUPPORTED (in vitro).** Conophylline showed IC₅₀ 0.17–1.49 µM across five human cancer cell lines (Bao et al., 2013). Grade: cell-line only.
- **Conophylline autophagy mechanism: SUPPORTED (mechanistic).** Thermal proteome profiling identified GPX4 as conophylline's binding target in autophagy induction (Kakegawa et al., 2021).

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Ayurvedic and Unani sources place the pinwheel shrub among **cooling, bitter-astringent** plants suited to inflammatory heat conditions of the skin and eyes, and to "rejuvenation" (rasayana-adjacent) memory use. The cooling/bitter attribution here is **inferred from the pattern of traditional indications** (skin inflammation, eye complaints, latex used topically) rather than quoted verbatim from a classical text — treat it as a working classification, not a sourced doctrine, until the classical citation is confirmed **[USER: verify]**.

### Contraindications

- **Not for self-medication.** All therapeutic activity is preclinical; no human dose or safety margin exists.
- **Apocynaceae alkaloid load.** The indole/bisindole alkaloids of this family carry cardio- and neuro-active potential; unstandardised extracts have unpredictable alkaloid content.
- **Latex is an irritant.** The milky latex can irritate skin, eyes, and mucous membranes on contact.
- **Cholinergic interaction risk (theoretical).** Because extracts inhibit acetylcholinesterase, concurrent use with cholinergic drugs, other cholinesterase inhibitors, or in anticholinergic-sensitive individuals is a theoretical hazard — untested in humans.
- **Pregnancy/lactation, paediatric, hepatic/renal impairment:** no safety data — avoid.
- **Substitution hazard:** do NOT confuse "Tagar/Tagara" with Valerian (see Origin & Tradition). Verify botanical identity before any use.
