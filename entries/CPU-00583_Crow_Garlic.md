# Crow Garlic — wild garlic of the skorodon chapter (Dioscorides 2-182: ophioscorodon, elaphoscorodon)

## *Allium vineale* (spine botanical; named only inside the translator's gloss for Dioscorides 2-182, "Allium hortense, Allium sylvestre, Allium ursinum [Fuchs, Linnaeus], Allium sativum, Allium vineale, Allium oleraceum, Porrum sativum — Garlic") | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity warning.** No text read has a chapter for crow garlic as such. The Dioscorides chapter is for garlic, and *Allium vineale* is one of seven Latin names the translator lists for it. The Assyrian and Avicenna tags reach this row only through the genus. The existing garlic entry (I_112, *Allium sativum*) and the ramsons entry (I_507, *A. ursinum*) may rest on the same chapter (not checked here). [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-182, "SKORODON, LEUKOSKORODON, OPHIOSKORODON, ELAPHOSKORODON" (text line 6115; the index lists "Allium vineale 308"). Dioscorides first describes the cultivated garlic: that "in Egypt has only one head like the leek — sweet, inclining to a purple colour", elsewhere "compacted of many white cloves that the Greeks call aglithai". Then: "There is another wild kind called ophioscorodon (that is, serpent's garlic). It has a sharp, warming, biting quality. It expels flatulence, disturbs the belly, dries the stomach, causes thirst and puffing up, breeds boils on the outside of the body, and dulls vision." Ophioscorodon "does the same things when eaten, as well as elaphoscorodon (as we should say, hart's garlic)". The chapter says of garlic that, eaten, it "draws out broadworms and draws away urine" and "is good like nothing else for those bitten by vipers", applied as a poultice to anyone bitten by a mad dog. It "clears the arteries" and lessens "old coughs". Taken with a decoction of origanum it "kills lice and nits". With honey it "takes away vitiligines, lichenes, freckles, running ulcers on the head, dandruff, and psoriasis". A decoction of the leaves is a hip bath for the menstrual flow and afterbirth. Which of these belong to the wild kinds, and which of the wild kinds is *A. vineale*, the chapter does not say.

### Active Components

Alliums in general carry sulfur compounds released on cutting or crushing, which give the smell and the bite. That fits Dioscorides' "sharp, warming, biting". The content of this species was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The sourced uses are Dioscorides' for garlic and for the wild garlics as a group. The Codex recommends none of them, and gives no protocol for this plant. It is a common lawn and field weed in some regions, and the Codex makes no claim about its edibility.

### Preparation & Dosing

Dioscorides gives preparations without a quantity: eaten raw or boiled, pounded in wine, applied as a poultice, burnt and mixed with honey, boiled with pitch pine and frankincense and held in the mouth (2-182, lines 6115 to 6127). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** *(c) Absent.* No attestation for this species was found, and none is asserted. The blocking warning on Assyrian identifications applies; no tablet number or deity is given.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent* as to cultivation status. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. What the chapter models for a cultivator is attention: he tells the Egyptian one-headed garlic from the many-clove kind, names a wild serpent's garlic and hart's garlic, and says plainly what they do to the belly, the thirst and the sight. He does not say they nourish anything.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent.* The tag rests on list rows for garlic and onion. Nothing read places crow garlic in the Unani frame of innate heat and radical moisture.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing to cultivate with this plant on the evidence read; it is a weed that happens to share a genus with a food. Dioscorides' warnings, such as boils, thirst and dull sight from the wild garlics, are a reason for restraint.

### Contraindications

The Dioscorides chapter itself lists thirst, a disturbed belly, "boils on the outside of the body" and "dulls vision" as effects of the wild kinds. General cautions for alliums apply: heartburn in sensitive people, and care with bleeding disorders and blood-thinning medication. Do not use it for snakebite or rabid-animal bites. Seek emergency care. Because the species is not clearly identified in the texts, do not forage it on the strength of this entry.
