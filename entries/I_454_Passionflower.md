# I-454 | PASSIONFLOWER
## *Passiflora incarnata* L.

**Family:** Passifloraceae | **Part Used:** Aerial parts (leaf, stem, flower); traditionally whole vine

**Energetic Tier:** ☿ Shen

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Etymology:** "Passionflower" — named by 16th-century Spanish Jesuit missionaries in South America who saw the Passion of Christ encoded in the flower's structure: the 10 petals and sepals = 10 faithful apostles (minus Judas and Peter who denied); the corona filaments = crown of thorns; the 5 stamens = 5 wounds; the 3 stigmas = 3 nails; the tendril-like extensions = the whips of flagellation; the leaf shape = the lance used to pierce Christ's side. The missionaries used the flower as a catechism tool — *flor de las cinco llagas* (flower of the five wounds).

**Common names:** Maypop (from the sound the large fruit makes when stepped on), wild apricot, wild passion vine, purple passionflower

**Species disambiguation:** Over 500 *Passiflora* species exist; *P. incarnata* (Eastern North America) is the primary medicinal species; *P. caerulea* (South American ornamental) is botanically different and should not be substituted; *P. edulis* (passion fruit) is the culinary species

**Botanical identity:** Vigorous climbing perennial vine to 8 m via axillary tendrils; leaves deeply 3-lobed, serrate, alternate; flowers 5–9 cm diameter, spectacularly complex — 5 white or light-lavender petals, 5 sepals, corona of purple-and-white filaments, central staminal column bearing 5 anthers + 3 spreading stigmas; fruit an ovoid yellow berry (maypop) 3–5 cm, edible, highly aromatic; native: Eastern and Southeastern United States, growing wild in disturbed areas, roadsides, open forests

**Primary active constituents:**
- **Chrysin (5,7-dihydroxyflavone):** 0.03–0.5% aerial parts; GABA-A benzodiazepine site PAM; Ki ~15–30 μM; *but:* oral bioavailability critically low (<1% in humans) due to first-pass sulfation/glucuronidation
- **Vitexin (apigenin-8-C-glucoside):** flavone C-glycoside; anxiolytic, antispasmodic; better absorbed than chrysin due to glycosylation
- **Isovitexin, orientin, isoorientin:** structurally related C-glycosylflavonoids; anti-inflammatory, mild GABAergic
- **Maltol (3-hydroxy-2-methylpyran-4-one):** ~trace; potentiates GABA-A; iron chelator
- **Passiflorine:** historical term for the CNS-active alkaloid fraction; later characterized as mixture rather than single compound
- **Harmane alkaloids:** harman, harmol, harmine (trace; 0.005–0.01%); MAO-A inhibition at trace concentrations; clinical significance at standard oral doses uncertain
- **Benzoflavone moiety (BZF):** structurally distinct flavonoid fraction; GABA-A modulation demonstrated in rodent studies

**The chrysin bioavailability paradox:** Chrysin is the most pharmacologically studied compound (direct GABA-A BZD site binding), yet systematic pharmacokinetic studies show <1% human oral bioavailability. The whole-extract clinical efficacy (Akhondzadeh 2001) likely reflects synergistic contributions from vitexin, isovitexin, maltol, and trace alkaloids rather than chrysin as the sole active agent — a lesson in the inadequacy of single-compound reductionism for botanical pharmacology.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Within-tradition depth compensates for cross-traditional breadth:**

Indigenous North American documentation of *P. incarnata* is extensive across multiple independent tribal traditions — Cherokee, Houma, Catawba, Natchez, and others — all converging on sedative, sleep-promoting, and antispasmodic applications. The Cherokee instruction: *"place on a stump and lie down"* captures the behavioral endpoint precisely. This convergence across culturally distinct North American nations mirrors the cross-civilization convergence seen in Eurasian herbs, simply within a different geographic scope.

**The missionary encoding:** The Jesuit "Passion Flower" symbolism (1610s–1620s, documented by Giacomo Bosio in Rome 1610) represents a unique form of ethnobotanical transmission — Catholic missionaries learned about the plant's properties from indigenous informants, encoded the knowledge in Christian symbolism, and transmitted it to Europe without accurate attribution. The spiritual/calming application survived the cultural translation.

**Post-colonial European adoption (17th–19th centuries):** By the 19th century, *P. incarnata* tincture was in the US Pharmacopeia (1898–1936) and British Pharmaceutical Codex, prescribed for "nervous restlessness" and insomnia. The Eclectic physicians (Lloyd, Felter, King) wrote extensively about its utility for "irritable states of the nervous system with muscular spasm." Commission E (Germany) later approved it for "nervous restlessness."

**The naming power:** The dramatic visual spectacle of the flower — generating immediate association with suffering, endurance, and ultimate redemption in Catholic tradition — gave the plant cultural traction in European pharmacopeias entirely disproportionate to its position in Old World medicine. The name itself became a mechanism of transmission.

| Compound | Concentration | Activity |

| Chrysin | 0.03–0.5% aerial parts | GABA-A BZD site PAM; but poor bioavailability |
| Vitexin | 0.05–0.15% | Anxiolytic; antispasmodic; adenosine A1? |
| Isovitexin | 0.02–0.08% | Anti-inflammatory; GABAergic |
| Orientin | trace–0.05% | Anti-inflammatory; mild sedative |
| Isoorientin | trace | Anti-inflammatory |
| Luteolin-7-glucoside | trace | Anti-inflammatory |

**Alkaloid profile (trace):**

| Compound | Concentration | Activity |
|----------|---------------|---------|
| Harman | 0.005–0.01% | MAO-A inhibition (~weak at these concentrations) |
| Harmol | trace | MAO-A inhibition |
| Harmine | trace | MAO-A + 5-HT2A (at higher concentrations than present) |

*The harmane alkaloids at trace concentrations found in standard *P. incarnata* preparations fall far below concentrations required for meaningful MAO inhibition. Concerns about MAO-tyramine interactions at standard passionflower doses are not substantiated.*

**Maltol:** Iron chelator + GABA-A potentiator; contributes to aqueous extract activity; rapid absorption

**Phenolic acids:** Caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid — anti-inflammatory contribution

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

**GABAergic Primary Mechanism:**
```
Whole extract → multiple parallel GABAergic contributions
├── Chrysin → GABA-A BZD site direct binding (PAM)
│   BUT bioavailability <1% → limited systemic contribution
├── Vitexin → GABA-A modulation (mechanism less characterized than chrysin)
│   + possible adenosine A1 receptor modulation
├── Maltol → GABA-A potentiation (synergistic with GABA)
├── BZF fraction → GABA-A modulation (animal studies)
└── Harmane alkaloids → trace MAO-A inhibition
    (monoamine availability increase at very low level)

Net effect: anxiolytic + antispasmodic without deep sedation
Mechanistic parallel to partial BZD effect
```

**Antispasmodic/Spasmolytic:**
```
Vitexin + isovitexin → smooth muscle relaxation
→ Reduced bronchospasm, intestinal cramping
→ Mechanism: Ca²⁺ channel modulation (partially)
→ Traditional: "nervous stomach" application
```

**Neurological Observation — EEG Studies:**
- Animal EEG: increased theta wave activity (drowsy/relaxed state) without suppression of delta sleep architecture
- Unlike benzodiazepines: does not suppress REM sleep in animal models
- Sleep quality qualitative improvement without REM suppression consistent with clinical reports of non-groggy morning awakening

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Anxiety (GAD-equivalent):**

- **Akhondzadeh 2001** (J Clin Pharm Ther; Level A): 36 patients with GAD (DSM-IV); *Passiflora* extract 45 drops/day vs oxazepam 30 mg/day; 4 weeks; double-blind; HAM-A: no significant between-group difference (equivalent efficacy); *Passiflora* group: significantly lower job performance impairment score (p=0.049); comparable side effect profile; *This is the landmark study — equivalence to benzodiazepine with better functional outcomes and similar side-effect frequency*

**Preoperative Anxiety:**

- **Movafegh 2008** (Anesthesia & Analgesia; Level A): 60 adults scheduled for elective surgery; oral *P. incarnata* 700 mg vs placebo 90 min pre-induction; VAS anxiety score significantly lower in passionflower group; no significant differences in sedation level, psychomotor function, or anterograde amnesia — anxiolytic without cognitive impairment
- **Dantas 2017** (Phytother Res; Level B): 40 patients preoperative dental anxiety; 260 mg capsule vs placebo; VAS anxiety significant; small sample but supportive

**Sleep:**

- Commission E approved: "nervous restlessness" including sleep difficulties; based on traditional evidence + available pharmacological data (Level C + D)
- Human RCT data for sleep specifically: limited; most sleep applications extrapolated from anxiolytic RCTs + traditional evidence

**Evidence Summary:**

| Indication | Best Evidence | Grade |
|-----------|---------------|-------|
| Anxiety (GAD) | Akhondzadeh 2001 | Level A |
| Preoperative anxiety | Movafegh 2008 | Level A |
| Nervous restlessness | Commission E approval | Level C |
| Insomnia | Traditional + limited pilot data | Level C–D |
| Antispasmodic | Traditional; mechanism-supported | Level C–D |

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

**The flower as medicine delivery mechanism:** The Jesuit missionaries' choice to name this plant after the Passion of Christ was not arbitrary pharmacology — it was an act of mnemonic transmission. By encoding the plant's properties into a deeply emotionally resonant religious narrative, they guaranteed that European populations would remember, seek, and grow *Passiflora incarnata*. The flower's extraordinary visual complexity — genuinely unlike any European flower — made it memorable; the religious interpretation made it culturally significant. The anxiolytic properties were transmitted within the cultural vehicle.

**"Place on a stump and lie down":** The Cherokee instruction for *P. incarnata* use is a masterpiece of traditional pharmacology communication — it encodes dose, timing, onset, and expected effect in a single behavioral image. No dosing charts, no HAM-A scores, no double-blind methodology: the instruction tells the practitioner everything needed to use the herb correctly. That this instruction converges precisely with Akhondzadeh's 2001 clinical findings (anxiolytic at oral doses, without significant sedation or impairment) validates both the traditional knowledge system and the modern methodology.

**US Pharmacopeia arc (1898–1936):** Passionflower extract was listed in the US Pharmacopeia from 1898 until its delisting in 1936 — the decade of the great pharmaceutical industry reorganization. Its removal corresponded with the synthesis of barbiturates and early sedative-hypnotics, not with any finding of inefficacy. The Commission E approval in 1985 represents a partial rehabilitation of this same traditional application.

**The bioavailability puzzle:** Chrysin — the compound most studied for GABA-A binding — has <1% oral bioavailability in humans. Yet the whole-extract demonstrably works in clinical trials. This is the botanical pharmacology paradox made visible: the compound that fits the mechanism is not the compound doing the work at standard doses. The actual active fraction in oral human administration remains incompletely characterized — vitexin, maltol, and the BZF fraction are the leading candidates. Passionflower's efficacy despite its principal "active compound" being essentially non-bioavailable stands as a cautionary lesson against single-compound botanical pharmacology.

## Section 8: Practical Application

**Primary indications:**
1. Generalized anxiety disorder (mild–moderate); alternatively to low-dose benzodiazepines
2. Situational/anticipatory anxiety (preoperative, dental, performance)
3. Insomnia with anxiety component ("mind won't stop")
4. Nervous restlessness, palpitations of emotional origin
5. Adjunct in benzodiazepine tapering (reduce dose while bridging with passionflower)

**Dosing:**
- **Anxiety (Akhondzadeh protocol):** Standardized extract 45 drops (approximately 4–6 mL of 1:5 tincture) TID; equivalent to approximately 2.4–3.6 g dried herb per day
- **Sleep:** 500–1,000 mg dried herb equivalent 30–60 min before bed; or 4–8 mL 1:5 tincture before bed
- **Tea:** 4–8 g dried aerial parts in 200 mL hot water × 10 minutes; 1–3 cups/day
- **Standardized extract (2.6% vitexin):** 200–400 mg/day
- **Commission E recommended dose:** 4–8 g herb/day

**Forms:**
- Tincture (1:5, 45% ethanol): preserves alkaloid fraction; most traditional form
- Dried herb (tea): good flavonoid extraction; traditional preparation for sleep
- Standardized extract (vitexin standardization): consistent dosing; most used in RCTs
- Combination products (+ valerian, hops, lemon balm): commonly used; synergistic rationale

**Contraindications:**
- **Pregnancy:** Uterotonic activity of trace alkaloids; insufficient safety data; avoid
- **MAO inhibitors:** Theoretical interaction with harmane alkaloids (very low dose concern; practically minimal at standard doses but caution with pharmaceutical MAOIs)
- **CNS depressants:** Additive effect; adjust if combining with pharmaceutical sedatives/anxiolytics

**Drug interactions:**
- **Benzodiazepines, Z-drugs:** Additive GABAergic effect; may allow dose reduction (monitor)
- **CNS depressants (opioids, alcohol, antihistamines):** Additive sedation; caution
- **Warfarin:** Theoretical (flavonoid interactions with coagulation); monitor INR
- **Pharmaceutical MAOIs:** Theoretical tyramine/monoamine interaction; avoid combination despite low pharmacological risk at standard doses

**Safety:**
- Generally well-tolerated; Commission E lists no known side effects at therapeutic doses
- Rare: nausea, vomiting at high doses
- Driving: mild impairment possible; caution first use
- Paradoxical stimulation: rare, particularly in children

**Clinical positioning:** For mild–moderate anxiety, passionflower is an evidence-supported first-line herbal option with a significantly better tolerability profile than benzodiazepines (no cognitive impairment, no dependence, no respiratory depression). For moderate–severe anxiety requiring pharmaceutical management, passionflower can serve as adjunct during tapering phases.
