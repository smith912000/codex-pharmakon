# Crude niter — Po Xiao (朴消) "Slaked Lime" (Yang 1998)
## *The entry the project's spine labels "crude niter" (crude sodium sulphate); Yang 1998 prints "Slaked Lime" for Po Xiao, the project list gives the Chinese 朴消, and Yang's footnote suspects that this entry and the next, Xiao Shi, have been "mistakenly juxtaposed", so the identity is unsettled* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** Po Xiao is printed in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`) on PDF page 24, under "Jades and Stones: Superior Class" (headings at lines 907 and 985; names row `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv` row 9, line 962, translator identification "Slaked Lime"; list `_sources/shennong.csv` 朴消, line 9). The text (lines 962-967): "Po Xiao (Slaked Lime) is bitter and cold. It is nontoxic and mainly treats hundreds of diseases. It eliminates cold and heat and evil qi and expels accumulations and gatherings in the six bowels as well as firmly bound retention and aggregation [of water and food]. It is able to transform 72 kinds of stone. If it is taken after being sublimated, it may make one an immortal with a light body. It is produced in mountains and valleys." The identity is in conflict. The spine's label is "Crude niter" with the formula "Na2SO4 crude" and the note "high; id-uncertain", whereas the translator's printed gloss is "Slaked Lime", which is calcium hydroxide and not a sulphate. Yang's footnote 41 (lines 977-981), attached to the next entry, Xiao Shi, says: "Mirabilitum is a very good cathartic which is often prescribed to precipitate or purge food and drink accumulation from the stomach and intestines. However, in this text, the translator suspects that Mirabilitum and Slaked Lime have been mistakenly juxtaposed in terms of their indications." The note does not say which way the mismatch runs, and the sources read give no way to decide it. The word "niter" does not appear in the translation of this entry. Two older Codex entries touch the same Chinese names: `Volume_II_Mineral_Alchemical/entries/II_100_Saltpeter_Xiao_Shi.md` (CPU-00848), which quotes Po Xiao and Xiao Shi together under saltpetre, and `Volume_II_Mineral_Alchemical/entries/II_113_Calcium_hydrate_lime.md` (tagged DMM only), which covers quicklime in Dioscorides. Neither is edited here. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

If "Slaked Lime" is right, the substance is calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2, a white alkaline powder made by adding water to quicklime (calcium oxide). If the spine's reading is right, it is crude sodium sulphate (a Glauber's-salt type), a neutral salt. These are chemically different, and the sources read do not say which Po Xiao was. The sources read give no composition.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Classic records Po Xiao as a drug for "hundreds of diseases", for cold and heat, for accumulations in the six bowels and for retained food and water, with the Daoist clause that, sublimated, it "may make one an immortal with a light body". Swallowing slaked lime would burn the mouth and gullet, so the Codex cannot take the entry as a usable indication. The Codex offers no protocol, dose or preparation.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation printed is sublimation: "If it is taken after being sublimated" (line 966). Sublimation is the Classic's word as translated, and the method is not described. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Calcium hydroxide is a caustic alkali: it is used in building and in food processing (lime-treated maize) and burns skin and mucosa on contact. Sodium sulphate is an osmotic laxative. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: an upper-grade drug (上品), with an unsettled identification.* Po Xiao stands under "Jades and Stones: Superior Class" (Yang lines 907, 985). Yang's preface says the superior class medicinals "mainly nourish life and correspond to heaven. They are nontoxic and taking them in large amounts and for a long time will not harm people. If one intends to make one's body light, boost the qi, prevent aging, and prolong life, one should base [one's efforts] on the superior class" (lines 352-358). The monograph prints "nontoxic" and, for the sublimated form, "it may make one an immortal with a light body" (lines 966-967). The text prints no *jing*, *qi* or *shen* claim for it, and none is made here. If the substance were slaked lime, "nontoxic" and "taken ... may make one an immortal" would not describe it; that is a reason the translator's identification is doubted by him in footnote 41 and not a claim made in this entry. The grade attaches to the Chinese name Po Xiao in the Classic; it does not attach to calcium hydroxide, and it does not widen to sodium sulphate. Yang's introduction (lines 312-328) places the book in the Daoist elixir tradition and warns of illness from "such external elixirs compounded out of potentially toxic materials".

**Practitioner's note.** An upper-grade drug in the Chinese scheme, whose identity the translator does not settle. Nothing here supports taking it: if it was lime it is caustic, and if it was a sulphate it is a purge. The grade is the Classic's, and the entry is a record of what the elixir tradition ranked among the "nourish life" stones. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Slaked lime is caustic to the mouth, gullet and gut when swallowed, and a sulphate purge causes dehydration and electrolyte loss when overused. Powdered mineral products are unstandardised, and what is sold under "Po Xiao" cannot be assumed to be either. The Classic's "immortal with a light body" and "transforms 72 kinds of stone" are not claims of fact. Purchase of mineral medicines is regulated in many places.
