**Etymology:** Latin *nux myristica* = "aromatic/fragrant nut" (*myristos* = fragrant, anointed, from Greek *myrrha* related to *smyrnē* = myrrh). The "musc/moscade" (muscat/muscade) names reflect the musky aromatic quality. English "nutmeg" = *nux myristica* contracted. The genus name *Myristica* derives from the same Greek aromatic root as myrrh.

**Nutmeg and mace — same plant, different parts:**
- **Nutmeg**: The seed kernel after the red/orange aril (mace) is removed; higher myristicin content
- **Mace**: The dried aril surrounding the seed; slightly different EO ratio; used separately in cooking and medicine
- Both share the same general pharmacological profile

⚠️ **Psychoactive-toxic warning:** Nutmeg consumed in large amounts (>5g, approximately 1–2 whole nutmegs) produces a delayed (3–6h onset) hallucinogenic-toxic syndrome: visual distortions, dry mouth, tachycardia, extreme anxiety, delirium. The syndrome is unpleasant rather than pleasurable; nutmeg "trips" are associated with severe dysphoria, vomiting, and hospitalization. NOT a recommended recreational substance. This dose-dependent psychoactive-toxic profile means nutmeg is the only common culinary spice with a documented hallucinogenic syndrome.

## 2 · Cross-Traditional Presence

**Islamic medical tradition / CM:** Nutmeg arrived in Europe via Islamic medicine. Arab traders brought nutmeg from the Maluku Islands (via Indian Ocean trade routes) to the Islamic world centuries before Europeans reached the Maluku Islands directly. Ibn Sīnā mentions *jauz al-tīb* (nutmeg) for digestive conditions and as a liver tonic. The CM tradition documented *ròu dòu kòu* (肉豆蔻, flesh-aromatic nut) for warming and astringent digestive applications — particularly for chronic diarrhea, which reflects the anti-diarrheal tannin and EO combined mechanism.

**Sushruta Samhita (SS):** *Jātiphala* is documented in Ayurveda for digestive (*dīpana*, stimulates agni), sedative/sleep-promoting, and analgesic applications. The sedative application is pharmacologically significant: myristicin at sub-toxic doses produces mild sedation — the same compound that causes delirium at high doses causes drowsiness at low doses (dose-response distinction).

**Chinese Bencao Gangmu:** Li Shizhen's extensive coverage of nutmeg emphasizes: "warms the spleen and stomach; strengthens fire [Yang]; stops diarrhea; moves Qi downward; regulates Qi to stop abdominal pain." The anti-diarrheal + carminative combination is mechanistically consistent (tannins = astringent/anti-diarrheal; EO = antispasmodic).

## 3 · Convergence Analysis

**Convergence Score: 6/9**

The carminative-digestive convergence (CM/SBJ, SS, DMM-era Islamic) is the primary signal. The sedative-analgesic convergence (SS *jātiphala* for sleep) represents a tradition that correctly intuited the sub-toxic CNS effects of myristicin. The absence from BM/SA reflects Maluku Island geographic restriction to Old World trade routes (Portuguese reached Maluku only in 1512 CE — too late for Badianus 1552 CE documentation of independent SA tradition).

**The psychoactive convergence:** Islamic medical tradition documented nutmeg for *faraḥ* (joy, euphoria) at small doses — reflecting awareness of the psychoactive properties at sub-toxic levels. This cross-traditional awareness of dose-dependent psychoactive effects (therapeutic at small dose; toxic/hallucinogenic at large dose) mirrors the SS documentation of nutmeg as both analgesic-sedative (therapeutic) and as *jātiphala* that "disturbs the mind" in excess.

## 4 · Phytochemical Composition

**Essential oil (from kernel, ~10–15% of kernel weight):**

| Compound | Content | Action |
|----------|---------|--------|
| **Myristicin** | 3–12% of EO | Primary psychoactive precursor; CYP450 → MMDA (phenethylamine); inhibits MAO; antimicrobial |
| **Elemicin** | 3–7% of EO | Psychoactive precursor → TMA (phenethylamine); antimicrobial |
| Safrole | 0.5–3% | ⚠️ Procarcinogenic (IARC Group 2A); also psychoactive at high doses; CYP450 → safrole-1'-oxide (genotoxic) |
| **Eugenol** | 3–8% | COX-2; local anesthetic; antimicrobial |
| Isoeugenol | 1–5% | Antimicrobial; anti-inflammatory |
| Methyl eugenol | 2–5% | ⚠️ IARC Group 2B; antimicrobial |
| α-Pinene | 15–25% | Anti-inflammatory |
| β-Pinene | 10–20% | Anti-inflammatory |
| Sabinene | 20–35% | Primary monoterpenoid; antifungal |

**Non-volatile:**
- Myristic acid (14-carbon saturated fatty acid): 75% of fixed oil; named for this genus (it was first isolated from nutmeg). Nutmeg is ~36% fixed oil by weight.
- Tannins: astringent; anti-diarrheal mechanism
- Neolignans: anti-inflammatory; antioxidant

**Myristicin → MMDA conversion:**
The biotransformation of myristicin (the natural compound) to MMDA (3-methoxy-4,5-methylenedioxyamphetamine, a phenethylamine) occurs via CYP450 enzyme-mediated amine addition — effectively an *in vivo* synthesis of a methylenedioxy-amphetamine analog. MMDA has psychedelic and entactogenic properties (serotonin and dopamine release). This bioactivation pathway explains why nutmeg's psychoactive effect is delayed (3–6h after ingestion) — it takes time for hepatic CYP450 to convert myristicin to MMDA in sufficient quantities.

Similarly, elemicin → TMA (3,4,5-trimethoxy-amphetamine, the reference compound for which the peyote alkaloid mescaline was named — mescaline = 3,4,5-trimethoxyphenethylamine, a homolog).

## 5 · Mechanisms of Action

### Carminative / Antispasmodic

- Myristicin and sabinene: antispasmodic on GI smooth muscle (Ca2+ channel-mediated)
- Eugenol: COX-2 inhibition → reduced GI inflammation → reduced spasm
- Tannins: astringent effect on GI mucosa → anti-diarrheal

### Analgesic

- Eugenol: COX-2 + Na+ channel local anesthetic (see I_164 Clove)
- Myristicin at non-psychoactive doses: mild CNS depression → reduced pain perception
- Anti-inflammatory (α-pinene, β-pinene): indirect analgesic

### Psychoactive (Dose-Dependent) — Mechanism

**Low dose (0.5–2g nutmeg):**
- Mild digestive stimulant; sedative; mild warmth
- Sub-threshold myristicin-to-MMDA conversion → no detectable psychoactive effect

**Moderate dose (3–5g nutmeg):**
- Detectable myristicin → MMDA conversion
- Mild serotonergic + dopaminergic activation → mild euphoria, warmth
- MAO inhibition from myristicin: prolonged monoamine effects

**High dose (>5–10g nutmeg = 1–2 whole nutmegs):**
- Significant MMDA + TMA production
- Hallucinogenic delirium; visual disturbances; extreme anxiety
- Anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, tachycardia, urinary retention) from safrole
- Vomiting, extreme dysphoria
- Duration: 6–24 hours (some cases 48+ hours for complete resolution)
- Serious poisoning: hypertension, seizures, coma in overdose

### Antimicrobial

- Eugenol, isoeugenol: membrane disruption (same as clove, I_164)
- Myristicin: anti-Gram-positive bacteria
- Sabinene: antifungal

## 6 · Clinical Evidence

### Carminative / Anti-diarrheal (Traditional Use — Limited RCT Data)

Nutmeg at culinary doses (0.25–1g): EMA THMP traditional use listing for symptomatic treatment of mild digestive disorders. No large-scale RCTs for nutmeg monotherapy specifically, but the tannin + EO mechanism is pharmacologically sound and traditional use documentation is extensive.

### Analgesic (Topical)

Nutmeg EO in topical preparations: analgesic studies limited; TRPV1 agonism (mild) + COX-2 inhibition provides mechanistic rationale for traditional topical use for joint/muscle pain.

### Psychoactive / Toxicology (Case Series)

Multiple case reports and poison control center analyses of nutmeg intoxication. Nutmeg is among the most frequently reported "natural psychedelic" inquiries to poison control centers in North America and Europe, particularly among adolescents who attempt to use it as a legal/cheap hallucinogen. The consistent toxicological picture: delayed onset, unpleasant dysphoric effect, rarely requiring hospital treatment but always subjectively described as aversive.

## 7 · Phenomenological Record

**Malcolm X and the prison nutmeg account:** In *The Autobiography of Malcolm X* (1965), Malcolm X describes using nutmeg as a drug substitute while imprisoned at Charlestown State Prison (1946): "I found out that mace comes from the same fruit as nutmeg. I could buy a pack of nutmeg in the commissary for a few cents... they could get a high from nutmeg and mace... it was a high that lasted for days." This is one of the most famous first-person accounts of nutmeg intoxication in English literature, documenting the prolonged duration and euphoric-dysphoric effect from prison inmates who had discovered the psychoactive property through desperation. Malcolm X's account contributed to nutmeg's reputation as a "prison drug" and helped establish awareness of its psychoactive properties in popular American culture.

**The Banda Islands Massacre (1621) and nutmeg's colonial blood price:** As noted in the clove entry (I_164), Jan Pieterszoon Coen's VOC campaign that massacred the Bandanese people was equally motivated by nutmeg (endemic only to the Banda Islands) as by cloves (endemic only to Ternate and Tidore). Approximately 15,000 Bandanese were killed or enslaved for control of the nutmeg trade. The pharmacological compound myristicin — present in the seed of every nutmeg tree the Dutch seized — was the indirect motivation for one of the earliest genocides of the colonial era. The spice that caused hallucinations at high doses caused enormous real-world suffering through the economic violence of its monopoly control.

**Manhattan for nutmeg:** In the 1667 Treaty of Breda (ending the Second Anglo-Dutch War), England formally ceded the island of Run (the only Banda island under British control, rich with nutmeg trees) to the Dutch in exchange for New Amsterdam (modern Manhattan). This exchange — which gave the British colonial foothold that became New York City — was made possible because the British negotiators valued nutmeg control as highly as a prime American harbor settlement. The nutmeg-Manhattan trade reflects the extraordinary economic value of a single spice's pharmacological compound (myristicin) in the 17th-century world economy.

**Myristic acid and the naming of a compound class:** Myristic acid (14-carbon saturated fatty acid), the dominant fatty acid in nutmeg fixed oil and named for the *Myristica* genus, is now known as a ubiquitous biological molecule. N-myristoylation — the covalent attachment of myristic acid to protein N-termini — is a fundamental post-translational protein modification found in all eukaryotes, including humans. Every N-myristoylated protein in the human body (including Src kinases, G-protein α-subunits, and many signaling proteins) was named using a compound first identified from nutmeg. The biochemical designation of a ubiquitous cellular modification carries the name of a Maluku Island spice tree.

## 8 · Practical Application

### Clinical Use

**Culinary/digestive (primary safe application):**
- Culinary amounts (0.25–0.5g = a pinch to ¼ teaspoon): safe carminative; digestive warming
- Traditional application: nutmeg in warm milk for insomnia (mild sedative effect at culinary dose)
- Bechamel sauce, baked goods, eggnog: traditional culinary contexts providing therapeutic digestive benefit
- For functional GI disorders: 0.25–0.5g nutmeg powder in cooking or tea; maximum 1g/day

**Topical analgesic:**
- Nutmeg EO 1–3% in carrier oil: apply to painful joints/muscles (traditional warming rubefacient)
- Contains eugenol and pinenes for local anti-inflammatory effect

### Contraindications ⚠️

- **DO NOT TAKE IN LARGE DOSES:** >5g nutmeg can cause delirium, extreme anxiety, anticholinergic syndrome, cardiovascular disturbance. This is the most important safety message for nutmeg.
- **Pregnancy:** Myristicin is uterotonic and potentially teratogenic; culinary amounts may be safe but therapeutic doses should be avoided
- **MAO inhibitor medications:** Myristicin inhibits MAO; MAOI combination → serotonin syndrome risk
- **CNS medications:** Additive psychoactive effects; caution with antidepressants, anxiolytics, antipsychotics

### Drug Interactions

- **MAO inhibitors:** Additive; serotonin syndrome risk
- **CNS depressants:** Additive sedation at therapeutic (low) doses
- **Anticoagulants:** Eugenol component → monitor INR with warfarin
- **SSRI/SNRI:** Myristicin (mild MAO inhibitor + serotonergic) + SSRIs → potential serotonin syndrome at large doses

### Safety Profile

**At culinary doses (≤0.5g):** Excellent. GRAS. Standard culinary spice; no documented adverse effects.
**At medicinal doses (1–4g):** Caution. Mild psychoactive effects possible; sedation; GI disturbance (nausea/vomiting at 4g+).
**At recreational/toxic doses (>5g):** Dangerous. Documented hallucinogenic-toxic syndrome; case reports of deaths (rare; mostly pediatric cases with very large accidental ingestions).
**Safrole content:** IARC Group 2A (probable carcinogen); at culinary doses the exposure is minimal and de minimis risk; not a concern for normal use.

### Preparation Forms

| Form | Dose | Indication | Safety |
|------|------|------------|--------|
| Freshly grated culinary | 0.25–0.5g | Digestive; flavor; mild sleep | Safe |
| Warm milk with nutmeg | 0.25–0.5g | Insomnia (mild sedative) | Safe |
| EO in carrier (topical) | 1–3% | Joint/muscle pain | Safe topically |
| High-dose internal | >5g | ⚠️ AVOID | Toxic |
