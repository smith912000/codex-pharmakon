# Meconopsis horridula — tsher.sngon, Tsher ngon (rGyud-bzhi); "Blue Poppy"

## *Meconopsis horridula Hook f. et Thoms, the one binomial Clark 1995 prints for tsher.sngon, glossed "Blue Poppy"; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary also prints "Meconopsis horridula" and "Blue poppy"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts in this row.** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and that a great deal of Tibetan materia medica "has remained either totally unidentified or only tentatively identified". For *tsher.sngon* Clark prints a single binomial, but the Codex still does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1027).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads: "tsher.sngon: Blue Poppy: Meconopsis horridula Hook f. et Thoms: [has four names and grows on high, rocky hills and mountains. It has a small sword-like green leaf, brown petiole, attractive blue flower with yellow stamens and is rendered very coarse by a covering of fine thorns. Blue poppy has a bitter taste and] heals skull fractures, sustains bone resin [and cures bone fever]." The square brackets are as printed.

Clark's preface (line 82) says "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". So the part outside the brackets, that the plant "heals skull fractures, sustains bone resin", is the root-text layer as Clark gives it. Everything else is in the commentary layer: the four names, the habitat, the description of leaf, petiole, flower and thorns, the bitter taste, and the claim that it "cures bone fever". Within one line the taste therefore belongs to the commentary and the two main actions to the root text. The text does not explain "bone resin"; the Codex leaves the term as printed.

The next Clark line (1028) is a different drug: "smug.chhung.mdan.yon: a type of Blue Poppy: Meconopsis integrifolia: [is called by some smug.chhung.ber.mgo and has a green sword-shaped leaf. It has a brown flower with yellow stamens and resembles blue poppy in being covered with fine thorns, whilst its taste is slightly bitter]. It has the same uses [as blue poppy]." That drug is not this entry, but it ties its uses to this one. A third line (892) prints "ut.pa.la: Blue Lotus: Nelumbo nucifera: Meconopsis: [... It is of four types depending on the color of the flower, namely, white, red, yellow and blue ...] It cures all fevers of the lungs and liver." That is a different Tibetan name with a bare genus, and it is not read as this drug. Clark's name index (line 1734) lists *tsher.sngon* with no further information.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (Explanatory Tantra).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The materia medica summary at line 3433 reads: "Tsher ngon heals fractured bone and protects spongy bone. Muk chung dhenyon has the same efficacy as Tsher ngon." This agrees with Clark in kind (a bone-healing action, and the same efficacy shared with the second blue poppy) but the wording differs: "fractured bone" for "skull fractures", and "spongy bone" for "bone resin". Which English term renders which Tibetan term is not settled by the two translations alone. The index (line 7537) gives "Tsherngon 207".

The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate blocks. "Tsher ngon" is at line 5916 and "tsher sngon" (Tibetan script column) at line 5918. The scientific names at lines 5988 to 5995 run "Santalum album, Artemesia dracunculus, Ephedra saxatilis, Meconopsis horridula, Capsicum annum, Rubia manijith, Heracleum candicans", and the English names at line 5997 begin "Blue poppy Red pepper Indian Madder". The order of the names in the blocks is consistent with *Tsher ngon* standing against *Meconopsis horridula* and "Blue poppy", but the blocks hold different numbers of entries, and the glossary does not print the pairing in one row. The Codex therefore reads the pairing from Clark's single line (1027) and treats the glossary as consistent with it, not as independent proof. [USER: verify] The same glossary lists, at line 5492, "Mukchhung dhenyon smug chung mdan yon Meconopsis sp.", the second blue poppy, with no species.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. As a general fact, *Meconopsis* belongs to the poppy family (Papaveraceae), and the genus grows in high mountain regions. The family is known for alkaloids, but nothing in the Tibetan lines connects this drug with opium or with any constituent, and no property of the Tibetan drug is stated here from chemistry. Which part of the plant is used is not stated in the lines read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a thorny blue-flowered mountain plant that "heals skull fractures, sustains bone resin" and, in the commentary layer, cures bone fever; the Men-Tsee-Khang text says it "heals fractured bone and protects spongy bone". These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for fractures or for bone disease, and the botanical identity is not certain.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation and no quantity for *tsher.sngon*, and they do not say which part is used. Clark (line 1027) gives the habitat, appearance and actions, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text (line 3433) gives the action. Clark's preface (line 74) gives rules for collecting herbs in general: from fertile areas, not shaded by very large trees, not near built-up areas or a cemetery; cool-powered plants from north-facing slopes and warm-powered ones from south-facing valleys. The entry prints a taste (bitter, in the commentary layer) but no power, so which of the two rules applies to this plant is not stated. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes the plant by what it does: it heals skull fractures and sustains bone resin, with a commentary claim that it cures bone fever. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. Clark's preface (lines 69 to 71) says that certain plants (it names Round-leaf Sundew, Angelica, Polygonatum and Salep orchid), "certain flowers" and some minerals are renowned in "essence-extraction" compounds. *Tsher.sngon* is not named there. "Certain flowers" is not specified, and the Codex does not read the blue poppy into it. In the lines read, the plant has no essence-extraction (*bcud len*) role and no practice. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a bone-healing drug in the Tibetan texts and nothing more can be said from them. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. A fracture or bone fever needs medical care and must not be left to a remedy recorded in a materia medica. The botanical identity rests on one printed binomial and a glossary that does not pair in a row, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. The conservation and legal status of wild high-altitude *Meconopsis* was not researched. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
