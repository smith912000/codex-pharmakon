# Artemisia capillaris — Yin Chen Hao 茵陳蒿 (herb), printed "Yin Chen Haa" (Shennong Bencao Jing)

## *Artemisia capillaris, as the pharmaceutical Latin "Herba Artemisiae Capillaris" that Yang 1998 prints for the entry whose name the text file gives as "Yin Chen Haa"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Shennong text (SBJ).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The entry is in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`). The Latin name is the translator's pharmaceutical Latin and was matched to the binomial by stem in the extraction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, row 105). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The text file prints the name as "Yin Chen Haa". The Codex's own list of Shennong names (`_sources/shennong.csv`, line 142) prints the characters 茵陳蒿 alone, with no transliteration, so the Codex reads "Haa" as an OCR form of "Hao". The characters come from that list and not from Yang's English text. [USER: verify]

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *Yin Chen Haa (Yin Chen Hao), PDF page 65.* The page marker `[[p65]]` is at line 2459 and the heading "Herbs: Middle Class" is printed at line 2460, directly above the entry, which runs from line 2461 to line 2464. The entry is the first on the page. The heading is a running heading repeated at the top of pages (the previous page-top heading is at line 2389 and the next at line 2526), and none is printed for this entry alone. The text reads (lines 2461 to 2464):

"Yin Chen Haa (Herba Artemisiae Capillaris)147 is bitter. It is nontoxic, treating mainly wind, damp, cold, and hot evil qi as well as bound heat jaundice. Protracted taking may make the body light, boost the qi, and slow aging. It grows on the hills and sides of Mount Tai."

The entry gives no other name. Yang's note 147 (lines 2469 to 2482) is the translator's modern commentary and not part of the classic. It begins: "Artemisa Capillaris is able to eliminate wind dampness and bound heat and disinhibit urination. It mainly treats heaven-current seasonal epidemics, headache, head spinning, eye pain, malaria, and concretions and conglomerations. It is specifically effective against jaundice." It goes on to describe yin jaundice and yang jaundice by their signs, and to say that for yin jaundice one prescribes the plant "together with Ginger and Aconite", and for yang jaundice "in combination with Rhubarb and Gardenia". It puts these in the present tense, and it does not say they are the classic's. The note spells the genus "Artemisa" as printed.

*The class heading and what the preface says of the class.* Yang's table of contents prints "Herbs: Middle Class" (line 716). The preface (lines 385 to 390) says of the middle class: "They mainly nurture personality and correspond to humanity. They may or may not be toxic, and [therefore,] one should weigh and ponder before putting them to their appropriate use. If one intends to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion, one should base [one's efforts] on the middle class." Yang's commentary (lines 358 to 362) says that middle-class medicinals "are able to cultivate personality or modify temperament" and that "Since this class of medicinals may be toxic, their prescription requires care." This entry says "It is nontoxic", and it carries a sentence on protracted taking ("make the body light, boost the qi, and slow aging") in the terms the preface uses for the superior class (lines 352 to 355 and 383 to 384). The Codex records both as printed and does not reconcile them.

*Other Artemisia entries in the same text.* The Shennong text prints other *Artemisia* drugs under their own names, with their own Latin: An Lu Zi (*Herba Artemisiae Keiskeanae*, line 1857, superior class), Bai Hao (*Folium Artemisiae Argyi*, line 2004, superior class) and Cao Hao (*Herba Artemisiae Apiacae*, line 3584, inferior class). They are different entries and the Codex keeps them apart.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Artemisia capillaris*, *Yin Chen* and *Yinchen* found no entry on this plant. I_249 (*Artemisia absinthium*) names *Yin Chen Hao* (*Artemisia capillaris* or *A. scoparia*) in its prose (line 69) as the Chinese herb for the liver and gallbladder. I_013 (Artichoke) uses *Yin Chen Hao* as a TCM analogy. I_203 (Rhubarb) names the formula *Yin Chen Hao Tang* in its prose. I_437 (*Artemisia vulgaris*) and I_329 (*Artemisia argyi*) are entries on other *Artemisia* whose spine rows list Shennong. The spine also holds FMT-only rows for *Artemisia annua* (CPU-01863) and *Artemisia sieversiana* (CPU-01864), not yet written. None is an entry on this plant. Nothing is merged.

General composition, stated without numbers: *Artemisia capillaris* is a plant of the daisy family (Asteraceae), and plants of the genus *Artemisia* are generally known as aromatic plants with essential oils. This is a general statement about the genus. The composition of the herb as the Shennong text meant it was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Shennong text lists the entry's actions in its own terms (wind, damp, cold and hot evil qi, bound heat jaundice, and, with protracted taking, making the body light, boosting the qi and slowing aging). These are not modern diagnoses, and they are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The entry gives no preparation. It says "Protracted taking may make the body light, boost the qi, and slow aging", without a quantity, frequency or duration. The translator's note names combinations with Ginger and Aconite or with Rhubarb and Gardenia (lines 2480 to 2482), which are the translator's present-day prescriptions and not the classic's, and gives no quantity. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SBJ:** "bitter" and "nontoxic" (line 2461). No warm or cold nature is printed in the entry, and no meridian or other system of later Chinese medicine is printed in it. The note's "yin jaundice" and "yang jaundice" are the translator's.

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: the middle class (中品), directly under the page-top heading.* The class is assigned by the printed heading "Herbs: Middle Class" (line 2460, `[[p65]]`), which stands immediately above the entry. It is a repeated page-top heading and not a heading printed for this entry alone. This is a printed class, and not a promise of cultivation use. In the preface (lines 385 to 390) the middle class mainly "nurture[s] personality", may or may not be toxic, and is the class on which one bases the effort "to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion". It is not the class the preface ties to making the body light and prolonging life (lines 352 to 355, 383 to 384), which is the superior class.

The entry's own words go further than the class heading: it says "It is nontoxic", it treats "bound heat jaundice", and it carries the sentence "Protracted taking may make the body light, boost the qi, and slow aging." The phrase "make the body light" is *qing shen* 輕身, which the cultivation spec says has no pharmacological equivalent and must not be translated into one. Yang's own note on that phrase in the preface (lines 373 to 378, in a stretch where the OCR order is scrambled) ties such phrases to Daoist writing on elixirs; it is the translator's commentary and not the classic's. The entry asserts no *jing*, *shen* or *neidan* action, and none is added here. The grade (a) is for the class heading. The long-taking sentence is the entry's, and the Codex does not turn it into a second status.

**Practitioner's note.** A middle-class entry in the Chinese scheme, whose class the preface says is weighed before use and used to control disease. The entry itself says "nontoxic" and carries a long-taking sentence, with no preparation, no quantity and no duration, and the Codex has no dose and no clinical record to set beside it. Jaundice is a condition for medical assessment and not for self-treatment with a classic's herb. Nothing here supports taking this herb as a cultivation practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The Shennong entry says "It is nontoxic" and prints no warning. The preface says of the middle class generally that it "may or may not be toxic" and calls for weighing before use. The identity rests on a translator's Latin name, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Jaundice needs medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
