### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** Bryan 1930 (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`) treats milk first as a vehicle. In the chapter on vehicles (line 1131) "Beer, Milk, and Water" are said to be "employed with well balanced impartiality", and "Milk also afforded them some scope; it appears as Fresh Milk, Sour Milk, Spoiled Milk, Cooked Milk, Ass's Milk, Cow's Milk, Man's Milk, Milk-of-a-Woman, Milk-of-a-Woman-who-has-borne-a-Son, Milk-of-the-Sycamore, and Milk-juice". The list `_sources/ebers.csv` has the matching rows "Cow's milk" (line 64), "Human breast milk (from mother of a son)" (line 65) and "Donkey's milk" (line 66), whose source references are "Ebers" plus a Wikipedia page and a German academy site; Bryan's English is the text read here. "Milk" also appears in Bryan's list of the substances taken from animals (line 1417). The forms found in the English are these. *Milk-of-a-Woman*, mixed into one and drunk (line 1635). *Milk-of-a-Woman-who-has-borne-a-Son*, as a drink for coryza, in a charm that begins "Spit it out, thou Slime, Son of Slime" and ends "Then I brought thy remedy for thee, thy drink for thee, to drive away, to heal it: Milk-of-a-Woman-who-has-Borne-a-Son, Fragrant Bread" (lines 3090-3106); to be washed on the eyes with the fruit of the dompalm in one of two clay vessels, the other holding Cow's Milk, "bathe both eyes from the Fruit-of-the-Dompalm. Next wash the Eyes with the Cow's Milk four times for six days" (line 2750); and "to allow the met to take up the remedy" (that is, to enable the body to take up the other remedies), left standing "in a New Vessel until the Cream separates", then smeared on the patient (lines 3206-3210). *Ass's Milk*, with fresh dates, as a douche once the vaginal pustules have appeared (lines 2557-2558), and in two skin remedies (lines 2654, 2702). *Cow's Milk*, put to the fire with thigh-tallow, "Add Milk thereto and let her take for four days" (line 2604), and with fresh bread and bullock's fat as a chewing-remedy (line 3136); and "Cream-from-the-Milk-of-a-Cow" with incense in goose-grease (line 3032). *Fresh Milk* appears in many recipes: a diuretic of water from the bird-pond, swill-of-beer and fresh milk (line 1381), an enema of fresh milk and sea-salt (line 2635), a lotion for the eyes with chips of a new *hennu* pot (line 2734), and the like. A burn is treated with an incantation that closes "To be spoken over Milk-of-a-Woman-who-has-Borne-a-Son" (lines 2289-2314, two incantations). Bryan does not describe the milk itself.

### Active Components

Milk is an emulsion of fat in a water solution of the sugar lactose, with proteins (caseins and whey proteins), minerals such as calcium, and vitamins. Composition differs by species and by stage of lactation. Cream is the fat that rises on standing. Sour or spoiled milk contains lactic acid and the organisms that make it. The sources read give no composition.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. In the Egyptian text milk is a vehicle for other drugs and a drug in its own right, often joined to a spoken formula. Whole milk as food needs no protocol here; the entry does not offer one. Human milk from another woman has infection risks (see below), and the Codex does not recommend taking it. The Codex offers no protocol, dose or preparation.

### Preparation & Dosing

EP: standing in a new vessel until the cream separates, then smeared on (line 3208); boiled with thigh-tallow (line 2604); mixed into one and drunk (line 1635); used as an eye wash four times for six days (line 2750). The only quantities in the lines read are the "four days" and "four times for six days" durations. AT: nothing read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Milk is a food; its nutritional composition is well established. Unpasteurised milk can carry pathogens such as Salmonella, Listeria and Brucella, and cow's milk causes allergy and lactose intolerance in some people. Human milk is the standard infant food, and sharing it informally carries infection risks. No clinical studies were retrieved for the traditional uses. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(b) Framed within *heka*; no cultivation status attested.* The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. *Heka* attaches to this substance in two places that were read: the burn incantation ending "To be spoken over Milk-of-a-Woman-who-has-Borne-a-Son" (lines 2289-2314, two incantations) and the coryza charm in which the milk is "thy drink" (lines 3090-3106). Spell and drug are components of one intervention; leaving out the recitation would lose half the prescription. No deity attribution is made here beyond what the printed lines show, and the Bryan English names the god in the burn incantation as Horus ("O son, Horus!"). That is a therapeutic and ritual use, not a cultivation claim.

### Contraindications

Raw (unpasteurised) milk can carry dangerous bacteria, especially for infants, pregnant women, the elderly and the immunosuppressed. Cow's-milk protein allergy and lactose intolerance are common. Informal sharing or buying of human milk carries a risk of bacterial and viral infection and contamination. Sour, spoiled or old milk should not be taken as medicine. Milk used as a vehicle for another remedy takes on that remedy's risks.
