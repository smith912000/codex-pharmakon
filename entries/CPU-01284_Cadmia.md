# Cadmia (cadmium earth) — Kadmeia (KADMEIA)
## *A furnace-made and mine-dug mineral used in eye medicines and skin-forming plasters; the translator's heading to 5-84 reads "Cadmium Earth — Cadmia", and the chapter text gives no composition* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Cadmia is chapter 84 of Book Five of the *De Materia Medica*, in the section "METALLIC STONES", headed KADMEIA (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10220-10227; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` carries it as 5-84, line 10220, and `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 811 has "Kadmeia", class mineral, identification marked uncertain; the book's page index prints "KADMEIA 781"). The translator's heading (line 10221) reads "SUGGESTED: Cadmium Earth — Cadmia". The chapter is not marked "TOXIC". The chapter falls into parts: kinds and choice, properties, manufacture, tests, and burning and washing.

*How to choose it, and its kinds (line 10222).* "The best cadmia is the Cyprian called botryites, which is thick and not very heavy, but rather inclining to lightness, with the outside in clusters, coloured like ashes, but ashy and rusty within when broken. The next best is an azure [blue] colour on the outside and whiter within with veins like an onyx stone. These are dug out of old mines." Two more kinds follow: *placodes*, "with veins (or stripes) like girdles, which is why they called it zonitis", and *ostracitis*, "slender and mostly black, with an earthy or baked clay outside"; "the white cadmia is useless". Of these, "Botryitis and onychitis are useful for eye medicines, and the rest for plasters and dry medicines that are able to make a new skin. For these things the Cyprian is the best to use. That which is brought out of Macedonia, Thrace or Spain is useless."

*What it does (line 10222).* "It is binding — filling up sores, and cleaning their foulness. It stops discharges of the pores, and dries, draws to a scab, and represses abnormal growths of the flesh, and creates a new skin on malignant ulcers."

*How it is made, and what it is found with (line 10223).* The text says cadmia "also comes from brass made red-hot in a furnace, from the soot sticking to the sides and top of the furnaces". Great iron sticks "called acestides by the metalworkers" are joined at the top so that "the particles that are carried up from the brass may be attached and settle there"; adhering, "they grow into a clump", and "sometimes one kind of it, sometimes two or all are made". It is also made "from a burned stone called purites [pyrites] dug out of a hill that lies over Solis", and "in the same mound are found (as it were) some veins of chalcitis, misy, sory, melanteria, coeruleus, chrysocolla, calcanthus and diphryges". The last of these, chrysocolla, has its own chapter (5-104; see the Chrysocolla entry).

*How to tell the real from the false (lines 10223-10227).* Dioscorides says that some hold cadmia to be "found in metals growing in stones, but they are deceived by the stones having a great resemblance", as with a stone found at Cumae that "has no strength". The stone is recognised "because they are lighter than cadmia, and chewed, they are not mild to the taste but offend the teeth as if stones were being chewed". "Cadmia remains chewable as it has a yielding nature." Three further tests are given: cadmia pounded small, made into a paste with vinegar and dried in the sun "comes together, but that from stone does not allow this"; the stone put in fire "leaps", while cadmia "remains quiet and yields smoke that is a yellowish brassy colour surrounding various other colours"; and the stone, "burnt and cooled again, is altered in colour and lighter", while cadmia "alters in no respect unless you burn it for many days". Cadmia is "also made in the furnaces for silver — paler and lighter — but less effective."

*Burning and washing (lines 10226-10227).* "They burn the previously mentioned cadmia, covering it in coals until it becomes transparent and casts out flaming sparks as plates of iron do, then it must be quenched in Aminaean wine. That which is for scabies [itchy parasitical disease] medicines, must be quenched in vinegar." Some beat the burnt cadmia fine with wine and burn it again in an unfired clay jar "until it is made to look like pumice", then beat and wine it again and burn it a third time "until it is formed perfectly into ashes without any roughness", and "they use this instead of spodium [calcinated powder]". For washing: "Being pounded with water in a mortar, the water then poured away until no filth remains on it washes it; then it is made into balls and put in jars."

*Other chapters that name it.* Cadmia is the standard other recipes are measured against: deer's horn is "washed in a similar way to cadmia [5-84]" (2-63, line 5190); ochre is burnt and washed "like cadmia" (5-108, line 10393); cerussa washed the same way as cadmia is called by some *sacyx* (5-103, *psimuthios*, line 10362); the Phrygian stone has "partitions of white in-between like cadmia" and "is washed like cadmia" (5-141, line 10571); and *psoricum* (5-116, line 10431) is made of two parts of calcitis mixed with one of cadmia, pounded with vinegar, shut in a jar covered in dung for forty days and then burnt. In 5-85 (*pompholux*, translator's heading "Zinc Oxide — Pompholyx", lines 10229-10234) brass-refiners sprinkle "crumbled cadmia" on the working brass to improve it, and pompholux is also made from cadmia "intentionally blown with the bellows". Those are other substances with their own chapters; they are named here only to show where cadmia is cited.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent beyond the translator's gloss "Cadmium Earth". In general terms, the material described, a deposit that forms on the walls of furnaces working brass or zinc-bearing ores, is largely an oxide of zinc, and such furnace deposits often carry other metals from the ore as well, which can include cadmium and lead. The element cadmium was later named after this older mineral name. Cadmium and lead compounds are poisonous. The translator's gloss is an identification offered in modern terms, not a statement made by the text. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records a binding, drying, scab-forming and skin-forming mineral used in eye medicines, plasters and dry applications, and it is chiefly valuable to the Codex for the way it is taught: through its choice by form, its provenance, and its tests against a counterfeit. The Codex does not recommend using it. Furnace deposits of the metals involved are toxic to inhale, swallow or put in a wound, and no part of the text supports a safe modern use.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives the preparations above: burning in coals until transparent with sparks, quenching in Aminaean wine (or in vinegar for scabies medicines); the three-fold burning with wine in an unfired clay jar to an ash used in place of *spodium*; and washing by pounding in a mortar with water, pouring off the water until no filth remains, and forming balls to store in jars (lines 10226-10227). It gives the tests of the real material (chewing, vinegar paste dried in the sun, behaviour in fire). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. The wine "Aminaean" is printed as such and is not explained in the lines read. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Zinc oxide is a mild astringent and drying agent, used for centuries and still in modern skin protectants; it is applied to the skin, not swallowed in quantity. Cadmium compounds are toxic, with the kidney a principal target on chronic exposure, and cadmium is a recognised carcinogen. Fumes from heated zinc-bearing metals cause metal-fume fever when inhaled. Lead compounds are poisonous. Roasting and grinding furnace deposits creates dust that is hazardous to breathe. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** The text prints these qualities for cadmia (line 10222): "binding", and that it "dries, draws to a scab", fills sores, cleans foulness, stops discharges of the pores, represses abnormal growths of flesh and forms new skin. It prints no hot or cold, and no degree. It also states which kinds suit which use: *botryites* and *onychitis* for eye medicines; the others for plasters and dry medicines.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is asserted here. What this chapter offers a practitioner is the discipline of attention that runs through his work: provenance (Cyprian best; Macedonian, Thracian and Spanish worthless), form (clustered, ash-coloured, rusty within), and a set of tests against counterfeit (chewing, the vinegar paste, the behaviour in fire). That is a practice of assay, and it is attested for this substance in the chapter itself. It is not a cultivation status; the chapter makes no claim about the person's constitution, vitality or long life.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. Cadmia is a dangerous metallic furnace product that Dioscorides used for eyes and ulcers; the Codex does not recommend taking, burning or applying it. The chapter's lasting lesson is the chewing, paste and fire tests: how to inspect a material before trusting it, and how readily a stone can pass for a drug. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Toxicity:** furnace deposits of zinc, lead and cadmium are poisonous; do not swallow, burn, inhale the dust or fumes of, or apply this material, and never put it in or near the eye.
- **Fumes and dust:** burning and grinding it, as the text's recipes direct, releases hazardous fume and dust.
- **Wounds and ulcers:** the text uses it on malignant ulcers; metallic dusts in open wounds can poison and delay healing.
- **Pregnancy and children:** unsuitable.
- **Identification:** "Cadmium Earth" is the translator's gloss; the text identifies cadmia by form, source and the tests it gives, not by composition.
