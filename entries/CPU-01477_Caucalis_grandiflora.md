# Caucalis grandiflora — Kaukalis (Dioscorides 2-169)

## *Caucalis grandiflora (the translators' suggestion; "Caucalis, Hedge Parsley, Bur Parsley")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The SUGGESTED line lists three binomials, "Caucalis grandiflora, Caucalis pumila, Caucalis platycarpus", so the choice of the first as the row name is the Codex's, not the translators'.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), heading *2-169. KAUKALIS* at line 5995, SUGGESTED line at 5996, text at lines 5997-5998.

The whole chapter reads: "Caucalis (also called wild daucus) has a little stalk a foot in length or more, with leaves similar to marathrum [3-81], very divided and rough, and on the top of it a white tuft with a fragrant scent. This is also used as a vegetable (eaten either raw or boiled), and is diuretic. It is also called caucum, or myitis, Democritus calls it bryon, the Romans, pes gallinaceum, some call it pes pulli, and the Egyptians call it seselis."

So the chapter gives a short description (a stalk of a foot or more, divided rough leaves like those of marathrum, a white fragrant tuft at the top), one food use (a vegetable, raw or boiled), one action (diuretic) and a run of names. It gives no habitat, no season and no preparation beyond eating. The chapter sits in Book Two among the vegetables (running head "Lachana: Vegetables"), directly after 2-168 Skandux, a different plant already on the spine as I_1005 (*Anthriscus sylvestris*), and before 2-170 Euzomon. The translators' index lists Caucalis at pages 78 and 295; the page 78 mention was not opened for this entry. [USER: verify] The comparison to marathrum points to chapter 3-81, which was not read for this entry.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. The *Caucalis* species named by the translators belong to the carrot family (Apiaceae), a family that contains both familiar food herbs and deadly poisons, and whose members are easily confused with one another. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a wild herb eaten as a vegetable and said to be diuretic. These are the text's claims. The Codex does not recommend foraging it: the identification is the translators' and uncertain across three suggested species, and carrot-family plants carry real risk of confusion with fatal look-alikes.

### Preparation & Dosing

2-169 gives only eating the plant "either raw or boiled". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

A diuretic effect is a claim about increased urine flow, and no study of any *Caucalis* species was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints "diuretic" and no warming, cooling, drying or other quality for 2-169.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant by its parts (the foot-long stalk, the divided rough leaves, the white fragrant tuft), what it is called in several languages, and what is claimed for it. It assigns the plant no status, and the identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. As a wild vegetable of the carrot family it asks for certain identification before any use, which is Dioscorides' own discipline of attention. If identification is not certain, the answer is to leave it.

### Contraindications

Wild carrot-family plants are easily confused with poison hemlock and other deadly relatives, and the sap of wild umbellifers can irritate the skin. Do not forage from this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. A diuretic is not a safe thing to take unsupervised by anyone with kidney, heart or fluid-balance problems or who is pregnant. [USER: verify]
