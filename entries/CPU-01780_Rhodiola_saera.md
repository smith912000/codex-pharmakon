# Rhodiola saera — sro.lo, Solo (rGyud-bzhi); "Rhodiola saera (Prain) Fu" as Clark prints it

## *Rhodiola saera (Prain) Fu, the first of four identifications Clark 1995 prints for sro.lo (then Strobilanthes japonicus, Solms-Laubachia eurycarpa and Gypsophila dahurica); the spelling "saera" is as printed and may be a misprint of "sacra"; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text prints Solo and sets Rhodiola crenulata, not this species, against it by position* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and under this binomial on Clark's edition alone.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Shennong (Yang 1998), Sushruta, Ebers and Assyrian texts on disk for *Rhodiola* found no hit. The Dioscorides text prints *Rhodiola rosea* in the translators' SUGGESTED line (8403) for a Greek drug, *rhodia radix*, which is a different species and is not read as this one. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 134) the row is keyed by the Tibetan name *sro.lo*, with the identification "Rhodiola saera (Prain) Fu; Strobilanthes japonicus (red type); Solms-Laubachia eurycarpa (Maxim) Botsch.; Gypsophila dahurica (brown type)" and the locus "Clark 1995 (line 1011)". The spine row (CPU-01780) took the **first** identification, *Rhodiola saera*, and not a later one. Clark's name index prints "Rhodiola saera" (line 1639) as well.

**Identity doubt.** Clark's entry does not say which of its plants is meant by which type. It gives *Rhodiola saera* and *Strobilanthes japonicus (red type)* before the second name *sug.hdra*, and it gives three other Latin names for the "white" type in the commentary (below). Clark's preface does not explain the epithet "saera". The Codex's existing entry I_318 (line 59) writes "Rhodiola sacra" for this drug, without a line reference or authors. The lines read do not show whether "saera" is a misprint of "sacra", and the Codex prints it as the spine and Clark do. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" and (line 68) that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect". [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1011).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry begins line 1011, after *gser.tig* (line 1010), and the same long line runs on into the entries that follow (*gyah.kyi.ma* and others); a page number ("102") stands on line 1012. The entry reads: "sro.lo: Rhodiola saera (Prain) Fu: Strobilanthes japonicus (red type): sug.hdra: Solms-Laubachia eurycarpa (Maxim) Botsch.: Gypsophila dahurica (brown type): [sro.lo is of two types. The white one (Cochlearia scapiflora: Cochlearia rusticana: Platycodon grandiflorum) grows in rocky, snowy mountains and has a thin root with large trunk and bark. It has a thick, glossy leaf and an extremely attractive white flower. White sro.lo cures lung diseases and dysentery]. sug.hdra, [the brown type, has a single-stranded root whence springs the trunk, brown petioles, white flower bearing dewdrops and large, flat, crescent moon-shaped pod. There is also a subspecies of this type with thin pods. (All types of sro.lo)] cure lung fevers."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, and that those in round brackets come from the oral instructions of Dr. Yeshe Donden and Dr. Pema Dorje. Everything about the two types, their habitat, and "White sro.lo cures lung diseases and dysentery" is therefore the commentary layer. The phrase "(All types of sro.lo)" is from the oral instructions. What stands outside the brackets is the name, the identifications, which are Clark's, and the verb phrase "cure lung fevers". The commentary's description of the white type names three other plants, none of them *Rhodiola*, so the one binomial the spine took is not the one the commentary describes. Clark's name index lists *sro.lo* (line 1768).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (read through the name, not the binomial).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), line 3428, within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines": "Solo and Sugdra cure hot disorders of the lungs." In chapter 21 (colophon at line 3689), line 3611 reads: "Chugang, Shingngar, Gundrum, Tarbu, Ruta, Gadhur, Atong, Solo karpo and Sugdra are the group of medicines that cure disorders of the lungs." The index lists "Solokarpo 221" (line 7498). The Men-Tsee-Khang text prints no "Rhodiola saera" or "sacra"; it prints *Rhodiola crenulata* (line 5834). The glossary's "S" block gives seventeen phonetic names (lines 5774 to 5785), among them "Solo" (eleventh), "Solo karpo" (twelfth), "Subka" (fourteenth) and "Sugdra" (fifteenth), with the transliterations "sro lo" and "sro lo dkar po" (lines 5793 to 5794). Its scientific names, counted in order, put *Rhodiola crenulata* eleventh and "Pegaeophyton sacapiflorum" (as printed) twelfth (lines 5834 and 5838), and *Anemone tatrasepala* fourteenth (line 5844). So by position "Solo" stands against *Rhodiola crenulata* and "Solo karpo" ("white Solo", as the Tibetan *dkar po* is white) against *Pegaeophyton scapiflorum*, which agrees with Clark's commentary placing *Cochlearia scapiflora* with the white type. The count is checked on "Shingngar" (first, *Glycyrrhiza glabra*), "Shudag" (fifth, *Acorus calamus*), "Shumoza" (seventh, fenugreek) and "Sumchutik" (seventeenth, *Elettaria cardamomum*). It is not matched at "Sugdra" (fifteenth), whose scientific name is "Androsace" in that count, and Clark's *sug.hdra* has two other identifications, so the count is not confirmed there. The glossary does not print the pairings in rows, so they are by position only. [USER: verify]

The two editions agree in kind: a lung drug *Solo* (sro.lo) beside *Sugdra* in the Men-Tsee-Khang lists, and Clark's "cure lung fevers" for the group of *sro.lo* and *sug.hdra*. The Codex reads *Solo* and *sro.lo* as one drug from the spelling and that agreement; neither text says so. The Men-Tsee-Khang text sets a different *Rhodiola* species against the name, so the two editions agree on the Tibetan drug and not on the species. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Rhodiola saera*, *sro.lo* and *Solo karpo* found no entry on this binomial. I_318 (*Rhodiola crenulata*, Rose Root) is the entry for the glossary's species and has a paragraph on *sro.lo* that glosses it to another *Rhodiola*; this entry does not repeat it. I_201 (*Rhodiola*, tags FMT, CM) and I_202 (*Rhodiola rosea*, tags TCM, FMT) are entries on other species; I_202 names the Tibetan *sro lo dmar po* ("red sro lo") with no line reference, and I did not check its claims against the lines above. I_1300 (*Hippophae tibetana*) quotes line 3611 for neighbouring drugs. I_1229 (*Anemone tatrasepala*) is the entry for the glossary's fourteenth scientific name in the same block. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Rhodiola* is a genus of the stonecrop family (Crassulaceae), whereas the other names Clark prints (*Strobilanthes*, *Solms-Laubachia*, *Gypsophila*, *Cochlearia*, *Platycodon*) belong to several other families, so the identifications point to unrelated plants. The lines read give no composition for the Tibetan drug, and nothing is stated here about what it contains.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug whose types "cure lung fevers" (Clark, line 1011), whose white type "cures lung diseases and dysentery" in the commentary layer, and which with *Sugdra* "cure[s] hot disorders of the lungs" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3428). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only through Clark's first of four names, and they are not Codex protocols. A lung illness or dysentery needs medical care, and the Codex gives no protocol and no dose for either.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary describes root, trunk, leaf, flower and pod of the types and does not say which part is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 1011 prints no taste, power or post-digestive taste for *sro.lo* or *sug.hdra*. The Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3428, 3611, 5793 to 5794) print none either. What is printed is the lung use in both editions and, in the commentary, the white type's use against dysentery.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: its types cure lung fevers, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text lists it among the lung medicines. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names (*sro.lo*, *sug.hdra*, *Solo*, *Sugdra*, *Rhodiola*) found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, colophon at line 3950), and the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence extraction (lines 971, 1043, 1078, 1103, 1121 and 1144) do not include it. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name more securely than to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a lung-fever drug in a short list, one of four identifications Clark offers for a Tibetan name, with no preparation and no dose. The strength-giving reputation that *Rhodiola* carries in modern markets has no support in the lines read, and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Lung illness and dysentery need medical care, the identity is open between plants of several genera and families, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Several of the plants named (*Rhodiola* among them) are high-altitude species of which wild collection is a conservation concern in some regions; that is a general statement and no conservation information was read for this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
