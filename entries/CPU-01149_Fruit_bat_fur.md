# Fruit bat fur — bya.ma.byi (Tibetan, as transliterated by Clark 1995); "Fur of the flying fox" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *The hair of the fruit bat, which Clark glosses "Fruit Bat: Vespertilio superans" and the Men-Tsee-Khang translation calls the "flying fox"; Clark prints "tail feathers" for the part used* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The text is Clark's 1995 translation of *The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`), Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, "the powers of medicines". Under the heading "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147) it says: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body. Their indications and powers are explained as follows" (line 1148). The fruit bat is under the heading "Hair" (about line 1191), in this entry (line 1195):

"bya.ma.byi: Fruit Bat: Vespertilio superans: [has sixteen names]. Its [tail] feathers expel womb disorders."

The first element, *bya.ma.byi*, is the Tibetan name in Clark's transliteration; "Vespertilio superans" is his binomial gloss; the square-bracketed "[has sixteen names]" and "[tail]" are his. In his preface Clark says that in preparing the translation he "interpolated liberally from the Blue Aquamarine commentary" (line 80), so I take the square brackets to mark such additions, but his text does not say so for this entry. [USER: verify] Two things in the line are odd and I leave them as printed: the word is "feathers", for an animal that has fur, and "tail" appears only in brackets. The entry's name, "fur", follows the second translation on disk.

That second text is the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 *Root and Explanatory Tantra* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`). Its list of the same thirteen animal substances names "fur" in place of "hair" (line 3503), and the passage reads (line 3559): "Fur of the flying fox cures disorders of the uterus and aids in delivering a fetus." The two translations agree on the animal's family and on a womb indication. Clark gives "expel womb disorders"; Men-Tsee-Khang gives "cures disorders of the uterus and aids in delivering a fetus". The second adds the delivery, which I do not find in Clark's line. The 2011 text lists the part as fur and says nothing of the tail.

Clark's list has a separate row for bat flesh: "bya.wang.sha: Bat flesh: [has thirteen names]. It is anti-emetic [and dispels spirits]" (line 1170). The Tibetan word is different (*bya.wang.sha*, not *bya.ma.byi*), and I do not decide whether the two animals are the same or related. Bat flesh is not part of this entry.

Both translators' prefaces say that animal products have largely fallen out of use. Clark: "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57). The Men-Tsee-Khang translators: "the use of animal products was negligible. Presently ... animal products in Tibetan medicine have been completely substituted by equally effective herbal substances. However, in order to reveal the richness of Tibetan medical knowledge ... their potencies have been revealed in this translation as it is reflected in the Gyueshi" (line 687).

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, fur or hair of a mammal is keratin, a structural protein rich in sulfur-containing amino acids, with a thin coating of skin oils and, in a wild animal, dust, parasites and microbes. Nothing in the entry links any constituent to the uses given.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The rGyud-bzhi records bat fur as a remedy for womb disorders, and in the 2011 translation as an aid in delivering a fetus. The Codex does not recommend it. Disorders of the womb and a delayed or obstructed delivery need medical care, and a substance taken to expel or deliver a fetus can do serious harm. Wild bats are carriers of viruses that can infect people, including rabies-related viruses and others, and many fruit bat species are threatened. The entry is a record of what the two translations say.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Hair is keratin and has no known uterine action. The traditional terms ("womb disorders", "expel") belong to the Tibetan frame and cannot be mapped onto a modern diagnosis. Bats, including fruit bats, are reservoirs of several viruses that can pass to people through contact with their bodies, saliva or excreta. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The fruit bat's hair is named in the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica among thirteen kinds of animal substance, as a remedy for womb disorders (and, in the 2011 translation, delivery). The project's cultivation spec places Tibetan medicine as the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice and not reaching the root of illness (the three poisons); that is a statement about the tradition's frame, not about this substance, and no passage read connects this substance to that frame, to *bcud len* (essence extraction), or to any practice. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted. Both translators say animal products have largely been given up in practice, and the 2011 text gives Buddhism's rejection of harm to any being as part of the reason (line 687). The attestation for this entry rests on single lines of two translations.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The line is a record of a remedy for womb disorders, the tradition itself has largely replaced animal products with herbs, and the Codex does not recommend it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy and delivery:** the 2011 text gives the fur as an aid in delivering a fetus; do not use any such material in pregnancy or labour; seek obstetric care.
- **Womb disorders:** need medical assessment.
- **Zoonosis and hygiene:** bats carry viruses; avoid contact with bats and with their fur or excreta, and seek medical advice after any bite or scratch.
- **Conservation and law:** many bat and flying fox species are threatened or protected in law; do not take any animal for these uses.
- **Ethics:** the Tibetan tradition itself, in the 2011 translators' words, denounces causing harm to any being; the Codex does not recommend harming any animal for these uses.
