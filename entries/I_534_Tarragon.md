# I-534 | Tarragon

**Binomial:** *Artemisia dracunculus* L.
**Family:** Asteraceae (Anthemideae)
**Common Names:** French tarragon; estragon; tarragon; *Estragon* (German/French); *Tarkhun* (Arabic/Russian); *Tarhun* (Turkish); *Tardkhun* (Persian)
**Cultivar Distinction:** 'Sativa' (French tarragon — culinary standard; low-thujone; seed-sterile; propagated vegetatively) vs. 'Inodora' (Russian tarragon — seed-fertile; lower estragole; more thujone; medicinally distinct)
**Part Used:** Aerial parts (herb, particularly leaf); fresh herb most pharmacologically relevant; dried herb for pharmaceutical use
**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (carminative, digestive, emmenagogue, anti-infective)

---

## I. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

*Artemisia dracunculus* L. — the epithet *dracunculus* means "little dragon" (from Latin *draco*), cognate with the Arabic *tarkhun* and Spanish *estragón* (both meaning "little dragon"). The name's etymology encodes the traditional use: the herb was believed to cure the bites of "little dragons" (serpents, venomous creatures) — an antivenin attribution parallel to the artemisia-vulgaris/snakebite connection in multiple traditions. The Arabic *tarkhun* → French *estragon* → English "tarragon" preserves this linguistic transmission.

**French vs. Russian tarragon — critical distinction:**

| Feature | French tarragon (*A. dracunculus* 'Sativa') | Russian tarragon (*A. dracunculus* 'Inodora') |
|---|---|---|
| Seed fertility | STERILE — no seeds (must propagate vegetatively) | Fertile — produces seeds |
| Flavor | Intense anise-like; complex; culinary premium | Milder; somewhat medicinal; less complex |
| Estragole content | HIGH (50–80% EO) | LOW (5–20% EO) |
| Thujone | LOW (<5% EO) | HIGHER (10–30% EO) |
| Medical use | Lower thujone safety; primary food-medicine herb | Higher thujone; more traditional medicinal |
| Recognition | Michelin-star kitchens; béarnaise sauce | Central Asian folk medicine (*Tarkhun* drink) |

French tarragon cannot be grown from seed — it is propagated exclusively by division, cuttings, or tissue culture. The presence of viable seeds in commercially labeled "French tarragon" indicates adulteration with Russian tarragon or another *Artemisia*.

The sterility of French tarragon may reflect extreme selective breeding in medieval European cultivation — maximum estragole (flavor compound) production selected at the cost of reproductive fertility. This is a rare case in plant pharmacology/pharmacognosy of human selection producing seed sterility through cultivation pressure.

**Central Asian and Persian (unlisted in matrix):** *Tarkhun* has extensive traditional use in Persian/Iranian, Turkish, Armenian, Georgian, and Central Asian folk medicine (the Caucasus region) — as a food, beverage flavoring (*Tarkhun* soda is a traditional Georgian green carbonated drink made with tarragon), and medicine (digestive, carminative, anti-fever, toothache remedy). This tradition is outside the 9-source matrix but represents a robust cross-cultural record.

**Convergence Score: 2/9** — (CM ✓; DMM ~)

Low convergence score reflects geographic restriction: tarragon is native to Central Asia and steppe regions of Russia/Mongolia; it reached the Mediterranean relatively late (after the Arab-Islamic transmission) and was not present in ancient Egyptian, Mesopotamian, South Asian, East Asian, or American traditions before the medieval period.

## III. Convergence Analysis

Despite the low cross-traditional convergence, tarragon occupies a specific pharmacological niche that multiple independent traditions have recognized for the herbs within their respective pharmacopoeias: carminative + digestive + mild analgesic. The Arabic-Islamic tradition provides the clearest pharmacological record, and the Unani characterization of *tarkhūn* as warming, carminative, sleep-promoting, and toothache-relieving maps precisely onto estragole (carminative/antispasmodic mechanism) and eugenol-like phenylpropanoids (dental analgesia mechanism).

**Tarragon as a borderline food-medicine herb:** Among all the Artemisia entries (I-531, I-532, I-533, I-534), tarragon is the most heavily weighted toward food use and the least toward pharmaceutical practice. Its position in the Codex is taxonomically appropriate (Artemisia sequence) and pharmacologically coherent (the mechanisms are real) but its primary modern relevance is as a culinary herb with pharmacological properties, not a pharmaceutical preparation in the European sense.

## IV. Phytochemical Composition

### A. Essential Oil (0.3–1.0% DW) — Dominated by Estragole

**Estragole** (methyl chavicol; 1-allyl-4-methoxybenzene; MW 148.2): 50–80% of French tarragon EO; the primary flavor compound producing the distinctive anise/fennel-like character of French tarragon. Also present in basil, fennel, anise, and various *Artemisia* and *Ocimum* species.

**Pharmacology of estragole — dual nature:**
- CARMINATIVE: estragole at culinary concentrations relaxes GI smooth muscle (L-type Ca²⁺ channel antagonism → reduced intestinal spasm → reduced flatulence). This is the mechanism for the "digestive stimulant" traditional use.
- GENOTOXICITY CONCERN: estragole is a procarcinogen. Its metabolic activation pathway: estragole → 1'-hydroxyestragole (CYP1A2) → 1'-sulfooxyestragole (SULT1A1) → reactive carbocation → DNA adducts. The reactive sulfate ester alkylates N7-guanine in DNA → potential mutagenesis. Estragole showed dose-dependent hepatocarcinogenicity in rodent studies.

**EU Scientific Committee on Food (SCF) assessment (2001):** Estragole is genotoxic and carcinogenic in rodents; no safe threshold can be established. However, at typical culinary use levels, calculated human cancer risk is negligible (margin of exposure analysis: estimated lifetime risk from culinary tarragon consumption is orders of magnitude below regulatory concern thresholds). SCF recommendation: minimize exposure in food additives and herbal preparations with concentrated estragole; culinary use as fresh herb is acceptable.

**Clinical implication:** Culinary use of fresh tarragon (1–5 g per meal) — safe. Pharmaceutical preparations with concentrated estragole (EO preparations, high-dose capsules of concentrated tarragon extract) — not recommended due to estragole genotoxicity concern. Do NOT use tarragon EO internally.

**Ocimene (cis- and trans-):** 5–15% of EO; monoterpene with anti-inflammatory properties; anti-mite, insect deterrent (acaricidal applications in veterinary use).

**Phellandrene, sabinene, terpinolene:** Remaining monoterpene fraction; aromatic; antimicrobial.

**Thujone (French tarragon):** Very low (<3% EO); safety advantage over wormwood and mugwort for culinary use.

### B. Phenylpropanoids (Non-EO)

**Herniarin** (7-methoxycoumarin): Present; antispasmodic (same compound as in chamomile); photosensitizing (mild; not clinically significant at culinary doses).

**Isoquercitrin, chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid:** Phenolic fraction; antioxidant.

### C. Flavonoids

**Patulitrin (6-methoxyquercetin-3-glucoside):** *Artemisia*-specific; anti-inflammatory.

**Jaceosidin, eupatilin (polymethoxylated flavones):** Same compounds as in *A. vulgaris* (I-531); anti-inflammatory; gastroprotective (eupatilin — Stillen connection).

**Axillarin (6-hydroxy-3,5,7,4'-tetramethoxyflavone):** Characteristic tarragon flavonoid; anti-inflammatory; cytotoxic in vitro against cancer cells.

### D. Polyacetylenes

**Dehydromatricaria ester and C₁₄ enynes:** Present in root; antimicrobial; anti-inflammatory.

### E. Coumarins

**Scopoletin (6-methoxy-7-hydroxycoumarin), esculetin:** Anti-inflammatory; mild antispasmodic; present in small amounts.

## V. Mechanisms of Action

### A. Carminative / Antispasmodic (GI)

**Estragole EO fraction:** L-type Ca²⁺ channel blockade in GI smooth muscle → reduced tone and contractility → reduced gas retention and spasm → carminative effect. This mechanism is shared with anise (I-≈555), fennel (I-≈556), and dill (I-≈554) — all estragole or anethole-containing Apiaceae with the same GI smooth muscle relaxation pharmacology. Tarragon's carminative mechanism is pharmacologically equivalent but provided in an Asteraceae plant.

**Herniarin coumarin:** Additional antispasmodic via Ca²⁺ antagonism; synergistic with estragole.

### B. Analgesic (Dental)

**Eugenol-equivalent mechanism:** Tarragon contains trace eugenol and eugenol-analogs; estragole itself has TRPV1 (capsaicin receptor) interaction that produces transient burning followed by desensitization — potential contribution to dental pain management. The traditional toothache remedy (place fresh tarragon leaf on aching tooth) is pharmacologically plausible from this mechanism, though less potent than clove (*Eugenia caryophyllata*, highest eugenol density in Codex).

### C. Anti-diabetic (Emerging — Russian Tarragon)

**Russian tarragon (*A. dracunculus* 'Inodora') aqueous extracts:** Extensive research by Zuberi lab (Texas Tech University, 2000s–2010s) showing Russian tarragon extract (water-soluble fraction, not estragole-rich EO) significantly improves insulin sensitivity, reduces postprandial glycemia, and reduces HbA1c in diabetic rodent models and small human pilot studies.

**Active fraction:** Polymethoxylated flavonoids (specifically methoxylated flavones and polyacetylenic compounds in the aqueous extract rather than the EO). Level B pilot evidence in human Type 2 diabetes (Bays 2012, n=18, open-label pilot, Russian tarragon extract 1 g/day × 90 days; FBG −8.6 mg/dL, insulin sensitivity improved, P<0.05). This anti-diabetic research distinguishes **Russian tarragon** from French tarragon — the flavonoid/polyacetylene anti-diabetic fraction is higher in Russian tarragon; estragole (carcinogenicity concern) is lower.

### D. Antimicrobial / Antifungal

**Estragole + ocimene EO fraction:** Bacteriostatic against *S. aureus*, *E. coli*, *Candida* in vitro (MIC 0.1–2 mg/mL). Relevant for food preservation (culinary tarragon use in pickling, marinades → antimicrobial preservation mechanism). Traditional food-preservation use of aromatic herbs (tarragon, thyme, rosemary, sage) is pharmacologically rational — the antimicrobial EO compounds reduce food-borne pathogen load.

## VI. Clinical Evidence

### A. Anti-diabetic — Russian Tarragon (Level B)

Bays et al. (2012): Level B pilot; significant FBG and insulin sensitivity improvement. Mechanistically supported by multiple rodent studies. Insufficient for formal recommendation but supports the Russian/Central Asian folk use for diabetes-related conditions ("reduces blood sugar" in Central Asian herbalism).

### B. Dental Analgesia (Level C / Traditional)

Traditional use (CN) documentation; mechanistically plausible (estragole TRPV1 interaction, eugenol-analog); no formal clinical trials. Level C.

### C. Carminative (Level C / Commission E)

Commission E did not formally evaluate tarragon (insufficient evidence as pharmaceutical preparation); the carminative use is supported by mechanism and traditional record. Level C.

## VII. Phenomenological Record

**Béarnaise sauce and pharmacological reduction:** Béarnaise sauce — the emulsified butter sauce that defines French haute cuisine — is distinguished from hollandaise by its inclusion of tarragon (and shallots, reduced in white wine and wine vinegar). The reduction step (tarragon simmered in acid medium) partially hydrolyzes estragole ester bonds, reduces thujone content (thujone is volatile and partially lost), and concentrates the herniarin coumarin fraction. The culinary preparation that produces the finest French sauce also produces the most pharmacologically safe tarragon preparation — an unintentional optimization of safety alongside flavor.

**Soviet *Tarkhun* soda (Georgia):** *Tarkhun* is a bright green carbonated soft drink first produced in Tbilisi, Georgia (then part of the Russian Empire) in 1887 by confectioner Mitrofan Lagidze. It is made by steeping fresh tarragon in carbonated water with sugar — a food-medicine tradition producing a beverage consumed for its distinctive taste and traditional digestive properties. Soviet-era production of *Tarkhun* made it one of the most recognizable traditional beverages in the former USSR; it is now produced by multiple companies across the Caucasus, Central Asia, and Russia. The tarragon soda represents the clearest surviving commercialized food-medicine tradition from Central Asian tarragon culture — pharmacologically active (carminative, digestive) at the doses consumed in one or two servings.

**Arabic anesthesia history:** Ibn Sīnā (*Al-Qānūn*) described *tarkhūn* for toothache and sleep promotion — the two applications most directly supported by estragole's TRPV1-desensitization analgesic mechanism and its mild CNS effects at higher doses. The Arabic tradition of "soporific sponges" (*spongia somnifera*) for surgical anesthesia used a combination of herbs including *A. dracunculus* relatives; tarragon's TRPV1 effects and mild sedation at medicinal doses may have contributed meaningfully to this pre-modern anesthetic tradition even without the vocabulary to characterize the mechanism.

## VIII. Practical Application

### Regulatory Status

**No Commission E monograph** for *Artemisia dracunculus*. Not formally approved as a pharmaceutical herbal medicine in the EU or EMA.

**Food status:** Tarragon (French) is classified as a food additive/flavoring (GRAS in USA; QPS in EU) at culinary use levels. The SCF genotoxicity assessment applies to concentrated estragole preparations, not to culinary use of fresh or dried herb at typical seasoning amounts.

**Culinary pharmaceutical principle:** In this entry, the primary therapeutic application is as a medicinal food — carminative and digestive at quantities consumed as food seasoning. This is explicitly different from the concentrated pharmaceutical preparations of the other *Artemisia* entries.

### Therapeutic Doses (Culinary Medicinal)

**As food/seasoning (primary mode):** 1–5 g fresh French tarragon per meal, incorporated in food preparation; the carminative effect is meaningful at these doses and the estragole genotoxicity concern is negligible.

**Infusion (tisane):** 1–2 g dried tarragon per 200 mL water, steep 10 minutes; 1–2 cups after meals for carminative/digestive effect. Maximum 4 weeks continuous use.

**NOT recommended:** Tarragon essential oil internal use; concentrated tarragon extract capsules; preparations claiming >1% EO tarragon equivalent (estragole genotoxicity concern at elevated doses).

**Russian tarragon extract (anti-diabetic — emerging):** 1 g/day standardized aqueous extract (per Bays 2012 pilot); not yet a formal recommendation; monitor FBG if used adjunctively.

### Contraindications and Safety

**Pregnancy:** Emmenagogue tradition; estragole metabolism; avoid at medicinal doses. Culinary use (1–2 g per meal) generally considered safe in pregnancy but not studied.

**Estragole-sensitive individuals:** Rare individuals with enhanced CYP1A2 and SULT1A1 activity may generate more 1'-sulfooxyestragole from equal estragole intake; theoretical increased genotoxic risk; precaution with concentrated preparations.

**Asteraceae allergy:** Standard precaution; cross-reactivity with *Artemisia* pollen (major aeroallergen class in Central/Eastern Europe).

**Drug interactions (minimal at culinary doses):** No significant pharmacokinetic interactions documented at food-level exposure; concentrated EO preparations may weakly inhibit CYP enzymes.
