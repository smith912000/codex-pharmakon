# Lepidium apetalum — Dharyaken, dar.ya.kan (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary by column position; Clark 1995)

## *Lepidium apetalum, as the binomial printed in the scientific-name run of the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary's "D" block, set against the Tibetan name "Dharyaken" by column position; Clark 1995 prints it as the fourth of six identifications for dar.ya.kan* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. Both print *Lepidium apetalum*: Men-Tsee-Khang in its glossary (line 5217) and Clark in the entry for *dar.ya.kan* (line 1011; the Latin-name index, line 1609, prints "Lepidum apetalum"). A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk for *Lepidium* found other species and a genus-level Shennong drug, and no *Lepidium apetalum*. They are described below and are not attributed to this row. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 258) the key is the glossary's own scientific name, *Lepidium apetalum*, at "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 5217)", with the note "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". Line 5217 prints five binomials in a row (Hippuris vulgaris, Schisandra spenanthera, Lepidium apetalum, Arisaema jacquemontii, Croton tiglium), and the key is the third of them. The glossary gives one binomial per entry. In Clark's entry, by contrast, *Lepidium apetalum* is the fourth of six identifications, and the extraction made no Clark row for it (the Tibetan extraction holds no row for *dar.ya.kan* at all).

**Identity doubt.** Clark prints for *dar.ya.kan* six names from several genera: *Moerhingia latifolia*, *Draba nemorosa*, *Scabiosa comosa*, *Lepidium apetalum*, *Lepidium latifolium* and *Astragalus tongolensis* (spellings as printed). Two of the six are *Lepidium*. The glossary gives one, *Lepidium apetalum*. Clark's footnote 15 to the entry says: "There are twenty-five different types of dar.ya.kan" (line 1544), and her preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance". The Codex cannot say which plant the Tibetan texts meant, and the entry stands for the Tibetan drug. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "D" block, lines 5179 to 5261).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs, and the pairing of a Tibetan name with a binomial is not printed in one row. The phonetic names run from "Dali" (line 5181) to "Drita sazin" (line 5196). Counting each name separately, "Dali" is first, "Dhambu kara" second, "Dhatig" third (line 5183, which holds "Dhatig Dharyaken") and "Dharyaken" fourth, then "Dhawa" fifth (line 5184). The transliterations include "da trig dar ya kan" (line 5200). The scientific names begin at line 5215 with "Rhododendron laudandum" (the first), and line 5217 gives, in order, "Hippuris vulgaris" (second), "Schisandra spenanthera" (third), "Lepidium apetalum" (fourth), "Arisaema j acquemontii" (fifth) and "Croton tinglium" (sixth). The English names (lines 5222 to 5229) read "-", "Chinese magnolia vine", "-", "Snake plant", so the third has the English "Chinese magnolia vine" and the fourth has "-". So "Dharyaken" stands against *Lepidium apetalum*, "Dhatig" against *Schisandra* and "Dhawa" against *Arisaema*. One neighbour checks against Clark: "Dhambu kara" (transliteration "'dam bu ka ra", line 5199) stands second against *Hippuris vulgaris*, and Clark's *hdam.bu.ka.ra* (line 1057) prints "Hippuris vulgaris". "Dhawa" stands fifth against *Arisaema*, which is the pairing I_1233 reads. The pairing is by position and is not printed in a row. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (lines 1011 to 1014).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry begins part-way along line 1011, which also carries several neighbouring entries; a page number (102) is printed at line 1012; the entry ends at line 1014. It reads:

"dar.ya.kan15: Moerhingia latifolia (L) Tenzl.: Draba nemorosa: Scabiosa comosa: Lepidium apetalum: Lepidium latifolium: Astragalus tongolensis Ulbr.: [is a rare and secret medicine with boundless qualities and has been classified in various ways by different scholars. It grows between rocks and has a thin three-pointed leaf. It has a bitter yellow root and, when compounded, cures the four hundred and four kinds of disease. The leaf and root vary from pale blue, whilst the flowers may be bluish and slightly brown or pale yellow, blue or red. It has long thin pods containing small black seeds and stiff leaves and trunk. The white-flowered type has four petals, and its seed resembles lapis. In the Kalacakra Tantra it is known as kha.rag.krod. dar.ya.kan has green leaf and petiole and] dries lymph accumulations in the chest, unites skull fractures, maintains bone resin [and is beneficial in oedema]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Nearly the whole description is in the commentary layer: that it is "a rare and secret medicine with boundless qualities", the habitat, the root, the claim that "when compounded" it "cures the four hundred and four kinds of disease", the flower and seed, the Kalacakra name and "is beneficial in oedema". Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the six Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the actions "dries lymph accumulations in the chest, unites skull fractures, maintains bone resin". The commentary's "when compounded" is a statement about the drug in formulae, and no formula is named. Clark uses the name *dar.ya.kan* in other entries, as part of other drugs' names or as a type of another drug: *yu.gu.shing* (line 1019, "The brown type is said to be dar.ya.kan"), *skyi.wa* (line 1116, "also known as ngang.pa.gchig.rgyug.dar.ya.kan") and *bya.rkang* (line 1125, with the name "sngon.mo.dar.ya.kan"). These are other drugs and are not read as this one.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* In chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), line 3432 reads: "Dharyaken dries up the chhuser of the abdomen, joins fractured skulls and preserves spongy bone." The index lists "Dharyaken 207" (line 7321). Clark's "dries lymph accumulations in the chest, unites skull fractures, maintains bone resin" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "dries up the chhuser of the abdomen, joins fractured skulls and preserves spongy bone" agree on the skull and on drying, and are worded differently on the rest ("lymph accumulations in the chest" and "chhuser of the abdomen"; "bone resin" and "spongy bone"). The Codex does not treat these as one term in each pair.

*Other texts that name a Lepidium.* Dioscorides 2-205 LEPIDION (`names.csv` line 394) has the translator's gloss *Lepidium latifolium*, which is also Clark's fifth name for *dar.ya.kan*; Dioscorides is a different tradition and the match of genus is not read as the same drug. Yang 1998 prints Ting Li, "Semen Lepidii Seu Descurainiae" (line 3236, Herbs: Inferior Class), whose footnote 199 (line 3248) says "There are two kinds of Lepidium, the sweet and bitter"; the extraction resolves it only to the genus. Neither names *Lepidium apetalum*, and neither is attributed to this row.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Lepidium apetalum*, *dar.ya.kan*, *Dharyaken* and Clark line 1011 found no entry on this plant or drug. I_1233 (*Arisaema intermedium*) and I_1259 (*Corydalis melanochlora*) describe the same "D" block, including line 5217, for their own names. I_1240 (*Astragalus yunnaneansis*) mentions *Astragalus tongolensis* in this Clark entry as a different drug. I_734 (Field Pepperwort, *Lepidium campestre*; tags AT, DMM, CM) and I_111 (Garden Cress, *Lepidium sativum*; tags EP, AT, DMM, CM, SBJ, SS) are other *Lepidium* species from other traditions. I_644 (Hedge Mustard) carries a note on *Ting Li Zi* naming *Lepidium apetalum* with *Descurainia sophia*; it is a Chinese-tradition note and is not the Tibetan drug. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. Clark's commentary records "a bitter yellow root". It is a general fact that *Lepidium* is a genus of the mustard family (Brassicaceae) and that glucosinolates are characteristic of the family. Which part was used is not said, and with six candidate plants no general statement about the Tibetan drug's constituents is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "dries lymph accumulations in the chest, unites skull fractures, maintains bone resin" (Clark, line 1014) and "dries up the chhuser of the abdomen, joins fractured skulls and preserves spongy bone" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3432). The Clark commentary adds that it "cures the four hundred and four kinds of disease" when compounded; that is the commentary's own claim of range and is not a Codex protocol. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and does not translate the Tibetan terms into modern diagnoses. Fractures need medical care.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary (line 1011) prints "a bitter yellow root"; no power or post-digestive taste is printed. Men-Tsee-Khang prints no taste or power for "Dharyaken" in the lines read. ### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it dries lymph accumulations, unites skull fractures and maintains bone resin, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text gives a parallel set of actions. Clark's commentary calls it "a rare and secret medicine with boundless qualities", which is a claim about the drug's range and rarity in the commentary layer, and says "when compounded" it cures "the four hundred and four kinds of disease"; neither phrase is a cultivation status. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes, and not in the rejuvenation material; no line read places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name, not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a drug for lymph accumulations and fractured skulls in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and the plant behind the Tibetan name is uncertain among six. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Fractures and chest disease need medical assessment; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the identity is uncertain, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
