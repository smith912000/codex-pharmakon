# Daphne gnidium — Knidelaion (Dioscorides 1-43), with Thumelaia (4-173) and Oinos thumelaites (5-78) as related chapters

## *Daphne gnidium (the translators' third-listed name on the 1-43 SUGGESTED line, where it stands with "Gnidium, Cnidium" and "Thymelaea hirsuta"; "Oil from Grains, Seeds of Gnidium, Spurge Flax")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the 1-43 chapter id (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The SUGGESTED line (lines 3898 and 3899) reads "Gnidium, Cnidium, Daphne gnidium, Thymelaea hirsuta — Oil from Grains, Seeds of Gnidium," and then "Spurge Flax", followed by the cross-reference "see 4-173" (line 3900). Note that this chapter is about an oil prepared from the seeds, so the plant itself is not described in it.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *1-43. KNIDELAION*, heading at line 3897, text at line 3901. It stands in Book One among the oils, after the *hyosciaminum* chapter.

The whole chapter reads: "Gnidium is prepared in the same way from rubbings of grains [seeds] that have been pounded and pilled. Taken as a drink it is able to loosen the bowels."

"In the same way" refers back to the preceding oil chapters. The sentence just before the heading, in the *hyosciaminum* chapter, describes steeping pounded dry seed in hot water as "previously described in amagdalinum [1-39]", sunning and mixing it until it turns black and strong-smelling, straining it through linen and squeezing it. The preparation is therefore a pounded-seed oil of the type described in 1-39, but the full method for *knidelaion* is not given in 1-43 itself, and 1-39 was not read for this entry. [USER: verify] The chapter after it, 1-44 *KNIKELAION*, makes *cnicinum* "in the same way" from safflower and says it is "somewhat weaker"; it is a different plant, not read here.

**Related chapters already on the spine.** The cross-reference "see 4-173" leads to 4-173 *THUMELAIA* (heading at line 9612), whose SUGGESTED line is "Thymelaea hirsuta, Daphne gnidium — Gnidium, Spurge Flax, Flax-leaved Daphne" and whose text ends by saying that "cnidium grain — which is the seed — is gathered", and that some people mistakenly take it for the fruit of *chamelaea*. The same shrub appears in 5-78 *OINOS THUMELAITES* (heading at line 10186), a wine made from its branches and fruit, with the same two binomials. Both chapters belong to the existing entry I_987 (*Thymelaea hirsuta*), which also reads 4-173 and 5-78; this entry does not repeat that reading and is kept apart from it by the spine's own rows (CPU-01499 here, I_987 there). The Codex does not merge them. [USER: verify] The translators' index lists *Daphne gnidium* at pages 42, 728 and 778; page 42 is the page of 1-43 (the page marker "42" falls after 1-44), 728 that of 4-173, and 778 that of 5-78.

### Active Components

The constituents of the oil or the plant were not read. In general terms, plants of the daphne and thymelaea group, the Thymelaeaceae, are widely known as irritant and purgative, but no composition is asserted here for the plant Dioscorides names. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. In 1-43 Dioscorides records a seed oil, drunk "to loosen the bowels", and nothing else. The Codex gives no protocol. The plant behind the oil is, on the translators' suggestion, a purgative and irritant shrub (see I_987 for the related chapters, where Dioscorides himself says the grain "burns a rough throat"), and no purgative from this plant should be used.

### Preparation & Dosing

1-43 gives one preparation: the oil "prepared in the same way from rubbings of grains [seeds] that have been pounded and pilled", "taken as a drink". The method of the preceding oils is only referred to. No quantity, frequency or duration is given in 1-43. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] (Chapter 4-173 does give "twenty of the grains" for a decoction of the shrub's inner part, but that is a different preparation in a different chapter and is not carried over to the oil.)

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality, and no taste. Its one stated action is that the drink "is able to loosen the bowels".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter is two sentences: an oil made as other oils are made, and a purgative effect. It gives no site, no season of gathering, no description of the plant, no test of quality and no status. The identification is the translators'. Of the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec credits to this tradition, this chapter offers only its cross-reference, which tells the reader to look at 4-173 for the shrub itself.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here gives a cultivator a practice. It is a single purgative oil recorded in two sentences, so it is history and not a practice. The only useful move is to follow the cross-reference to the plant chapter and read how Dioscorides warns of its burning effect.

### Contraindications

A purgative drink from a plant described in a related chapter as burning the throat is not something to prepare or take. Bowel complaints need medical care. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion shared among three chapters, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
