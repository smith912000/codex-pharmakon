# Talc — Hua Shi, "Talcum" (Yang 1998) / Abhraka, "Talc" (Bhishagratna, Appendix 3)
## *Talc, the soft magnesium silicate mineral; Yang's pharmaceutical Latin is "Talcum", and Bhishagratna's Appendix 3 pairs the Sanskrit name Abhraka with "Talc"; no Greek chapter on talc was found* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** No Dioscorides chapter on talc was found, and the word "talc" does not occur in the Greek text on disk (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). The spine records this as "DMM UNVERIFIED: no chapter heading match, but the full text mentions a possible referent; kept". The nearest candidate in the book of metallic stones is 5-145 SCHISTOS LITHOS, glossed "Crystalline Laminated Rock" (lines 10590-10592; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 918; `_sources/dioscorides.csv` flags it id_uncertain): "Schistos stone is found in Spain towards the west. That which looks like saffron in colour seems to be the best — brittle, easy to clean, compacted like amoniacum salt, with similar veins. It has the same uses as haematite only with less strength. Diluted with women's milk it fills up hollow sores, and it is very effective for hernia, prolapse of the eyes, thickness of the eyelids, and staphylomata [protrusion of the cornea]." Nothing in the chapter ties it to talc, and the edition does not say so. Two other soft stones in the same book are glossed otherwise: 5-134 GUPSOS, "Gypsum — Plaster of Paris" (line 10533: "astringent, closes pores, and restrains bleeding and sweating"), and 5-159 SELENITES LITHOS, "Selenite — Moonstone — crystalline Lime Sulphate" (lines 10656-10658). The identification of any of these with talc is the project's guess, not the text's. [USER: verify]

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`) has Hua Shi, 滑石, "Talcum", under the printed heading "Jades and Stones: Superior Class" (line 985, PDF p.25), lines 992-997: "Hua Shi (Talcum) is sweet and cold. It mainly treats generalized fever, afflux diarrhea, difficult lactation in women, and dribbling urinary block. It disinhibits urination, flushes accumulations and gatherings in the stomach [with] cold and heat, and boosts the essential qi. Protracted taking may make the body light and free from hunger and it may prolong life. It is produced in the mountains and valleys in Zhe Yang." Yang's footnote 42 adds that talcum "is also able to clear summerheat and both internal and external heat, quench vexatious thirst, and cure fulminant diarrhea, dysentery with pressure on the rectum, and vaginal discharge" (lines 1012-1014), and footnote 43 says Zhe Yang was an ancient county in what is now Shandong (line 1016). Talcum is the example Yang uses in his note on flavour and qi: "Its flavor is sweet, while its qi is cold" (lines 851-852). The row is in `_sources/shennong.csv` (line 12) and `extract/names.csv` (which cites text line 992).

### Active Components

Talc is hydrated magnesium silicate, the softest common mineral (it can be scratched with a fingernail). It is chemically inert when swallowed and is not absorbed. Natural talc deposits can be contaminated with other minerals, asbestos among them, and inhaled talc dust causes lung disease.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Chinese text has talc as a cooling, urine-promoting stone in the upper class of its three; the Greek and Sanskrit attestations are unverified. Powdered talc has an established modern use as a dusting and lubricating powder, not as a food or a long-term supplement. The Codex gives no protocol, dose or preparation.

### Preparation & Dosing

SBJ: "Protracted taking" is named with no preparation method and no quantity (line 996). No preparation is given in the Greek or Sanskrit lines read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Talc is a magnesium silicate; swallowed, it passes through the gut without absorption. It has no recognised effect on fever, diarrhoea, urination or lactation. Inhalation is the hazard: talc dust can cause lung inflammation and scarring, and talc products contaminated with asbestos are a recognised concern. No clinical studies were retrieved for the traditional uses. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* No talc chapter was found, and the candidate schistos chapter is a remedy list for sores and eye complaints. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. [USER: verify]

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent, and the attestation rests on a spine row.* No Avicenna text or list row was found. Innate heat and radical moisture cannot be tied to talc without a source and are not tied to it here. [USER: verify]

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: superior class.* Hua Shi stands under the printed heading "Jades and Stones: Superior Class" (Yang 1998, line 985). Yang's rendering of the preface says superior class medicinals "mainly nourish life ... are nontoxic and taking them in large amounts and for a long time will not harm people. If one intends to make one's body light, boost the qi, prevent aging, and prolong life, one should base [one's efforts] on the superior class" (lines 352-355 and 383-384), and Yang's own gloss is that these are "nontoxic medicinals that are able to nurture life and therefore bestow longevity" (lines 356-358). The entry's own line says that "Protracted taking may make the body light and free from hunger and it may prolong life" (lines 995-996); "make the body light" is the *qing shen* phrase and is recorded as the text's, not as a pharmacological effect, and "free from hunger" is read by Yang's note on the first jade entry as the Daoist belief in living long without eating (lines 859-862). The same line says it "boosts the essential qi", which is the text's wording and is given without any gloss. Yang ties the superior class to Daoists seeking elixirs of immortality (note, lines 374-379). That is the cultivation framing the text supports; the "nontoxic" claim is the text's and is not accepted here (see Contraindications).

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(c) Absent, and the attestation rests on a name-list row.* *Abhraka* is only a word in Bhishagratna's Appendix 3 in the lines read, with no text locus. No *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* claim is printed for talc. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** The Chinese text places talc in the grade of substances taken long-term to nourish life, and that is a real fact about the tradition, but nothing sourced here supports swallowing talc for cultivation, and the text's own claim of harmlessness is not borne out by what is known of inhaled talc. A cultivator can take from the sources the idea of a long-term, nontoxic regimen, and then ask whether this mineral is a sound member of it; on the evidence in the file, it is not recommended. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Talc dust must not be inhaled; breathing it causes lung disease (talcosis). Talc from natural sources may be contaminated with asbestos; cosmetic and pharmaceutical grades are controlled for this and other grades are not. Swallowing talc has no sourced benefit. Do not give powdered talc to infants or use it on broken skin. A "long-term nourishing" claim rests on the Chinese grade above and has no safety record in these sources.
