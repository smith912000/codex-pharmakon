# ANCHOUSA ETERA — Anchousa etera, "another anchusa" (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 4-24 and 4-25)

## *Two chapters headed "another anchusa". Identification: a plant of the alkanet and bugloss group (Boraginaceae), species unresolved; the translator's gloss for both chapters is "see 4-23, 4-128", whose candidates are Anchusa italica and Alkanna tinctoria (4-23) and Borago officinalis or Anchusa italica (4-128); confidence low* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **CAUTION.** The chapters say that the plant, chewed and spat into the mouth of a snake, kills it, and that the root is taken against broadworms. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should take this plant.

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk. This is a single-text entry.** The headings were searched in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`: 4-24 at line 8228 and 4-25 at line 8231, both headed "ANCHOUSA ETERA". They follow chapter 4-23 ANCHOUSA (line 8215) and precede 4-26 LUKOPSIS.

**Dioscorides (DMM), 4-24.** "Anchusa altera differs from the above in having smaller leaves yet equally sharp. There are thin little branches, with flowers of a purple colour drawing towards a Phoenician [red]. The roots are red and very long. Around harvest time they have something similar to blood in them. It grows in sandy places. The root and leaves are able to help those bitten by venomous creatures — especially the viper-bitten — eaten, taken as a drink, or hanged about one. Chewed and spat out into the mouth of a venomous beast, it will kill him. It is also called alcibiadian, or onocheiles." The translation prints the aliases as "alcibiadian" and "onocheiles".

**Dioscorides (DMM), 4-25.** "There is also another similar to the above, but with a smaller seed of a Phoenician [red] colour. Chewed and spat out into the mouth of a snake, it will kill him. An acetabulum [vinegar cruet] of a decoction the root (taken as a drink with hyssop [3-30] and nasturtium [2-185]) draws out broadworms."

**Cross-references.** The translator's gloss is "see 4-23, 4-128" for 4-24 and "see 4-128" for 4-25. Chapter 4-23 (line 8215) is the first anchusa: a rough, prickly-leaved plant whose root is "the colour almost of blood", which in summer is astringent, "dyeing the hands", used for burns, old ulcers, erysipelas and leprosy, as a pessary an abortifacient, and in decoction for jaundice and the spleen (lines 8215 to 8221). Chapter 4-128 BOUGLOSSON (line 9162) is a different plant: bugloss gathered in July, good for the chills of fevers, and "put into wine ... thought to be a cause of mirth". The two ETERA chapters share the red root with 4-23, and the red root is the text's own clue; the species is not decided here. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The chapters name no constituent. In general terms, the red root colour of the alkanet group is due to red pigments of the root, and many plants of the borage family (Boraginaceae) contain pyrrolizidine alkaloids, which are liver-toxic. Whether these apply to the plant of 4-24 and 4-25 was not checked. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Venomous bites, especially viper bites (4-24), and the drawing out of broadworms (4-25). The snake-killing statement is a claim about the plant, not a use for a person.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose for 4-24; for 4-25 one measure (below); no duration.

The Codex gives no protocol and does not recommend this plant.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-24: the root and leaves "eaten, taken as a drink, or hanged about one" (the last is an amuletic use, recorded and not endorsed). 4-25: "An acetabulum of a decoction the root (taken as a drink with hyssop and nasturtium)" for broadworms. The acetabulum is the only quantity given in the two chapters, and it is a Roman measure whose modern equivalent is not given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use it as a dose.** No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. No herb is a reliable treatment for snakebite, and venomous bites need emergency medical care. The borage family includes several plants with liver-toxic alkaloids. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** Dioscorides prints no hot, cold, dry or moist quality for the plants of 4-24 and 4-25. The only quality printed in the neighbouring chapter 4-23 is astringent, for the root in summer.

### Cultivation Register

**Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and these chapters claim none. They do show the discipline of attention that he offers: the plant is told apart from the first anchusa by leaf size, flower colour, root colour, seed size and habitat (sandy places), and the root is said to look blood-like "around harvest time". The amuletic use ("hanged about one") is recorded and not endorsed.

**Practitioner's note.** A red-rooted plant of the bugloss group used in antiquity for snakebite and broadworms. Nothing here supports any cultivation use, and the identity is unsettled. A cultivator should not use it.

### Contraindications

- **Snakebite.** An emergency; seek care. Do not rely on this plant.
- **Possible liver toxicity.** The family includes pyrrolizidine-alkaloid plants; avoid ingestion. [USER: verify for this species]
- **Pregnancy.** The first anchusa is described as an abortifacient (4-23); avoid. No statement for 4-24 or 4-25. [USER: verify]
- **Identity.** Species unresolved; do not substitute any bugloss.
