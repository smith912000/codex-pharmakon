# Rabbit droppings — rGyud-bzhi, "Rabbit droppings" (droppings of the rabbit, listed under "Droppings")
## *Droppings of the rabbit; the translator prints only "Rabbit droppings", with no Tibetan name and no binomial in the line read* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Rabbit droppings are listed in the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, the chapter on the efficacy of medicinal substances, in Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). They fall in section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), whose thirteen headings are "horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Under "Droppings" (heading line 1198) the long line 1199 reads, for the relevant clause: "Rabbit droppings: purge oedema [and are beneficial for wounds]." The clause gives no Tibetan name and no binomial; "[and are beneficial for wounds]" is in square brackets, which are Clark's interpolation: he says in his introduction (line 80) that he has "interpolated liberally from the Blue Aquamarine commentary" and relied on oral teachings, but the lines read do not state the bracket convention outright. [USER: verify]

The neighbours in the same line are: "Vulture's droppings: increase digestive heat, disperse tumors and suppurate swellings. Pig's stool: cures indigestion, septic disorders, infectious fevers and bile tumors. Human excreta: cure gall stones, neutralize poisons, reduce swellings [and subdue spirits]. Horse dung: is anthelmintic, anti-emetic, cures wind-bile combinations [and is beneficial against spirits]. Rabbit droppings: purge oedema [and are beneficial for wounds]. Dog stool: [is especially beneficial against spirits]. Wolf stool and droppings of Gong.mo ... [all three] reduce swellings. Bird and Rat droppings: both drain pus [accumulations]. phug.ron: Pigeon droppings ... suppurate swellings."

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3568) gives the same list with a different wording: "Rabbit dropping drain ascites." Clark therefore reads "purge oedema" where Men-Tsee-Khang reads "drain ascites" (the accumulation of fluid in the abdomen), and Men-Tsee-Khang does not carry the bracketed "beneficial for wounds". The Tibetan wording is not in the files read, so which rendering is closer, and whether "oedema" and "ascites" render one Tibetan term, cannot be said. [USER: verify]

The rabbit is also named in the Tantra's meat classification, among the "Game animals" (Clark, line 672; Men-Tsee-Khang, line 2907), and Clark's text says "Rabbit meat is coarse, increases digestive heat and stops diarrhoea" (line 679). That concerns the flesh and is not applied to the droppings. The Men-Tsee-Khang "Glossary on Animals" (running heads at lines 6808-7114) prints "Rabbit" with a garbled binomial ("Lepus iostclus Hodgson", lines 7104-7108); the lines read do not settle the species. ### Active Components

Rabbit droppings are the dry pellets of an herbivore: undigested plant fibre (cellulose and lignin), water, salts, bile pigments and gut bacteria. Rabbits also pass a softer, nutrient-rich form (caecotrophs) that they re-eat; the dry pellets are the ones usually seen. No active principle is claimed by the sources, and no composition specific to this entry was read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is one action in a materia medica list: rabbit droppings "purge oedema" (Clark) or "drain ascites" (Men-Tsee-Khang). The Codex offers no protocol. Swelling or fluid in the abdomen can signal heart, liver, kidney or other serious disease and needs clinical assessment, and animal droppings carry infection risk (see Contraindications). The Tantra gives no preparation, dose or route in these lines. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Oedema and ascites are fluid accumulations with many causes, including heart, liver and kidney disease, and are treated by finding and treating the cause and, where appropriate, with diuretics or drainage by a clinician. Animal faeces can carry *Salmonella*, *Escherichia coli* and parasites, and eating or taking them as a drink is a route of infection. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* Neither translation read assigns rabbit droppings any cultivation framing. They are a short action in a list of animal droppings. The frame around the list is the tradition's own: the project's cultivation spec records that the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) are derived from the three poisons and that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root. That point comes from the spec and not from any line about rabbit droppings. No source read attaches rabbit droppings to *bcud len* (essence extraction), and there is no formal Tibetan category of substances for subtle-body practice. Clark's remark (line 57) that "only very few animal products are used" nowadays is a note on usage, not on status.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a one-line action in a list of animal droppings, the two translations word it differently, and it carries infection risk with no supported benefit. The honest use of the entry is as a record of what the Tantra says.

### Contraindications

- **Infection:** animal faeces can carry bacteria and parasites; do not eat them, drink them or put them on wounds.
- **Not a treatment for swelling or ascites:** fluid in the abdomen or legs needs clinical assessment; do not rely on this line.
- **Wild and sick animals:** wild rabbits and hares can carry tularemia and other diseases; avoid handling their droppings with bare hands. [USER: verify]
- **Legal and conservation status:** collecting droppings does not require killing the animal, but hunting or trapping wild rabbits is regulated in many places. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** the droppings themselves do not require killing; the infection risk remains.
