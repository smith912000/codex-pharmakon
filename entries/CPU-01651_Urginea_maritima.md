### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM) under this name.** The name indexes in `Sources/*/extract/names.csv` and the list files in `_sources/` list no other text under the name *Urginea maritima* or *Skilla*. Two list rows give the same species under another binomial: `_sources/avicenna.csv`, line 22 ("Squill, Drimia maritima (L.) Stearn", Canon II) and `_sources/ebers.csv`, line 26 ("Sea squill, Drimia maritima"). Those rows have their own entry, I_222 (*Squill*, tagged DMM and CM); see the Overlap paragraph. The Codex does not read the two binomials as settled to be one row, and this entry takes only what the Dioscorides chapter says. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *2-202. SKILLA* at line 6314. Directly under the heading stands a plate caption, "Urginea maritima after FAGUET — 1888" (line 6316), and the page number 336 (line 6317). The SUGGESTED line is at lines 6319 and 6320, the printed label "VERY POISONOUS" at line 6321, and the text at line 6322. The text is cut by two page breaks (pages 337 and 338, lines 6327 and 6331) and by two plate captions that belong to other chapters ("Bulbus sylvestris, Gagea lutea from FUCHS — 1545" at lines 6325 and 6326, and "Ornithogalum umbellatum after FAGUET — 1888" at line 6330). It resumes at line 6334 ("shade and the pieces thrust through ...") and ends at line 6335. The chapter stands in Book Two, in the section "Herbs with a Sharp Quality" (from page 304; line 31), after *2-201. BOLBOS EMETIKOS* (line 6309) and before *2-203. PANICRATION* (line 6336). The name index lists the chapter at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 391, where the identification column holds the plate caption and not the SUGGESTED line. The translators' indexes print "Urginea maritima 336" (lines 11938, 11955, 18333), with further pages 754 to 757 for the two Book Five chapters named below.

The chapter is built around the preparation of the bulb, and it has no botanical description at all. It opens: "Scilla is sharp and burning but it is roasted and made useful for man's purposes." It then gives three ways of roasting. The bulb is wrapped in dough or clay and put into an oven or under hot coals "until the dough that enfolds it is sufficiently baked"; if the squill within is not tender, it is baked again in fresh dough or clay, "for that which is not thus roasted is hurtful if given, especially if it is (taken inwardly) carried to the bowels". It may be baked in a "tightly-corked ceramic jar" in an oven, when "the very middle part is taken, the part around the outside of it being thrown away". It is then cut into pieces and boiled, "the first water thrown away and fresh water poured on it, until the water becomes neither bitter nor sharp". The pieces are also dried in the shade, threaded on linen so that they do not touch, and "these pieces we use to manufacture oil, wine, and vinegar of squills".

The uses follow (line 6335). Externally: "For cracks in the feet the inner part of the raw squill is applied (either boiled in oil or else dissolved with rosin)"; boiled with vinegar it is a plaster "for those bitten by vipers"; roasted squill rubbed on "is good for hanging warts and chilblains". Internally: eight parts of roasted salt to one part of roasted squill, one or two spoonfuls given to those fasting "for softening the bowels"; put into liquid and aromatic medicines it is good "for those in whom we wish to induce movement of urine, for dropsy, a stomach in which the meat swims above, jaundice, griping, those troubled with a cough for a long time, the asthmatic, and those who spit up (blood)"; boiled with honey and eaten for the same purposes, it "very much facilitates mixtures" and "draws out the slimy stuff that sticks in the bowels". The seed, pounded and eaten with a dried fig or honey, "softens the bowels". One caution is stated: boiled squill taken in the same way "must be avoided by those who have an inward ulceration". The last sentence records a use of another kind: "It is also an antidote against poison hanged up whole before doors." The chapter does not say what the hanging does, or whether it is a drug use or an amuletic one, and it does not comment on it. It is recorded here and not endorsed.

**Related Book Five chapters (labelled cross-reference, brief).** The chapter's own sentence about oil, wine and vinegar of squills points to two later chapters whose SUGGESTED lines print the same binomials, both labelled "POISONOUS": *5-25. ### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is a general, well-established fact that the sea squill bulb contains cardiac glycosides of the bufadienolide type (the scillarens) and that these are the reason it is a poison, and the reason for the label "VERY POISONOUS" attached to the chapter. The text itself says only that the raw bulb is "sharp and burning" and "hurtful" unroasted. Nothing else is stated here about the composition of the translators' sea onion. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a poisonous bulb that is roasted, boiled or dried to make it usable, and the uses it lists (dropsy, urinary flow, cough, asthma, jaundice, the bowels, warts and chilblains, viper bites) are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex does not recommend squill in any form. The identity of the plant is only the translators' suggestion, the preparation is a matter of judgement by the maker, and the plant is a heart poison. No protocol is given.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives several preparations with locus. Roasting: in dough or clay, in an oven or under hot coals until the dough is baked, and repeated if the bulb is not tender; or in a corked ceramic jar in an oven, using only the middle part (line 6322). Boiling: cut into pieces, with the first water thrown away and fresh poured on until the water "becomes neither bitter nor sharp" (line 6322). Drying: in the shade on a thread (lines 6322 and 6334). Two doses are given (line 6335): "eight parts of roasted salt (pounded into small pieces) to one part of roasted squill we give one spoonful or two of it to those fasting", and "Thirty grains (taken as a syrup with honey) is sufficient". The chapter does not say which of the uses the thirty grains is for; the sentence stands after the list of urinary, dropsy and chest uses. No dose is given in the sources retrieved for the external uses, the boiled and honeyed squill, or the seed. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that squill is toxic and acts on the heart in the manner of digitalis-type glycosides, and that it has been used in the past as an expectorant, diuretic and rat poison. This is a statement about the squill plant in general and is not a verification of the chapter's claims or of its preparations.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 2-202 prints that the raw squill "is sharp and burning" (a taste and a heating action, as printed). It prints no moist or dry quality. The printed label is "VERY POISONOUS".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is unusually full, though it is a discipline of preparation and not of recognition: it gives no description of the plant for identification. It gives a sequence of tests on a dangerous material (is the bulb within tender, bake it again if not; use only the middle part; throw the first water away; boil until the water is neither bitter nor sharp), a statement of what goes wrong if the step is skipped ("hurtful ... especially ... carried to the bowels"), and a single stated caution for a class of patient (inward ulceration). The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The hanging of the whole bulb before doors as an "antidote against poison" is recorded without comment, and recording is not endorsing. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant, and the plant is a poison that the chapter itself hedges with a long preparation. The transferable point is the discipline of the assay: test, repeat, discard what is not safe, and stop when the water runs neither bitter nor sharp. That is a point about method. It is not a licence to prepare squill, which the Codex does not endorse. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 2-202 states that raw or under-roasted squill "is hurtful", especially if it reaches the bowels, and that the boiled squill "must be avoided by those who have an inward ulceration". The chapter is labelled "VERY POISONOUS" in the translation. Squill is a heart poison, and it can cause dangerous heart rhythm disturbances and vomiting; the Codex gives no dose for any person and does not advise using it. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no bulb should be gathered or prepared on the strength of this entry. Viper bites and dropsy are medical emergencies or conditions needing diagnosis, and a herb should not delay care. The chapter gives two doses and no safety information for pregnancy, children, or any medicine taken at the same time (people taking heart drugs, in particular). [USER: verify]
