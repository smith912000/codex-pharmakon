# Dodder — Tù Sī Zǐ (菟丝子), Chinese Dodder Seed

### Origin & Tradition

**Chinese materia medica (SBJ lineage):** *Tù Sī Zǐ* — the small brown-to-grey seed of the parasitic dodder vine — is one of the classic "seed" tonics of Chinese herbal medicine. It is grouped with the Kidney-Yang and essence (Jing) supplementing agents, yet it is prized precisely because it is regarded as balanced rather than hot: it is said to "tonify Yang without scattering, and secure the essence without being cloying." Traditional indications cluster around the Kidney's functions in classical physiology: seminal emission and impotence, frequent or dribbling urination, sore and weak lower back and knees, threatened-miscarriage patterns of Kidney-Liver deficiency, and dim vision attributed to the Liver and Kidney failing to nourish the eyes. It is almost always used in formula rather than alone, frequently paired with other seed tonics.

**Botanical peculiarity:** *Cuscuta* is a leafless, near-rootless obligate parasite — a filament of yellow-orange thread that twines onto a host plant and draws nutrition through haustoria. This biology is not incidental to its symbolic reading (below): a plant that lives by "borrowing" a host's vitality became, in the doctrinal imagination, a plant that helps the body borrow back and secure its own reserves.

**Tibetan sphere (FMT):** dodder seed is used within the broader Himalayan/Tibetan medical trade as a kidney and reproductive-seed tonic, consistent with its Chinese role. The naming and precise indications vary by lineage; treat cross-tradition detail as `[USER: verify]` for any specific classical citation.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Borrowed Thread:** Dodder is the plant that cannot stand alone; it is all connection and no root. In the Kenetica reading this is not weakness but a doctrine of *securing what flows*. The classical claim that dodder "secures the essence" maps onto a real pharmacological theme — its flavonoids act on bone and reproductive-endocrine signalling (see Scientific Correlation) — but the honest framing is that the symbol runs ahead of the proven mechanism. The thread that binds to a host is the emblem of a tonic that binds scattered reserves back to their store.

### Active Components

- **Flavonoids** — quercetin, hyperoside (hyperin), and related glycosides; the dominant characterised actives. HPLC of a *C. chinensis* extract identified **chlorogenic acid, quercetin, and hyperin** as major constituents (Mo et al., 2019).
- **Chlorogenic acid** and other phenolic acids — antioxidant phenolics.
- **Lignans and polysaccharides** — reported constituents contributing to antioxidant and tonic activity.
- Documented preclinical actions: modulation of bone-remodelling signalling (RANKL/OPG), antioxidant activity, and traditional reproductive-tonic effects (clinical human data are limited — see grading below).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

- **`[BUILD]`** — dodder is positioned as a slow reconstitution agent for the "deep store": lower-back and knee weakness, depleted drive, and the recovery of reproductive-endocrine tone after prolonged output. It is a background tonic, not an acute performance agent.
- **`[RESET]`** — its neutral-to-mildly-warm, non-drying profile makes it a candidate for restoration blocks where hotter Yang tonics would over-stimulate. ### Scientific Correlation

Evidence grade: **preclinical only** (animal and in-vitro); no robust human clinical trial base retrieved. According to PubMed:

- **Anti-osteoporotic / bone-protective (rat model).** *Cuscuta chinensis* extract (100 mg·kg⁻¹·day⁻¹) protected against glucocorticoid (dexamethasone)-induced osteoporosis in Sprague-Dawley rats — increasing bone mineral density and suppressing osteoclastogenesis, with effects attributed to modulation of the RANKL/OPG and RunX2 pathways; HPLC identified chlorogenic acid, quercetin, and hyperin as major constituents (Mo et al., 2019, *Braz J Med Biol Res*). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1590/1414-431X20198754)

This is consistent with the traditional use for weak lower back/knees and the "essence-securing" framing, but it is a mechanistic animal study, not proof of a human clinical effect. Reproductive-tonic claims remain traditional/preclinical and should not be stated as demonstrated human outcomes.

Attribution: findings from PubMed.

### Cultivation Register

**Shennong / classical Chinese (SBJ).** *Tù Sī Zǐ* 菟絲子 is attested by name in the *Shennong Bencaojing* and is placed in the **Superior Class** (上品) — the *san pin* grade of medicinals held to be non-toxic, taken over long periods, and directed at *nourishing life* (養命) rather than attacking disease. The classic's own text reads: it "mainly mends expiry and damage, supplements insufficiency, boosts the qi and [physical] strength, and makes one fat and strong. Protracted taking may brighten the eyes, make the body light, and prolong life" (Yang Shou-zhong, trans., *The Divine Farmer's Materia Medica Classic*, Blue Poppy Press, 1998, Herbs: Superior Class, p. 31). Two of those marks are cultivation vocabulary in the strict sense and must be left untranslated: 久服 *jiu fu*, "protracted taking", is the grammatical signature of an upper-grade drug, and 輕身 *qing shen*, "make the body light", is an effect-category with **no pharmacological equivalent** — it is not weight reduction and must never be rendered as one. This is therefore a genuine *yang sheng* substance in the classical scheme: conservational, cumulative, taken to husband a reserve rather than to expel a pathogen. **One sub-claim must be graded lower.** The classic text itself makes *no* essence claim for dodder. The familiar modern formulation — "benefits Jing", 填精 — belongs to the later *bencao* and standard-textbook stratum, and in the Yang translation the essence-and-marrow language ("replenish the essence, boost the marrow") appears in the *commentarial footnote*, not in the Han-era text. Given that *jing* 精 has neither a chapter nor a formal category in the materia medica at all, that claim should not be back-dated to the classic. Cultivation status: **(a) attested** — upper grade 上品, *jiu fu* and *qing shen* both in the base text (Yang Shou-zhong, trans., *The Divine Farmer's Materia Medica Classic*, Blue Poppy Press, 1998, p. 31); the *jing*-supplementing reading is post-classical and stands at **(b)** for the *Bencaojing* stratum.

**rGyud-bzhi / Tibetan (FMT).** No Tibetan cultivation status is attested for *Cuscuta chinensis*, and on the sources retrieved no secure Tibetan attestation of the species is available at all. Wangyal et al.'s dataset of Sowa Rigpa materia medica — compiled from the *Four Medical Treatises*, the *Crystal Orb and Rosary*, and Gawé Dorjé's *Stainless Crystal Mirror of Materia Medica*, covering roughly 700 of the most commonly used substances — records no *Cuscuta* (Wangyal, Tidwell, Dhondrup, Yungdrung, Dhondrup, He & Zhang, *Data in Brief* 33: 106498, 2020). The entry's own body already marks the Himalayan kidney-and-seed use `[USER: verify]`, and that marker is upheld here rather than laundered: a trade-route presence in the Himalayan sphere is not a *rGyud-bzhi* assignment. Two further disciplines apply. First, the Four Tantras contain **no formal category of "substances for subtle-body practice"**, so there is no slot into which a cultivation grade could be dropped even if the species were attested. Second, *bcud len* (essence extraction) is a real practice genuinely located inside the Four Tantras (Gerke, "'Treating the Aged' and 'Maintaining Health': Locating *bcud len* Practices in the Four Medical Tantras"), but it may only be claimed for a species a source actually places there, and none does so for dodder. Within Sowa Rigpa the framing that would apply is the three *nyes pa* (*rlung* / *mkhris pa* / *bad kan*), rooted in the three poisons, with medicine as the third of four treatment branches — support for practice, not a route to its root. Nothing attaches that framing to this seed. Cultivation status: **(c) absent.**

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does have a use for dodder seed, and the Chinese tradition is unusually explicit about the shape of it. This is an upper-grade drug, which is a statement about *duration and intent* before it is a statement about effect: it belongs to background, protracted taking (*jiu fu*) over weeks and seasons inside a formula, not to single doses and not to acute work. That matches the entry's own `[BUILD]` / `[RESET]` placement — a store-securing agent, taken in restoration blocks precisely because it is neutral-to-mildly-warm and does not force. What the cultivator should *not* do is import the tidy modern sentence "it tonifies Jing" back into the classic and treat the classic's authority as covering it; the Han text says the body becomes light and life is prolonged, and it says nothing about essence. Nor should 輕身 be quietly converted into a physical claim about the body's mass — that conversion is exactly the concordism this book exists to refuse. On the Tibetan side the honest instruction is: do nothing, because there is nothing here to act on. The seed's biology invites a doctrine-of-signatures reading — the rootless thread that borrows and binds — and that reading is a **cultivator's overlay**, not a *bencao* category; it may be held as an image, but it carries no attestation and no grade.

### Contraindications

- **Excess-heat and Yin-deficiency-with-heat patterns:** the tonifying, mildly warming action is inappropriate where there is marked heat, constipation from dryness, or blood-in-urine.
- **Pregnancy:** although traditionally used in certain fetus-calming formulas, self-directed use in pregnancy is not advised without qualified practitioner supervision.
- **Interactions:** flavonoid-rich extracts (quercetin) may theoretically affect drug-metabolising enzymes; caution with narrow-therapeutic-index medications. No well-characterised human interaction data.
- **General:** human safety and efficacy data are limited; the evidence base is preclinical.
