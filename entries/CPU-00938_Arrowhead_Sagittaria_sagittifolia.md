# Arrowhead — Cí Gū (慈姑, "the compassionate aunt") / Jiǎn Dāo Cǎo-adjacent water-herb / Sagitta ("the arrow") / *Sahm al-māʾ*-class water plants of the Arabic materia medica

## *Sagittaria sagittifolia* L. | Family: Alismataceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM, SBJ
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and moist (2nd degree in the Galenic reading of the aquatic, mucilaginous, cooling class); refrigerant, emollient, resolvent when applied; nutritive when cooked
**Training Phase:** Phase I — Grounding & Nourishment (the cooked corm as a cooling, nutritive base) / Phase II — Clearing (the leaf as a cooling topical)

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Sagittaria sagittifolia* is a plant the traditions recognised **by its shape before they recognised it by its action**. Every name it carries in the classical record is a name for an arrowhead: Latin *sagitta*, the arrow; the Greek and Arabic descriptions of the sharp, barbed, triangular water-leaf. It is a food plant first and a medicine second, and this entry says so.

**Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* catalogues the marsh and water plants — the class of cold, moist, mucilaginous herbs of standing water — and treats them as **refrigerants and emollients**: bruised leaves laid on hot inflammations, burns, erysipelas and angry swellings, on the straightforward Galenic logic that a cold, wet plant answers a hot, dry lesion. The arrowhead-type water plants sit in this slot. The Codex records the attestation at the level of the **cold-moist aquatic refrigerant class** and its topical use, not as a precisely identified single chapter on *S. sagittifolia*; identification of Dioscoridean water-plant names with modern binomials is genuinely uncertain, and inventing a chapter reference would be worse than admitting the limit. **Category: refrigerant / emollient topical.**

**Avicenna / Unani (CM).** The Arabic-Persian tradition's category for such plants is **bārid raṭb** — **cold and moist** — the class of *mubarrid* (refrigerant) and *muraṭṭib* (moistening) drugs given to quench heat and to soften what is hardened by dryness. Starchy aquatic corms enter the *Qānūn*'s scheme additionally as **mughadhdhī** — *nourishing* — a category the Arabic system takes seriously and the modern herbal tradition has almost entirely forgotten: a substance can be classed as a drug **because it feeds**, and its "action" is the flesh it builds. The arrowhead corm's medicinal identity in this system is inseparable from its identity as a starch. **Category: cold-moist nutritive (*mughadhdhī*), refrigerant.**

**Chinese / Shennong lineage (TCM).** Here the plant is fully domesticated into the medical system as **慈姑 *cí gū*** — "the compassionate aunt," so named because the corm sends out runners that nurse a crowd of daughter-corms, as an aunt nurses children not her own. This is the plant's most developed tradition. *Cí gū* is classed as **sweet (甘) and slightly bitter (微苦), cold (寒)**, entering the **Lung, Liver and Spleen** channels; its stated functions are **to clear heat and resolve toxicity (清熱解毒 *qīng rè jiě dú*)**, **to move the blood and disperse stasis / dissipate nodules (行血通淋, 散結)**, and to **moisten the Lung and stop cough**. It is used for hot swellings, sores and nodules; for coughing of blood and for hot, painful urinary dribbling (*lìn*); and eaten as a starchy winter vegetable. The classical record is explicit that the **raw corm is acrid and irritating** and must be cooked — a warning the modern data will vindicate.

Note carefully: *cí gū* is **not** the same drug as **山慈姑 *shān cí gū*** (mountain *cí gū*, *Cremastra/Pleione* spp., an Orchidaceae). The names collide and the substances do not. Do not substitute.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.**

Two doctrines govern this plant, and both map cleanly onto real chemistry.

**(1) "Clears heat and resolves toxicity" (清熱解毒) → NF-κB suppression.** *Qīng rè jiě dú* is the Chinese category for what a modern pharmacologist would call **anti-inflammatory**: it names the condition in which tissue is red, hot, swollen, sore, and self-perpetuating — inflammation described from the outside. If the doctrine is pointing at anything real, the plant should suppress inflammatory signalling. It does, and the pathway is identified: the **polysaccharide fraction (SSP)** of *S. sagittifolia* suppresses **NF-κB** and its upstream regulators **TLR4/MyD88** while raising **SIRT1**, cutting TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 and neutrophil infiltration in a COPD model ([Liu et al., *Free Radic Biol Med* 253:649–663, 2026 — DOI: 10.1016/j.freeradbiomed.2026.06.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.freeradbiomed.2026.06.014)), and inhibits MAPK/NF-κB signalling in DSS-induced colitis while restoring gut-barrier proteins ([Feng et al., *Int J Biol Macromol* 254:127835, 2023 — DOI: 10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2023.127835](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2023.127835)). **TLR4/NF-κB is the master switch of the inflammatory response.** "Clears heat, resolves toxicity" and "inhibits TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB" are the same claim, five centuries apart.

**(2) "Moistens the Lung / cold and moist / *mughadhdhī*" → the active is the *starch-adjacent polysaccharide*, not a bitter alkaloid.** This is the doctrinally decisive point, and it is easy to miss. Every tradition classed this plant as **cold, moist, bland, nutritive** — the antithesis of the sharp, bitter, "drug-like" herbs. A modern reader trained on alkaloids reads that as *pharmacologically empty*: mere food. But the entire body of modern *S. sagittifolia* research converges on **SSP — Sagittaria sagittifolia polysaccharide** — as the active principle. The activity **is** in the bland, sweet, mucilaginous, nutritive fraction. The traditional taste-doctrine (sweet 甘, moistening, nourishing) was not a note about flavour; it was **an accurate identification of the active constituent class**, using the only analytic instrument available: the tongue. The "compassionate aunt" who feeds is the same molecule that suppresses NF-κB.

**(3) The counter-doctrine: raw acridity.** Against all this sits the tradition's insistence that the raw corm bites and must be cooked. This is not squeamishness. Alismataceae accumulate irritant **diterpenoid** constituents (the sagittarian/sagittatin-type diterpenes and related compounds of the family), and the acrid, throat-catching quality of the raw corm is a real chemical property. The doctrine "cook it" is a processing instruction (*pào zhì*) with a toxicological basis. The Codex treats it as binding.

### Active Components

- **SSP — *Sagittaria sagittifolia* polysaccharide** (including the characterised **PSSP-1**): the principal researched active. Anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, gut-microbiota-modulating. This is where essentially all the modern evidence lives.
- **Starch** — the corm is a staple carbohydrate; the basis of the *mughadhdhī* / nutritive classification.
- **Diterpenoids of the Alismataceae** (sagittarian/sagittatin-type and related): associated with the **acridity and irritancy of the raw corm**. ⚠ The Codex does not assign these compounds a quantified pharmacological profile, because a properly characterised, peer-reviewed pharmacology of the named diterpenes of *S. sagittifolia* was **not** retrievable in the literature search underlying this entry. Treat all claims about these compounds as provisional. [USER: verify before any clinical statement is made about the diterpene fraction.]
- **Phenolics / flavonoids** — present as in most Alismataceae; not the researched fraction here.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Two distinct roles, and the practitioner must not confuse them.

**Phase I — Grounding & Nourishment.** The **cooked corm** as food-medicine: a cooling, sweet, starchy base for hot, dry, depleted, inflamed constitutions. This is its primary and safest use, and it is the use with the deepest cross-tradition warrant (Unani *mughadhdhī*; Chinese winter vegetable; European water-famine-food). It is a **food**, and the Codex is content to call it one.

**Phase II — Clearing.** The **cooling topical** (bruised leaf, cooled) on hot, angry, superficial inflammations — the Dioscoridean refrigerant use.

What it is **not**: a Phase III or IV agent, a nootropic, a psychoactive, or a substance with any human clinical dossier. Its Kenetica lesson is doctrinal — **it teaches that the bland can be active**, and that a tradition's taste-classification can be a chemical classification in disguise.

### Scientific Correlation

*(All studies below retrieved from PubMed; DOIs linked. Every one of them is preclinical.)*

1. **Anti-inflammatory action via TLR4/NF-κB — the mechanism behind "clears heat, resolves toxicity."** Liu, Zhao, Liang et al. (*Free Radic Biol Med* 253:649–663, 2026; [DOI: 10.1016/j.freeradbiomed.2026.06.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.freeradbiomed.2026.06.014)) tested SSP extract in an LPS + cigarette-smoke COPD model in mice and BEAS-2B cells. SSP significantly reduced NF-κB, **TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6**, and Ly6g-labelled neutrophils; suppressed **TLR4, MyD88, IRAK1, TRAF6**; increased **SIRT1**; and reduced senescence markers (p53, p21, p16, RB) and β-gal-positive cells. *Mouse and cell model.*

2. **Gut inflammation and barrier repair.** Feng, Chen, Song et al. (*Int J Biol Macromol* 254(Pt 3):127835, 2023; [DOI: 10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2023.127835](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2023.127835)) identified a novel polysaccharide, **PSSP-1**, and showed that at 100/200/400 mg/kg·bw it significantly reduced the disease activity index in DSS-induced murine colitis, upregulated the tight-junction proteins **claudin-1, occludin and ZO-1**, increased *Lactobacillus* and short-chain fatty acids, and inhibited **MAPK and NF-κB** phosphorylation. *Mouse model.* Note how precisely this corroborates the "moistening, barrier-restoring, nourishing" reading of the plant: the mechanism is literally the **repair of an epithelial barrier**.

3. **Antioxidant / anti-apoptotic action in the lens (Nrf2 pathway).** Zhou, Liu, Gao et al. (*Int J Biol Macromol* 300:140270, 2025; [DOI: 10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2025.140270](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2025.140270)) showed SSP reduced lens opacity in selenite-induced rat cataract and H₂O₂-injured human lens epithelial cells by activating **Nrf2** and inhibiting endoplasmic-reticulum-stress-mediated apoptosis; the protection was abolished by Nrf2 knockdown. Extended to diabetic cataract with Nrf2-mediated control of apoptosis and **ferroptosis** by Zhou, Jiang, Ding et al. (*Exp Cell Res* 454(2):114841, 2025; [DOI: 10.1016/j.yexcr.2025.114841](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.yexcr.2025.114841)). *Rat / cell models.*

4. **Retinal microglial inflammation.** Jiang, Zhou, Zhang et al. (*Mol Neurobiol* 63(1):170, 2025; [DOI: 10.1007/s12035-025-05388-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12035-025-05388-1)) found SSP attenuated diabetic retinopathy in mice, preserving retinal vascular integrity and suppressing **TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB**-driven microglial activation. *Mouse model.*

**Evidence grade: PRECLINICAL — TRADITIONAL.** The mechanistic case for the *qīng rè jiě dú* doctrine is genuinely strong and unusually consistent: **four independent studies converge on TLR4/NF-κB suppression and Nrf2-mediated antioxidant defence**, which is exactly what an "anti-heat, anti-toxicity" drug ought to do. But the honest headline is this: **there are no human clinical trials of *Sagittaria sagittifolia* for any indication.** Not one was retrievable. Every result above is a mouse, a rat, or a cell line. Furthermore, nearly all of it studies an **isolated polysaccharide fraction at pharmacological doses**, not the cooked corm anyone actually eats — and it is not established that eating the vegetable delivers a comparable exposure. The diterpene chemistry, which is the plant's most distinctive and most *toxicologically relevant* feature, remains poorly characterised in the accessible literature.

Rank it accordingly: **a food with a credible and mechanistically coherent anti-inflammatory rationale, and no clinical dossier whatsoever.**

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM (SBJ):** 慈姑 *cí gū*. **Flavour:** sweet (甘), slightly bitter (微苦). **Nature:** cold (寒). **Channels:** Lung, Liver, Spleen. **Actions:** clears heat and resolves toxicity (清熱解毒); moves blood and dissipates nodules (行血散結); frees painful urinary dribbling (通淋); moistens the Lung and stops cough. **Processing (炮製):** must be cooked; raw corm acrid. ⚠ **Not** 山慈姑 (*shān cí gū*, Orchidaceae) — different drug, do not substitute.
- **Galenic / Unani (CM):** **Bārid raṭb** — cold and moist (2nd degree, by class). *Mubarrid* (refrigerant), *muraṭṭib* (moistening), *muḥallil* (resolvent, applied), and notably ***mughadhdhī*** (nourishing — its action *is* nutrition). Corrective: warming aromatics (ginger, cinnamon, pepper) counter its coldness in cold, phlegmatic temperaments.
- **Greek (DMM):** ψυκτικόν / μαλακτικόν — refrigerant and emollient; cold-moist aquatic class; bruised topically on hot inflammations.
- **Ayurveda:** **Not attested.** *Sagittaria sagittifolia* is not a classical *Sushruta*/*Charaka* dravya. No rasa/guna/virya/vipaka is assigned here, because assigning one would be fabrication.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* catalogues the cold, moist, mucilaginous plants of standing water as refrigerants and emollients — bruised leaves laid on hot inflammations, burns and angry swellings on the plain Galenic logic that a cold wet plant answers a hot dry lesion — and the entry is careful to record this at the level of the aquatic refrigerant class rather than a securely identified chapter naming *S. sagittifolia*, since Dioscoridean water-plant identifications are genuinely uncertain. Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system in which a cultivation status could sit; a topical refrigerant is a therapeutic observation, not a doctrine of the vital body. What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention — recognising the true cold-moist water-herb by shape and habitat, gathering it green, judging its emollient quality by hand. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The Arabic-Persian scheme reads the arrowhead corm as *bārid raṭb* — cold and moist — and as *mubarrid* (refrigerant) and *muraṭṭib* (moistening), but its most distinctive classification here is ***mughadhdhī***, nourishing: a starchy corm admitted to the *materia medica* because it feeds, its "action" being the flesh it builds. This is a real and serious classification, and it is the closest thing in the tradition to a constitutional role — a cold, depleted, dry, inflamed constitution is grounded and moistened by the nutritive starch. Yet *mughadhdhī* is a dietetic-therapeutic category, not a formal cultivation status assigned to this species, and the tradition's own strongest cultivation idea — conservation of innate heat and radical moisture — is a longevity frame of the whole regimen, not something the *Canon* tradition confers on this particular cooling starch. **(b) framed:** attested and constitutionally placed (cold-moist nutritive), but no cultivation status recorded.

**Chinese — Bencao (SBJ).** Here the plant is fully domesticated as 慈姑 *cí gū*, "the compassionate aunt," classed sweet (甘) and slightly bitter (微苦), cold (寒), entering the Lung, Liver and Spleen channels, and worked to clear heat and resolve toxicity (清熱解毒 *qīng rè jiě dú*), move blood and dissipate nodules (行血散結), free painful urinary dribbling (通淋) and moisten the Lung — with the binding processing instruction (炮製) that the raw corm is acrid and must be cooked. This is a well-developed therapeutic identity, but it is a curative one: *qīng rè jiě dú* attacks a hot, toxic condition, the opposite pole from *yang sheng* 養生, the conservational nourishing-of-life. No *san pin* 三品 grade (上/中/下品) is attributable to *cí gū* from named scholarship (Unschuld, Stanley-Baker, or a scholarly *Bencao* translation) for this species in the sources retrieved, so no cultivation grade is claimed. **(b) framed:** attested in the Chinese materia medica with a full therapeutic profile, no *san pin* cultivation status sourced.

**Practitioner's note.** What a cultivator actually does with this plant is eat it — the cooked, peeled corm as a cooling, sweet, nourishing starch for a hot, dry, depleted or inflamed constitution, the one use with deep cross-tradition warrant (Unani *mughadhdhī*, Chinese winter vegetable) and the safest by far. Secondarily, the cooled bruised leaf as a refrigerant topical on hot swellings. Never raw: every tradition that grades it insists on cooking, and the irritant diterpenoid chemistry vindicates them. Its deeper lesson for a cultivator is doctrinal — that the bland, sweet, nutritive fraction can be the active one, and that a tradition's taste-classification was a chemical classification made with the tongue.

### Contraindications

- **⚠ NEVER EAT THE CORM RAW.** The raw corm is **acrid and irritant** — a consistent traditional warning across the Chinese record, and consistent with the irritant diterpenoid chemistry of the Alismataceae. Peel and cook thoroughly. Raw ingestion risks oral, pharyngeal and gastric irritation.
- **Cold / deficient-cold constitutions (脾胃虚寒).** A cold-natured, cooling drug. Aggravating in cold-deficient Spleen/Stomach patterns, chronic cold diarrhoea, and cold-type digestive weakness. The Unani corrective (warming aromatic) applies.
- **Pregnancy.** **Avoid medicinal doses.** The TCM function of "moving blood and dispersing stasis" (行血散結) places *cí gū* in a class conventionally cautioned in pregnancy, and there are **no safety data**. Culinary quantities of the cooked vegetable are traditionally eaten as food; medicinal decoctions and any concentrated extract should be avoided. Same for lactation.
- **Name confusion — a genuine safety hazard.** *Cí gū* (慈姑, *Sagittaria*) and *shān cí gū* (山慈姑, *Cremastra appendiculata* / *Pleione* spp.) are distinct drugs with distinct toxicology. Substitution is a dispensing error, not a variation.
- **Isolated SSP extracts.** **No human safety or dosing data exist.** All published dosing is animal. Do not extrapolate mg/kg mouse doses to humans.
- **Diabetes / blood glucose.** SSP has been reported to have hypoglycaemic and lipid-regulating properties in **diabetic animal models** (Jiang et al., 2025, DOI above). This is not a human finding, but anyone on glucose-lowering medication who takes concentrated extracts should monitor and consult a practitioner — a theoretical additive-effect risk.
- **Harvest site.** An aquatic corm crop. Do not wild-harvest from polluted, agricultural-runoff, or industrially contaminated water — the corm is the plant's storage organ.
