# I-286: Angelica sinensis (Dong Quai)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-286 | Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Angelica sinensis* (Oliv.) Diels
**Family:** Apiaceae

**Common Names:**
- Chinese: 当归 (*Dāng guī*) — "should return" or "fit returns"; poetically interpreted as "the herb that makes the blood return to its proper place"
- English: Dong Quai, Dang Gui, Female Ginseng, Chinese Angelica
- Japanese: *Toki* (当帰)
- Korean: *Danggwi* (당귀)

**Etymology:**
当归 (*Dāng guī*) carries deep semantic resonance in Chinese: *dāng* (当) = "ought to" / "fitting that"; *guī* (归) = "return / come home." Multiple classical interpretations exist:
1. "Blood should return to channels" — describes the blood-regulating function
2. Poetic reference to a wife waiting for a husband at war to "return" — the herb given to soldiers' wives as tonifying medicine; also given to returning soldiers to replenish depleted blood
3. "The herb that makes menstrual blood return to its proper rhythm" — gynecological reference

This etymological richness reflects *dāng guī*'s central position in Chinese gynecological medicine for ~2,000+ years.

**Geographic Origin:**
Native to high-altitude regions of southwestern China (Gansu, Yunnan, Sichuan, Hubei provinces; 2,500–4,000 m elevation). Gansu province (*Mǐn County*) produces the highest-grade *dāng guī* — the cool, moist mountain air and specific soil conditions are considered essential for quality. Attempts to cultivate elsewhere consistently produce inferior material (lower essential oil + less Z-ligustilide).

**Parts Used:** Root — differentiated by part:
- **Root head (归头, *guī tóu*):** nourishes and supplements blood (Bu Xue)
- **Root body (归身, *guī shēn*):** supplements blood + moves blood simultaneously
- **Root tail (归尾, *guī wěi*):** primarily moves and invigorates blood (Huo Xue)
- This tripartite use of root sections is unique in TCM pharmacy and reflects observed pharmacological differences in phthalide concentration gradients along the root

**Morphology:**
Perennial; 60–100 cm; feathery compound leaves; white umbel flowers; distinctive sweet, warm, slightly spicy aroma from root; most aromatic of the Angelica species.

**The "Four Pillars" of TCM:**

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domains:**

1. **Blood nourishing / tonic (Bu Xue)** — SBJ, FMT; primary TCM designation; anemia, pallor, dizziness, fatigue, dry skin
2. **Gynecological regulator — menstrual, dysmenorrhea, amenorrhea** — SBJ, FMT; European folk (secondarily)
3. **Blood-invigorating / circulation** — Root tail especially; anti-platelet, vasodilatory
4. **Analgesic (pain)** — Z-ligustilide mechanism; dysmenorrhea, headache, musculoskeletal
5. **Bowel moistening laxative** — rich oil content; moistens Large Intestine; classic TCM blood deficiency constipation

**The Blood-Qi Duality:**
*Dāng guī*'s remarkable pharmacological profile is its simultaneous blood-nourishing (increasing hematopoiesis, building blood) AND blood-moving (anti-platelet, vasodilatory, anti-spasmodic) activity. This dual function — "nourish and move" simultaneously — is unique among major blood tonics and explains its universal applicability in gynecological conditions: it both builds depleted blood AND prevents stagnation that causes dysmenorrhea. Most blood tonics (iron, folic acid) only build without moving; most blood-movers only move without building.

**Energetic Classification:**
- ◈ Jing (structural): Primary — blood nourishing at constitutional level; anemia; menstrual regulation
- ◉ Qi (functional): Secondary — blood-moving; anti-spasmodic; circulation
- ☿ Shen (neuromodulatory): Minor — anxiolytic in TCM blood deficiency pattern

**Primary Tier: ◈ Jing (blood tonic)**

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Phthalides (Primary Active — Essential Oil)

**1. Z-Ligustilide (Z-butylidenephthalide) — 50–60% EO; 1% dried root**
- PRIMARY compound; most studied constituent; volatile (sensitive to heat and oxidation)
- **Ca²⁺ channel antagonist:** smooth muscle relaxant (uterine, vascular, bronchial) — explains anti-spasmodic, uterine relaxation, vasodilation
- **Dopamine antagonist:** low-affinity D2 receptor modulation
- **CYP1A2 induction:** relevant drug interaction potential
- Concentrated in root body and tail; lower in head

**2. n-Butylidenephthalide** — similar; anti-platelet; vasodilatory
**3. Senkyunolide A, H, I** — anti-platelet aggregation; vasodilatory
**4. Ferulic acid** (major non-volatile) — primary non-EO active; anti-oxidant; anti-platelet (inhibits COX-1; reduces TXA2); anti-inflammatory

### Polysaccharides

**5. Angelica sinensis polysaccharides (ASP):** Immunomodulatory; hematopoietic stimulation; macrophage activation; potentiates erythropoiesis

### Coumarins

**6. Psoralens (bergapten, xanthotoxin):** Present but at lower concentrations than A. archangelica; phototoxicity risk lower for root preparations

### Phytosterols

**7. β-Sitosterol:** Anti-inflammatory; cholesterol modulation

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### Hematopoietic / Blood-Building Mechanism
- **Polysaccharide (ASP) stimulation of erythropoiesis:** Direct stimulation of bone marrow erythroid progenitors → increased red blood cell production; Level B evidence in cancer chemotherapy-associated anemia
- **Ferulic acid:** Antioxidant protection of erythrocyte membranes → reduced hemolysis → net blood volume maintenance
- These mechanisms support the TCM "Bu Xue" (nourish blood) function; clinical correlate = improved hemoglobin, reduced fatigue in deficiency states

### Gynecological / Uterine Mechanism — BIDIRECTIONAL
- **Z-Ligustilide → uterine relaxation:** Ca²⁺ channel antagonism in myometrium → inhibits uterine contractions → anti-spasmodic in dysmenorrhea
- **Ferulic acid → uterine relaxation:** Inhibits prostaglandin-induced uterine contraction (COX-1 inhibition → reduced PGF2α → reduced uterine spasm)
- **Estrogenic activity (weak):** Phthalides + ferulic acid → ERα/ERβ weak agonism; can stimulate uterine contractions at higher doses — this is the uterine-stimulating action (emmenagogue at high doses)
- This paradox explains traditional observations: low/moderate doses → anti-spasmodic; high doses → uterine-stimulating (emmenagogue)

### Anti-Platelet / Vasodilatory
- **Ferulic acid → COX-1 inhibition → TXA2 reduction → platelet aggregation inhibition**
- **Senkyunolides → cAMP elevation → platelet disaggregation**
- **Z-Ligustilide → vascular smooth muscle Ca²⁺ channel blockade → vasodilation → blood pressure reduction**
- Net: blood-thinning + vasodilatory = "moves blood," improves microcirculation

### Analgesic
- Z-Ligustilide: central CNS depressant + peripheral Ca²⁺ channel mechanism → pain reduction
- Ferulic acid: anti-inflammatory → reduced prostaglandin-mediated pain
- Combination explains efficacy in dysmenorrhea (both anti-spasmodic AND anti-inflammatory)

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Dysmenorrhea / Menstrual Regulation:**
- Multiple RCTs of *dāng guī* in combination formulas (Sì Wù Tāng; Wēn Jīng Tāng) for primary dysmenorrhea; reduction in pain scores; reduced NSAID use
- *Dāng guī* alone: fewer dedicated RCTs than combination formulas
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (combination formulas); **Level C** (dāng guī alone)

**Anemia / Hematopoietic:**
- Chinese clinical trials (lower methodological standard): *dāng guī* injection + polysaccharide extracts improve hemoglobin in chemotherapy anemia
- Systematic review: heterogeneous; publication bias likely
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (Chinese RCTs; methodological concerns); **Level C** (traditional use)

**Menopausal Symptoms:**
- Several RCTs of *dāng guī* alone for hot flushes; inconsistent results; some show improvement in combined menopausal symptom scores, others negative for hot flush specifically
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (inconsistent; some positive signal)

**Cardiovascular / Anti-Platelet:**
- Ferulic acid anti-platelet: Level A for pure ferulic acid; Level B for whole-root preparations
- Vasodilation: Level C

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Scent of Women's Medicine:**
*Dāng guī*'s aroma is unmistakable — warm, sweet, faintly spicy, slightly medicinal, with an earthy depth. It is the scent of Chinese gynecological medicine; any experienced TCM herbalist or patient recognizes it instantly. The smell of *Sì Wù Tāng* (Four Substance Decoction) decocting — *dāng guī* dominant — is one of the most culturally specific olfactory markers in East Asian medicine. For women who grew up receiving TCM gynecological treatment, this scent carries profound somatic memory: the smell of being cared for, of blood being restored.

**Cultural Gender Encoding:**
Unlike most herbs that exist outside gender categories, *dāng guī* is explicitly a "women's herb" in Chinese culture — but with important nuance. TCM does not restrict blood tonics to women; men with blood deficiency also receive *dāng guī*. But the cultural association with women's reproductive health is so strong that the English translation "female ginseng" emerged — a framing that simultaneously honors its status and over-simplifies it.

**The Return:**
The etymology of *guī* (归 = return) carries phenomenological weight. Clinically, patients with amenorrhea who respond to *dāng guī* formulas experience literally what the name promises: the menstrual cycle "returns." Women with irregular cycles or blood deficiency patterns after childbirth or illness describe a felt sense of physical restoration — of blood "returning" to support vitality, skin, cognition, and reproductive function. TCM practitioners considered this not metaphor but literal pharmacological description.

**The Four Seasons Formula:**
Classical TCM varied *Sì Wù Tāng* seasonally — adjusting ratios based on the season's energy. Spring: increase *chuān xiōng* (moving/ascending); Summer: decrease *shú dì huáng* (enriching/heavy); Autumn: increase *bái sháo* (astringent/containing); Winter: increase *dāng guī* (warming/nourishing). This seasonal dosing reflects awareness that the formula's blood-moving vs. blood-building ratio needed adjustment for the energetic demands of each season. It also demonstrates the sophistication of TCM pharmaceutical reasoning.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Standard Decoction (Raw Herb):**
- 6–15 g dried root per decoction (standard Chinese pharmacopoeia dose range)
- Simmer 30–40 minutes; typically used in combination formula (Sì Wù Tāng, Bā Zhēn Tāng, etc.)
- **Root section differentiation:** For blood nourishing = use root body (归身); for blood moving/circulation = use root tail (归尾); for comprehensive = whole root

**Standardized Extract:**
- Extract standardized to ferulic acid content: 0.2–0.5% ferulic acid; 500–2,000 mg/day
- Bioavailability concern: Z-ligustilide is volatile; extraction method matters; dried whole root preferred over highly processed extracts for volatile phthalide content

**Tincture:**
- 1:5 in 45–60% ethanol; 3–5 mL 3× daily
- Preserves volatile Z-ligustilide better than aqueous preparations

### Safety Profile

**Generally safe at TCM standard doses.** Extensive safety record in Chinese clinical practice over 2,000 years.

**CRITICAL Drug Interactions:**

**Warfarin / Anticoagulants:**
- **CLINICALLY SIGNIFICANT INTERACTION:** Ferulic acid inhibits COX-1 → anti-platelet; combined with warfarin = significantly increased bleeding risk
- Published case reports of elevated INR in patients taking *dāng guī* + warfarin
- **Monitor INR closely or AVOID combination**

**Estrogen / Hormonal Therapy:**
- Weak estrogenic activity: AVOID with hormone-sensitive conditions (ER+ breast cancer, uterine cancer, endometriosis, fibroids) unless supervised by qualified practitioner
- AVOID with oral contraceptives (potential hormonal interaction)

**CRITICAL — Pregnancy:**
- **ABSOLUTELY AVOID in pregnancy** (first and second trimester especially)
- Emmenagogue at higher doses → uterine stimulation → miscarriage risk
- Traditional TCM DOES use dang gui cautiously in late pregnancy for specific conditions (blood deficiency preparation for labor) — but only under expert TCM supervision

**Contraindications:**
- Pregnancy (medicinal doses) — CRITICAL
- Active bleeding / hemorrhagic disorders
- ER+ hormone-sensitive cancers
- Pre-operative (anti-platelet activity; discontinue 1–2 weeks pre-surgery)
- Diarrhea / loose stool (rich oil content moistens Intestine; can worsen diarrhea)
- Apiaceae allergy (furocoumarin phototoxicity; lower risk from root preparations)
