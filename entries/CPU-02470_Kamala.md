# Kamala — wars (ورس) and qanbīl (قنبيل) as printed in the Arabic texts

## *Wars: the red dye-powder of Yemen, identified in the modern literature with Flemingia grahamiana; qanbīl: the red glandular powder identified with kamala, Mallotus philippensis. The identification is the modern one and is uncertain for wars; see Origin & Tradition* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** CM, TBN

---

### Origin & Tradition

The word "kamala" is not in either Arabic text. It is the Indian name of the red powder from the fruit of *Mallotus philippensis*, and the spine files it here because the Arabic names *qanbīl* and *wars* are matched to it by the modern literature. The matching is by the descriptions, not by the texts: Avicenna writes two separate entries, one for each name, and compares them in a single clause (below). Ibn Qayyim writes only *wars*. The identification of *wars* with a single plant is the less certain of the two; it is listed as an identity doubt in the report for this entry.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM), wars.** *Canon* Book II, entry *wars*, Arabic text lines 12398 to 12402. What it is: "a deep red thing resembling ground saffron (*saḥīq al-zaʿfarān*), brought from Yemen; it is said to be scraped from its trees" (شيء أحمر قانىء يشبه سحيق الزعفران وهو مجلوب من اليمن ويقال أنه ينحت من أشجاره). Temperament: "hot and dry in the second [degree]". Action: "astringent" (*qābiḍ*). Cosmetic: "it benefits melasma (*kalaf*) and freckles (*namash*), and when drunk it benefits *al-waḍaḥ*" (the white patches of the skin). Swellings and pustules: "it benefits pustules." Wounds and ulcers: "it benefits scab (*jarab*), itch (*ḥakka*), *saʿfa* and *qūbāʾ*," which are skin eruptions in Avicenna's naming. No dose is given. The entry quotes no other author.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM), qanbīl.** *Canon* Book II, entry *qanbīl*, Arabic text lines 17515 to 17519. What it is: "sandy seeds topped with a redness like the redness of *wars*" (بزور رملية يعلوها حمرة كحمرة الورس). So the Canon itself draws the likeness of the two. Temperament: "hot and dry in the third." Property: "Ibn Māsawayh said: in it is a strong astringency." Excretory organs: "it kills worms and the gourd-seed worms (*ḥabb al-qarʿ*) and expels them, drunk and applied as a salve, as is said" (يقتل الديدان وحب القرع ويخرجه شربا وطلاء فيما يقال). Avicenna hedges it with "as is said". Elsewhere the Canon lists *qanbīl* among the drugs "most specific for the gourd-seed worms" (heading at line 39231, name at line 39234), and uses it in worm recipes that also name fern (*sarakhs*) among the drugs (lines 39241 to 39245). Those lines were read; the surrounding recipes were not studied further. A collyrium of equal parts of pepper, *dār fulful* and *qanbīl* is also given (line 25423); the complaint it is for was not read.

**Prophetic medicine (TBN), wars.** Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya, *al-Ṭibb al-Nabawī*, letter *wāw*, entry *wars*, Arabic text lines 7033 to 7047. He first gives three reports.
(1) "Al-Tirmidhī mentioned in his *Jāmiʿ*, from the hadith of Zayd ibn Arqam, from the Prophet, that he used to prescribe oil and *wars* for pleurisy (*dhāt al-janb*); Qatāda said: it is poured into the side of the mouth (*yuladdu bihi*), and it is poured from the side of the complaint" (lines 7033 to 7035).
(2) "Ibn Mājah related in his *Sunan*, also from Zayd ibn Arqam: the Messenger of God prescribed for pleurisy *wars*, *qusṭ* and oil, to be poured in the side of the mouth" (lines 7036 to 7037).
(3) "And it is sound (*ṣaḥḥa*) from Umm Salama that she said: the woman in childbed would sit forty days after the birth, and one of us would paint *wars* on her face for melasma (*kalaf*)" (lines 7038 to 7039).
Only the third carries a verdict by Ibn Qayyim in this entry, and it is his own word *ṣaḥḥa*, "it is sound". He gives no grading for the first two at this locus. The two Zayd reports were not checked against the Bukhari and Muslim collections on disk, because they are attributed to Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah, which are not on disk. [USER: verify the gradings of the Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah reports in a hadith source.]
Ibn Qayyim also gives the philologist's note, "Abū Ḥanīfa the lexicographer said: *wars* is sown (*yuzraʿu zarʿan*) and is not wild, and I do not know it outside the land of the Arabs, nor within it outside Yemen" (lines 7040 to 7041). That sits oddly beside Avicenna's "scraped from its trees": one calls it a sown crop, the other a tree product. Neither text reconciles them.
Then his medicine: "its power is hot and dry at the beginning of the second degree; the best is the red, soft in the hand, with little bran (*nukhāla*); it benefits melasma, itch and pustules on the surface of the body when applied, and has an astringent, dyeing power; when drunk it benefits *al-waḍaḥ*, and the draught of it is the weight of a dirham" (lines 7042 to 7045). "In temperament and benefits it is close to sea costus" (lines 7046 to 7047); smeared on *bahaq*, itch, pustules and *safʿa* it benefits them, "and the garment dyed with *wars* strengthens venery (*al-bāh*)" (line 7047).
A fourth report, elsewhere, uses it: in the chapter on pleurisy (heading at line 1427), Ibn Qayyim cites a sound hadith (*al-ḥadīth al-ṣaḥīḥ*) from Umm Salama, in which the household poured medicine into the Prophet's mouth during his last illness: "with Indian wood (*al-ʿūd al-hindī*), some *wars* and drops of oil" (lines 1462 to 1471). The Prophet said: "God would not have afflicted me with that disease," and then ordered all in the house to be dosed in the same way except his uncle al-ʿAbbās (lines 1470 to 1475). The grading word *ṣaḥīḥ* is Ibn Qayyim's. Lines 1462 to 1475 were read in this session.

### Active Components

The texts name no constituent. In general terms the red powder sold as kamala is the glandular hair of the *Mallotus* fruit, and its best-known constituent is rottlerin, a coloured phloroglucinol compound. [USER: verify.] Nothing in these two texts shows that *wars* and kamala are chemically the same, and for *wars* the plant is itself uncertain. No constituent of *wars* is given here.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The texts record external paint for melasma, freckles, itch and pustules; a drink for skin patches; a poured or drunk remedy for tapeworm-type "gourd-seed" worms; and pleurisy, for which Ibn Qayyim's reports have the drug poured into the mouth. Pleurisy and worm infestation are conditions for medical care. No protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

- **Wars, external:** painted or smeared on the skin (TBN lines 7038 to 7039 and 7043 to 7047; CM lines 12401 to 12402).
- **Wars, drunk:** "the draught of it is the weight of a dirham" (TBN lines 7044 to 7045). This is the only dose in either entry.
- **Wars with oil, poured into the side of the mouth:** for pleurisy, in the Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah reports (TBN lines 7033 to 7037).
- **Qanbīl:** "drunk and as a salve" (CM line 17518). No dose is given in the entry. No dose is given in the sources retrieved for the entry itself. [USER: verify.]

### Scientific Correlation

Kamala powder has a long record as an anthelmintic in Indian practice, and the Canon's worm use for *qanbīl* fits it. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify.] Nothing is stated for *wars*, whose botanical identity is the open question.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Unani / Avicenna (CM):** *wars* hot and dry in the second degree, astringent (line 12400); *qanbīl* hot and dry in the third degree, with strong astringency on Ibn Māsawayh's word (lines 17517 to 17518).
- **Prophetic medicine (TBN):** *wars* hot and dry at the beginning of the second degree, astringent, dyeing; close to sea costus (lines 7042 to 7047).
- No other tradition's classification is given.

### Cultivation Register

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** *Canon* Book II records *wars* and *qanbīl* as skin and worm remedies, with no word on the vital faculties, innate heat or radical moisture. The doctrine of innate heat and radical moisture as a finite endowment is the frame of the Canon as a whole, but no line read for these two entries applies it to them. **(c) Absent: no cultivation status is attested, and no mention of it is made.**

**Prophetic medicine (TBN).** *Wars* appears in the *sunna* as a medicine Zayd ibn Arqam reports the Prophet prescribing for pleurisy, and as a woman's cosmetic in the time of childbed in Umm Salama's report that Ibn Qayyim calls sound. This is *ṭibb nabawī*, medicine, and not a cultivation practice. Ibn Qayyim does not place *wars* among the six necessary things (air, food and drink, movement and rest, sleep and waking, retention and evacuation, the passions of the soul) in this entry. **(b) Framed: attested as Prophetic medicine; no cultivation status is attested.**

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing to practise here beyond reading. The honest use of this entry is as a record: the Canon's tapeworm drug and the dye-powder of Yemen, which two texts treat as distinct, and which the Codex has filed together on a modern match that is firm for kamala and doubtful for *wars*. A cultivator learns from it the discipline of keeping a name, a plant and a hadith report apart until the text joins them.

### Contraindications

- Worm remedies are drugs for a diagnosed infestation; Avicenna's own hedge ("as is said") is a reason for caution.
- The Canon calls both drugs hot and dry (second and third degree); the entries state no contraindication.
- Pleurisy is a serious chest condition; the reports are a record of medicine given by mouth to a seriously ill man, not guidance.
- Skin staining: *wars* is a dye by Ibn Qayyim's own word.
