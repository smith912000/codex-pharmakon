# Purple shellfish (purpura) — Dioscorides, 2-4 PORPHURA ("Purpura")
## *Purple-dye shellfish, burnt; the translator's gloss for Dioscorides' chapter heading "PORPHURA" is "Purpura" and adds "Shellfish - yields Tyrian purple dye"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` (line 4926 of the text file) and the chapter itself in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (lines 4926-4929; the name index prints "PORPHURA 184" and "Purpura 184", the page of this 2000 English edition, and "Purpurae 185"). The chapter is 2-4, in Book Two, which opens (line 4903) with the chapters on "Living creatures" beginning at 2-1. The whole chapter is three lines: the heading "2-4. PORPHURA"; the translator's gloss "SUGGESTED: Purpura / Shellfish - yields Tyrian purple dye."; and the text: "Burnt purpura dry and clean teeth, repress excrescent flesh, and draw boils and heal them."

That is all that is said of the shellfish in its own chapter. It gives no description of the animal, its place or season of capture, no way of telling a good specimen, no account of how it is burnt, and no quantity. The related chapters that follow give more. Chapter 2-5 (KUREKES, suggested "Buccinum species — Whelks") says "Burnt buccina do the same as the above but they are more caustic by nature", and describes burning them filled with salt "in an unfired clay jar" as a toothpaste and for burns, and that "The medicine must be left alone until it grows hard like a shell, for after the burn is healed it falls off of its own accord" (line 4934). Chapter 2-6 (IONIA, "The columellae of Buccinae [Whelks] and Purpurae") says the columellae are "the middle parts in the buccinae and purpurae around which they turn in or wind around", "burnt in the same way, but more caustic than the buccinae and purpurae because by nature they adhere less", and adds that "The flesh of the buccina has a good taste and is good for the stomach, but does not soften the bowels" (lines 4936-4937). Those sentences concern the whelk and the columella, not the purpura, and 2-4 does not say that the purpura is burnt in a jar or with salt. Whether it is burnt in the same way as the whelk, the lines read leave open. [USER: verify]

The purpura appears once more, as a dye, in the chapter on burnt wool (2-83, ERIA KEKAUMENA, line 5277): "Locks of wool that have been dyed with sea purple [pupurea — shellfish] are burnt in the same way." The chapter on burnt wool describes a method of burning in a jar and washing the ash for eye medicines; that sentence only says wool dyed with the shellfish colour may be burnt in the same way as undyed wool. It states no medicinal property of the dye.

### Active Components

The shellfish are marine snails (in the family Muricidae, which includes the genus *Murex* and related genera). The purple dye is made from a secretion of a gland in the snail, whose chief colouring matter is a brominated indigo (6,6'-dibromoindigo); the Tyrian purple dye the translator names is that. Shell is mostly calcium carbonate, and burning the shell converts it to calcium oxide (quicklime), which is caustic; this is a general chemical fact, and the Dioscorides lines read do not say what the ash was. The soft body is protein, water and salts. No composition specific to Dioscorides' material was read, and no active principle is claimed by the sources. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is Dioscorides' one-line chapter: burnt purpura "dry and clean teeth, repress excrescent flesh, and draw boils and heal them". The Codex offers no protocol. Caustic burnt shell on teeth, gums or open skin can burn tissue, and boils and overgrown tissue need clinical assessment (see Contraindications). [USER: verify]

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation in 2-4 is "burnt" (that is, the shellfish burnt, presumably to an ash or calx). The vessel, fuel, time and the way the burnt matter is applied are not given in this chapter. The chapter for the whelk (2-5, line 4934) gives a method, filling with salt and burning in an unfired clay jar, but does not say it applies to the purpura. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Burnt shell is mostly calcium oxide and, when moistened, calcium hydroxide, which are strongly alkaline and cause chemical burns; a caustic ash on the gums, tongue or skin can burn tissue. Shellfish can concentrate toxins and bacteria from the water, and shellfish allergy is common and can be severe. Muricid snails supplied the ancient Mediterranean purple dye. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** No quality is printed for the purpura in chapter 2-4: no warming, cooling, drying or astringent label. The chapter lists actions only (dry and clean teeth, repress excrescent flesh, draw boils). Chapter 2-5 says the burnt whelk is "more caustic by nature" than the purpura, which is a relative statement about the whelk, and 2-6 says the columella is "more caustic than the buccinae and purpurae"; neither prints a quality for the purpura itself. ### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied here. What he can offer a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, detection of adulteration), and this chapter has none of it: it gives no place or season for taking the shellfish, no sign by which good material is told from poor, and no account of adulteration. It is a one-sentence recipe for three complaints of teeth and skin. There is no cultivation status in the chapter, nor any amuletic use, so there is nothing to distinguish between recording and endorsing. The dye is a matter of trade and colour, and the chapter does not link it to any practice.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. The chapter is a record of what a first-century physician reported for teeth and boils, not a practice, and it offers neither an observational method nor a vital claim. Its use today is as a window onto how Book Two treats the shelled sea creatures.

### Contraindications

- **Caustic burns:** burnt shell is alkaline and can burn gums, tongue and skin; the chapter itself calls the related burnt whelk "more caustic by nature" (2-5).
- **Shellfish allergy and toxins:** shellfish allergy can be severe; wild shellfish may carry marine biotoxins and bacteria.
- **Not a treatment for boils or tooth disease:** both need clinical assessment; do not rely on this line.
- **Legal and conservation status:** harvesting wild molluscs is regulated in most places, and some muricid species are protected or locally depleted. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** killing sea animals for a remedy with no supported benefit is hard to justify.
