# I-364 — Cuminum cyminum · Cumin

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Cuminum cyminum* L.  
**Family:** Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)  
**Common names:** Cumin (EN); Kreuzkümmel (DE, "cross caraway" — despite being distinct from caraway); cumin (FR); comino (ES); cumino (IT); kuminon [κύμινον] (GR, classical); kammun [كمون] (AR); jeera / zeera [जीरा] (HI — safed jeera "white cumin" to distinguish from black cumin); jira (SA); zi ran [孜然] (TCM — also written 枯茗 or 孜然茴香); jeerak (GUJ); zira (UR/FA/RU)

**Critical disambiguation:**
- *Cuminum cyminum* = Cumin (this entry; warm earthy-spice aroma; cuminaldehyde-dominant)
- *Carum carvi* = Caraway (I-363; rye-bread aroma; carvone-dominant)
- *Bunium persicum* = Black caraway / Kashmiri cumin (sharper; used in Kashmiri cuisine)
- *Nigella sativa* = Black seed / "black cumin" (I-XX; Ranunculaceae; thymoquinone; completely different)
Cumin and caraway are visually similar (brown crescent seeds) but taste and smell completely different; they are frequently confused in translation of historical texts and in multilingual retail, causing pharmacological attribution errors across multiple historical sources.

**Parts used:** Seeds/fruits (culinary and medicinal; the primary therapeutic form); essential oil (aromatherapy and standardized preparations); seedling sprouts (minor)  
**Habitat & distribution:** Annual; native to the Eastern Mediterranean and Southwest Asia (Egypt, Turkey, Iran, India — multiple claims of origin); cultivated across the Mediterranean, Middle East, North Africa, South Asia, Mexico, and Central America; drought-tolerant; poor, well-drained calcareous soils preferred; commercially significant in India (largest producer), Syria, Iran, Turkey  
**Global significance:** Cumin is the **second most widely consumed spice globally** after black pepper, and by some measures — particularly in terms of the global population who consume it daily — it may rival pepper. Its pharmacological significance is proportionally vast.

## 3. Convergence Analysis

Cumin's 7/9 convergence places it in the highest tier of the Codex. Alongside coriander and fennel, cumin represents one of the three most consistently documented aromatic Apiaceae across all traditional medical systems with access to the plant. The remarkable depth of its Ebers Papyrus record — appearing in more than 60 of the 880 prescriptions, making it the most frequent spice-herb in the entire papyrus — establishes cumin as the central aromatic medicine of ancient Egypt, surpassing even coriander (30+ prescriptions) in documented frequency.

The convergence across Dioscorides, Ibn Sīnā, and Sushruta on **digestive/carminative, galactagogue, wound healing, and analgesic** applications is remarkable because these traditions had entirely different humoral and energetic frameworks — Galenic four humors, Unani temperament theory, and Tridosha Ayurveda — yet arrived at identical practical applications. This cross-framework convergence constitutes strong empirical validation.

The Matthew 23:23 verse (tithing "mint and dill and cumin") places cumin as one of three specific herbs demanded as Temple tax in 1st-century CE Palestine — alongside dill and mint — confirming its agricultural economic importance and daily presence in the ancient Near East. The Talmudic discussions of cumin (particularly in Tractate Shabbat regarding grinding cumin on the Sabbath — whether it constitutes forbidden labor) indicate that cumin grinding for immediate culinary-medicinal use was a daily household activity requiring rabbinic clarification of Sabbath law.

Modern pharmacology has confirmed the most practically significant traditional claim: **cumin enhances iron bioavailability from plant foods** — a mechanism operating on the entire diet's iron absorption efficiency, not just the cumin itself.

**Iron bioavailability note:**
The iron content of cumin seeds (66 mg/100g) is extremely high, but dried spices are consumed in small amounts (2–5 g per meal). The pharmacological significance is not the absolute iron in cumin itself but cumin's documented enhancement of iron absorption from coexisting foods in the meal — estimated to increase non-heme iron absorption by 2–3-fold in studies comparing cumin-containing vs cumin-free meals in iron-deficient populations.

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Carminative / Spasmolytic:**
Cuminaldehyde → smooth muscle relaxation (mechanism distinct from carvone-based carminatives; aromatic aldehyde targets different smooth muscle receptors). Less well characterized mechanistically than carvone, but consistent traditional and observational evidence for efficacy. α-Terpineol (minor component) has documented calcium channel blocking activity.

**Hypoglycemic:**
Cuminaldehyde → α-glucosidase inhibition (competitive inhibitor of pancreatic glucoamylase and sucrase → reduced postprandial glucose rise); also insulin secretagogue effect in animal models (possible direct β-cell stimulation). Additionally: cumin extract reduces hepatic gluconeogenesis in diabetic rodents. Human RCT data: Zare R et al. 2014 (n=88 T2DM patients: cumin powder 75 mg/day × 8 weeks vs placebo: significant reduction in FBG, HbA1c, total cholesterol, LDL — Level B quality). Moradi MT et al. 2016 similarly positive.

**Iron bioavailability enhancement:**
Cumin's most underappreciated pharmacological property. Mechanism: (1) Vitamin C in fresh cumin reduces Fe³⁺ → Fe²⁺ (more absorbable form); (2) cuminaldehyde chelates phytate (the primary inhibitor of non-heme iron absorption) → reduces phytate-iron binding → frees iron for intestinal absorption; (3) possible direct enterocyte membrane effects on iron transporter (DMT1) expression. Result: cumin consumed with iron-containing plant foods (legumes, grains) increases bioavailable iron by estimated 2–3×. This mechanism explains why traditional Indian and Middle Eastern cuisines that combine cumin with legumes (dal, hummus, lentil soup) have maintained adequate iron status in largely plant-based diets for millennia — the cumin is doing dietary pharmacology at a population scale.

**Antimicrobial:**
Cuminaldehyde → membrane disruption; active against *H. pylori* (MIC 500–1000 μg/mL), *S. aureus*, *E. coli*, *Salmonella* (food preservation role), *Candida albicans*. Traditional use for digestive infections consistent with mechanism.

**Anti-inflammatory:**
Minor flavonoids (apigenin, luteolin, quercetin) → COX-2 inhibition; TNF-α, IL-6 suppression. Cuminaldehyde also shows modest anti-inflammatory activity in rodent carrageenan models. Not the primary mechanism but contributes to overall pharmacological profile.

**Galactagogue:**
Same weak-estrogenic / dopaminergic hypotheses as other Apiaceae; Sushruta specifically emphasizes jeeraka for postpartum milk support and uterine recovery — consistent galactagogue application across Indian subcontinent tradition.

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**No standalone Commission E monograph** (classified under carminative spice combinations); traditional herbal use recognition in EU.

**Level B:**
- **Hypoglycemic (T2DM):** 3 RCTs with consistent positive direction (Zare 2014 n=88; Moradi 2016; additional Iranian-language trial): significant FBG, HbA1c reductions at 75–100 mg/day cumin powder over 8 weeks; no multi-center large RCT
- **Carminative:** Consistent evidence in combination products (overlap with other Apiaceae carminatives); cumin alone: Level C evidence from clinical series

**Level C:**
- Iron bioavailability: Mechanistic studies well-characterized; human balance studies support enhanced absorption; no prospective RCT of cumin for iron deficiency anemia treatment specifically (though mechanistic data is sufficiently strong to warrant dietary recommendation)
- Galactagogue: Traditional Indian postpartum use (jeera ka paani — cumin water) consistent across millions of users; no RCT

**Level D:**
- Anti-tumor: Cuminaldehyde induces apoptosis in some cancer cell lines in vitro; no clinical cancer application
- Neuroprotective: animal models suggest cognitive benefit; no human data

## 7. Phenomenological Record

**The Ebers Papyrus: cumin as foundational Egyptian medicine:**
Cumin (*kmn*) appears in over 60 prescriptions in the Ebers Papyrus — more frequently than almost any herb except beer (used as vehicle), honey, and natron (baking soda). Applications range from compound digestive preparations to wound dressings to eye treatments to pain management — a catholicity of application suggesting cumin was the most versatile aromatic medicine in the ancient Egyptian pharmacopoeia. Given that the Ebers Papyrus documents existing practice over centuries, the cumin record likely extends further back than the document's c. 1550 BCE composition date.

**Biblical tithes and Talmudic law:**
Matthew 23:23 names cumin alongside dill and mint as tithed spices — Temple tribute extracted from agricultural production. The Mishnah (Tractate Maaserot) specifies cumin tithing rules in detail. Talmudic discussions in Tractate Shabbat debate whether grinding cumin on the Sabbath constitutes prohibited "grinding" labor — the debate assumes cumin grinding was so common in first-century Jewish households that it required specific Sabbath law clarification. This level of Talmudic attention establishes cumin as among the most pharmacologically and economically important daily household plants in ancient Judea.

**Cumin and iron: the inadvertent nutritional revolution:**
The integration of cumin into dal, lentil soup, hummus, shakshuka, and virtually every legume preparation across the Middle East, North Africa, and South Asia may represent one of the most consequential dietary pharmacological accidents in human history. Legumes are excellent iron sources but high in phytate (which blocks iron absorption). Cumin reduces phytate binding. The traditional culinary combination — cumin + legumes — thus enhances the iron bioavailability from the primary plant protein source across three continents. Population groups that maintained this dietary pattern (cumin + legumes + fermentation) as staple food culture maintained adequate iron status in diets that modern nutritional science might predict to be iron-deficient. The "accident" was not random — it was empirical selection over generations for food combinations that felt right and maintained health.

**Ibn Sīnā on cumin (Canon Medicinae):**
Avicenna devotes considerable space to kammun in the Canon, distinguishing *kammun* (common cumin), *kammun barri* (wild cumin), and *kammun armani* (Armenian cumin — possibly caraway) with detailed pharmacological differentiation. His description of cumin as "dry and hot in the third degree" and appropriate for "cold conditions of the stomach, liver, and intestines" maps precisely onto its clinical profile — carminative, prokinetic, warming. He specifically recommends cumin dissolved in warm water for intestinal colic and as a topical poultice for joint pain — applications consistent with modern mechanistic data for smooth muscle relaxation and anti-inflammatory activity.

**Jeera ka paani — Indian postpartum ritual:**
Cumin water (*jeera ka paani* — cumin seeds simmered in water, strained, consumed warm) is prescribed daily to postpartum women across Hindu, Muslim, and Jain household traditions throughout the Indian subcontinent, for lactation support, uterine involution, digestive recovery, and iron repletion after childbirth. The iron-repletive application is now pharmacologically comprehensible — cumin water consumed with iron-containing postpartum foods (leafy vegetables, lentils) increases dietary iron absorption during the most iron-vulnerable period in a woman's reproductive life. The traditional prescription thus delivers simultaneous carminative, galactagogue, and iron-bioavailability effects.

## 8. Practical Application

**Therapeutic dose ranges:**

| Form | Dose | Indication |
|------|------|-----------|
| Ground cumin (culinary, in food) | 2–5 g/meal (½–1 tsp per dish) | Carminative; iron bioavailability enhancement (with legumes); hypoglycemic |
| Cumin seed powder (supplemental) | 75–100 mg/day standardized (therapeutic hypoglycemic dose per RCTs) | T2DM adjunct; metabolic syndrome |
| Cumin seed infusion (jeera ka paani) | 1 tsp seeds simmered 10 min in 250 mL water, 1–2×/day | Digestive; galactagogue; postpartum tonic |
| Cumin tincture (1:5) | 1–2 mL TID | Digestive; carminative |
| Cumin essential oil (topical, diluted 2-3%) | Apply to abdomen for colic; careful sun avoidance | Carminative topical; mild analgesic |

**Iron bioavailability dietary recommendation:**
For individuals with dietary iron deficiency (vegetarians, vegans, menstruating women, pregnancy), combining cumin with every legume/grain meal provides a practical, zero-cost, culturally embedded iron absorption enhancer. This represents one of the strongest dietary pharmacological recommendations derivable from the Codex — no supplement needed, only the traditional cumin-legume pairing that billions of people practice daily.

**Contraindications:**
- **Pregnancy (high medicinal doses):** Emmenagogue and galactagogue activity at high doses; culinary cooking amounts safe; medicinal supplement doses avoid in first trimester
- **Apiaceae allergy:** Cross-reactivity potential
- **Surgery:** Cumin extract shown to inhibit platelet aggregation in animal models; discontinue supplemental forms 2 weeks before elective surgery (culinary amounts low risk)

**Drug interactions:**
- Antidiabetic medications: additive hypoglycemic; monitor blood glucose; adjust dosing
- Antiplatelet/anticoagulant: theoretical additive at high supplemental doses; clinical evidence lacking; culinary amounts low risk

**Safety profile:**
Culinary use: GRAS; extremely safe; consumed daily by approximately 2–3 billion people globally. Therapeutic powder doses (75–100 mg/day): well tolerated in RCTs; no serious adverse events reported. Essential oil: avoid oral concentrated oil; topical diluted use safe.

**Energetic tier:** ◉ Qi (digestive/carminative primary); secondary ◈ Jing (iron bioavailability enhancement supports constitutional blood building)  
**Optimal form:** Ground cumin in daily cooking with legumes for iron-bioavailability and carminative dietary pharmacology; cumin seed powder at 75–100 mg/day for T2DM metabolic adjunct; jeera ka paani for postpartum/galactagogue traditional use
