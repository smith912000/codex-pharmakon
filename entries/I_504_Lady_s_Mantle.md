# I-504 | Lady's Mantle (*Alchemilla vulgaris* L. agg.)

**Volume I — Plant-Based Substances**
**Family:** Rosaceae (subfamily Rosoideae)
**Energetic Tier:** ◈ Jing (tissue repair, hemostasis, gynecological toning) primary | ◉ Qi (anti-inflammatory, astringent) secondary

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary binomial:** *Alchemilla vulgaris* L. sensu lato (aggregate species complex); *Alchemilla xanthochlora* Rothm. = European Pharmacopoeia reference species

**Synonyms & historical names:**
- *Alchemilla pratensis* Opiz
- *Alchemilla acutiloba* Opiz
- Numerous microspecies within the aggregate (*vulgaris* agg. = 100+ apomictic microspecies in Europe)

**Common names:**
- English: Lady's mantle, lion's foot, bear's foot, nine hooks
- German: *Frauenmantel* (lady's mantle — primary European name); *Löwenfuß* (lion's foot)
- French: *Alchémille*, *Pied-de-lion*
- Italian: *Alchemilla*, *Erba stellaria*
- Spanish: *Alquimila*, *Pie de leon*
- Latin: *Alchimilla* — the genus name derives from Arabic *al-kīmiyā* (alchemy) — Arabic alchemists valued the dew collected in the cupped leaves for use in alchemical preparations
- Chinese: 羽衣草 (*yǔ yī cǎo*, "feathered garment grass") — not in primary Chinese pharmacopoeias

**Etymology and alchemical tradition:**
*Alchemilla* derives directly from the Arabic alchemical tradition: the cupped, pleated leaves collect and hold dewdrops and rainwater in a remarkable manner — capillary action in the hydrophobic leaf hairs creates perfect spherical droplets. Arabic alchemists believed this dew was "prima materia" — the fundamental substance of transformation — and collected it for philosophical and practical preparations. The genus name preserves this medieval Arab-European alchemical crossover.

**Botanical authentication:**
- Low-growing perennial herb 10–50 cm; kidney-shaped to circular leaves with 7–11 lobes, lobes toothed (serrated), surface pleated/folded when young (like a gathered cloak = "mantle"); characteristic water-collecting cupped leaf surface with hydrophobic surface hairs causing perfect round dew/rain droplets; flowers tiny, greenish-yellow, 4 petals lacking (hypanthium only); no petals — the "flower" consists only of 4 sepals + 4 epicalyx segments; inflorescence loose cyme; apomictic reproduction (seeds without fertilization = all offspring genetically identical to mother = accounts for 100+ microspecies)
- Authentication markers: kidney-shaped pleated leaf + 7–11 toothed lobes + no true petals + water-bead behavior on leaf surface
- Harvest: aerial parts in summer; root in autumn (less commonly used)

**The apomictic species problem:**
The *vulgaris* aggregate consists of 100–300 apomictic microspecies across Europe. The European Pharmacopoeia designates *A. xanthochlora* Rothm. as the pharmacological reference species, but commercial herbal preparations may contain *A. vulgaris* s.s., *A. mollis* (ornamental; escaped), or other microspecies. The tannin and flavonoid profiles are broadly similar across the aggregate, supporting the pharmacopoeia's practical grouping under *vulgaris* agg.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Lady's mantle's pharmacological profile emerges primarily from its exceptionally high tannin content — some of the highest ellagitannin concentrations in any medicinal herb. The wound healing, hemostatic, and anti-inflammatory properties are mechanistically clear. The gynecological tradition is more complex: the ellagitannin fraction has demonstrated uterine smooth muscle toning effects in animal models; the astringent action reduces excessive mucosal bleeding; anti-inflammatory mechanisms reduce menstrual cramps; and phytoestrogenic effects (weak, via flavonoids) may modulate cycle regularity.

The alchemical tradition associated with dew collection is pharmacologically irrelevant — the collected dew contains none of the plant's active constituents and was used in philosophical rather than therapeutic practice. However, the cultural framing of the plant as "lady's" (in Arabic, German, English, French simultaneously) across independent naming traditions suggests a consistent cultural recognition of its gynecological applications — the "lady" in all these languages encoding the primary patient population, not merely botanical description.

The pleated, cupped leaf that gave rise to the alchemical name also functions as a natural water reservoir in rain, providing a microhabitat for insects and a distinctive visual identity that made the plant easy to recognize, cultivate, and document across European herbal literature from the medieval period onward.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Ellagitannins (dominant, defining constituents):**
- Agrimoniin: 2–8% DW (same compound as in agrimony I-502 and meadowsweet I-503 — a recurrent Rosaceae ellagitannin)
- Pedunculagin: 1–5% DW
- Laevigatin B: 0.5–2% DW
- Tellimagrandin I and II: 0.5–2% DW
- Total hydrolyzable tannins: 6–8% DW (PhEur marker)
- Condensed proanthocyanidins (catechin-based): 2–4% DW
- **Total tannin content:** up to 10–15% DW in some assays — among highest tannin-containing Rosaceae entries in this Codex
- Ellagic acid: hydrolysis product

**Flavonoids:**
- Quercetin-3-glucoside, quercetin-3-galactoside (hyperoside): 0.3–1.5% DW
- Kaempferol-3-glucoside: 0.2–0.8% DW
- Luteolin glycosides: minor
- Myricetin derivatives: trace
- Phytoestrogenic potential: weak (kaempferol, quercetin interact with ERβ; insufficient for clinical phytoestrogenic designation)

**Phenolic acids:**
- Ellagic acid: significant (tannin hydrolysis)
- Caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid: minor

**Saponins:**
- Trace triterpene saponins

**Sterols:**
- β-sitosterol: trace

**Essential oil:** <0.1% DW; minor terpenes; not pharmacologically significant at standard doses

**PhEur standardization:** *Alchemillae herba* — ≥6.0% tannins expressed as pyrogallol (hydrolyzable tannin hydrolysis product). This is one of the highest PhEur tannin content requirements for any herbal drug.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Astringent/hemostatic:**
Ellagitannins (agrimoniin, pedunculagin, tellimagrandin) precipitate surface proteins on mucous membranes and wound surfaces, forming protein-tannin complexes that:
- Contract wound margins (mechanical hemostasis)
- Reduce capillary permeability (anti-hemorrhagic)
- Create antimicrobial barrier
- Provide anti-inflammatory protection of wound bed

These mechanisms directly support menorrhagia (reduced uterine bleeding), wound healing, and pharyngeal anti-inflammatory applications.

**Uterine smooth muscle toning (ellagitannin mechanism):**
Animal studies (rat uterus *ex vivo*) demonstrate that *A. vulgaris* extract increases uterine smooth muscle tone without causing spasm — a "toning" rather than "contracting" effect. The mechanism is ellagitannin-mediated protein cross-linking at the cell surface level. This pharmacological property explains the traditional use for uterine prolapse support and menorrhagia better than simple astringency alone.

**Anti-inflammatory:**
- Quercetin, kaempferol: COX-2 inhibition, PGE₂ suppression; relevant to dysmenorrhea
- Ellagic acid: NF-κB pathway inhibition
- Combined anti-inflammatory effect reduces menstrual cramping; consistent with traditional use

**Wound healing (collagen-stabilizing):**
Ellagitannins cross-link collagen fibers (same mechanism as vegetable leather tanning), improving wound tensile strength and accelerating epithelialization. This is mechanistically identical to the collagen cross-linking in industrial leather production — the same condensed tannin chemistry.

**Antioxidant:**
High total polyphenol content → exceptional DPPH radical scavenging; likely contributes to preservation of wound tissue and anti-inflammatory antioxidant activity.

**Antimicrobial:**
- Ellagitannins: membrane disruption of *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Streptococcus pyogenes* (relevant for topical wound infection)
- Ellagic acid: antifungal vs *Candida albicans*
- Phenolic acids: broad-spectrum antimicrobial

**Mild phytoestrogenic modulation:**
- Kaempferol and quercetin: weak ERβ agonism; insufficient for definitive phytoestrogenic classification but may contribute to cycle normalization at whole-herb doses over extended use

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Menorrhagia/menstrual irregularity — Level C:**
- Commission E Monograph (1986): APPROVED for menorrhagia, unspecific diarrhea, mild inflammation of oral and pharyngeal mucosa, wounds, burns
- EMA/HMPC (2008): traditional use for menorrhagia, premenstrual symptoms; also traditional use for diarrhea and topical wounds
- Multiple uncontrolled European clinical case series positive; no formal RCT
- Mechanism strongly supports traditional use; Level C evidence is robust; Level A absent

**Perimenopausal symptoms — Level C:**
- Traditional European record of use in perimenopausal transition; mechanism plausible via weak phytoestrogenic + anti-inflammatory combination
- No formal trials

**Diarrhea — Level C (Commission E, same as agrimony I-502):**
- High tannin content → anti-diarrheal mechanism identical to agrimony and meadowsweet; Commission E approved; EMA traditional use
- Consistent with Rosaceae family-wide ellagitannin anti-diarrheal activity pattern

**Wound healing (topical) — Level C:**
- Traditional European wound herb; burns, abrasions, perineal wounds; consistent traditional record
- No modern RCTs; mechanism established; traditional use monograph appropriate

**Topical vaginal use (clinical series) — Level B:**
- Shrivastava R 2006 (*Phytomedicine* 13:388): pilot study; *A. vulgaris* vaginal gel formulation in women with cervical ectopia; significant regression of ectopic area after 4 weeks vs untreated control; mechanism: tannin-mediated epithelial repair
- Significant finding; small trial; not replicated in large RCT; methodological quality concerns

**Diabetes (preclinical) — Level D:**
- Multiple rodent studies: blood glucose reduction; mechanism unclear; no clinical trials
- Not a clinical recommendation

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The dew and alchemical imagination:**
The ability of *Alchemilla* leaves to hold perfect spherical water droplets — appearing as liquid mercury beads on a waterproofed surface — captivated medieval alchemists. The early 16th-century Swiss alchemist Paracelsus wrote extensively about this "celestial water" or "dew of lady's mantle," believing it concentrated cosmic virtue. The name *al-kīmiyā* itself — the Arabic origin of "alchemy" — is preserved in the genus name, making *Alchemilla* the botanical whose very name encodes the Western alchemical tradition.

The hydrophobic surface is mechanistically produced by microscopic trichomes (leaf hairs) coated in wax — a genuine superhydrophobic surface structure that anticipates modern biomimetic materials science. The Lotus effect (named for *Nelumbo nucifera*) and the Salvinia effect both describe analogous biological superhydrophobicity, but *Alchemilla* may have been the first plant to attract systematic Western scientific attention to the phenomenon — via alchemical observation rather than materials science.

**The "woman's herb" across cultures:**
In European folk medicine, no plant was more consistently associated with female physiology than lady's mantle. German *Frauenmantel*, Arabic *kaf Maryam* (Mary's palm), English "lady's mantle," French *alchémille* (used by women herbalists) — all position this plant in explicit relationship to women's bodies. The medieval European midwife tradition used lady's mantle decoctions for post-partum bleeding, menorrhagia, and perineal repair; the plant is documented in nearly every European herbal written by a woman or for women from the 12th century onward. Hildegard of Bingen (1098–1179) recommended it for skin conditions and gynecological complaints in *Physica*.

**The pleated leaf as botanical garment:**
The young leaves of *Alchemilla* emerge from the bud tightly folded and pleated, opening over days to reveal the cupped kidney shape. This expansion from folded bud to open cup was described by medieval botanists as resembling the unfolding of a cloth garment — hence "mantle" (cloak). The visual metaphor is strikingly apt and represents one of the more poetic botanical vernacular designations, relating a plant's developmental biology to its primary traditional use patient (women wearing cloaks/mantles = the "ladies").

**Nine hooks — the northern European name:**
The alternative English name "nine hooks" refers to the nine toothed lobes of the leaf, each lobe resembling a curved hook. This is both morphologically accurate and potentially pharmacologically encoded — the "nine" being an archaic European sacred number, and the hook form being interpreted as "grasping" or "holding" (consistent with the hemostatic, "holding the blood" function in menorrhagia).

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Standard preparations and doses:**
- **Tea/infusion:** 3–5 g dried aerial herb per 250 mL water; steep 10–15 minutes; 3–4× daily; for menstrual irregularity, menorrhagia, diarrhea
- **Tincture (1:5, 25% ethanol):** 1.5–4 mL 3× daily; higher flavonoid extraction
- **Cold maceration:** 10 g per 500 mL cold water, overnight; superior mucilaginous extraction; for vaginal wash, wound wash, sitz bath
- **Sitz bath/topical:** 20–30 g dried herb decoction per 5 liters warm water; for perineal healing, vaginal inflammation, hemorrhoids
- **Vaginal douche (traditional):** strong decoction cooled; for cervicitis, vaginal irritation, vaginal discharge

**Commission E approved (1986):**
- Internal: menorrhagia, mild nonspecific diarrhea, mild inflammation of oral/pharyngeal mucosa
- External: wounds, burns (mild), skin inflammation

**EMA traditional use (2008):** Menorrhagia, PMS; mild diarrhea; topical wounds; all as traditional use (not well-established use) classification.

**Gynecological application guidance:**
- Begin 1–2 weeks before expected period for cycle regulation (consistent with phytoestrogenic lag + toning effect onset)
- For acute menorrhagia: tea or tincture at onset; 3× daily during flow
- Post-partum perineal care: sitz bath (gentle) from day 3 post-delivery
- Prolapse support (traditional): internal + sitz bath combined; long-term maintenance therapy (months); tones pelvic floor ligament connective tissue via collagen cross-linking mechanism

**CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- Pregnancy: mild uterotonic properties (traditional and mechanistic basis); avoid internal use in pregnancy
- Lactation: insufficient data; avoid internal use
- Hypersensitivity to Rosaceae (rare cross-reactivity)

**Drug interactions:**
- Tannin-mediated drug binding: separate from iron supplements, alkaloid medications, and certain antibiotics by 2 hours
- Warfarin: theoretical interaction via tannin effects; monitor if combined (unlikely to be clinically significant at standard doses)

**Combination use (traditional):**
- Yarrow (*Achillea millefolium*) + lady's mantle: classical European gynecological combination for menorrhagia and cycle regulation; additive hemostatic + anti-inflammatory mechanisms; widely used in European phytotherapy
- Raspberry leaf (*Rubus idaeus*) + lady's mantle: uterine toning combination; often recommended in second/third trimester of pregnancy for birth preparation (consult midwife; evidence limited but traditional practice widespread in European obstetric nursing)

**Laboratory monitoring for menorrhagia:**
Lady's mantle does not address the cause of menorrhagia; patients should be evaluated for uterine pathology (fibroids, endometrial hyperplasia, coagulation disorders) before long-term herbal management. Symptomatic improvement with lady's mantle does not preclude underlying pathology.
