# Krauncha flesh — Krauncha, flesh of a bird of the swimming-bird (*Plava*) group, Sushruta Samhita
## *The flesh of the bird Bhishagratna prints as "Krauncha" (the chapter's OCR "Kraimcha") and glosses as "heron" in Kalpasthana ch.1; his Vol. 3 glossary reads* Ardea jaculator, *"A kind of heron", by column order* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The Krauncha is named in the "Meat Group" of *Sutrasthana* ch.46, the chapter on food and drink (*Annapana-Vidhi-madhyaya*), in Bhishagratna's English (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt`, line 738, where the OCR prints "Kraimcha"; `_sources/sushruta.csv` line 252 lists "Krauncha", source "Sutrasthana ch.46 (Annapanavidhi); Bhishagratna trans. vol.1", flagged identity-uncertain). The chapter says the flesh of animals that are aquatic, marsh-dwelling, village-dwelling, carnivorous, unbifurcated-hoofed or high-ground "is generally used as food", each succeeding kind superior to the one before it (lines 385-394). The marsh-dwellers (*Anupa*) fall into five groups (lines 659-669), and the second is the *Plavas*, "divers or swimmers". The Krauncha is the third bird named in that group: "Birds such as the Hansa, Sarasa, Kraimcha [Krauncha], Chakravaka, Kurura ... belong to the Plava family. These birds are found to move about in large flocks" (lines 732-745; names omitted where the OCR is damaged).

The text gives qualities for the group, not for this bird by name. "The flesh of any one of this family is cooling, demulcent, and spermatopoietic and destroys the deranged Vayu. It proves beneficial in cases of haemoptysis, is sweet in taste and of digestion, and is possessed of laxative and diuretic properties" (lines 747-751). The next sentence is about the Hansa alone (see III_234); there is none for the Krauncha.

Elsewhere the name occurs in three places. *Kalpasthana* ch.1, in the signs by which poisoned food is detected: "The note of the cuckoo becomes hoarse and a Krauncha (heron) becomes excited" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/kalpa/SS_Kalpa_ch01.txt`, lines 175-177). *Chikitsasthana* ch.7, on urinary calculus (*Asmari*): "The bones of the Krauncha, camel and ass, as well as the drugs known as Svadamshtra, Talamula, Ajamoda, Kadamba roots and Nagara pounded together and administered through the vehicle of wine (Sura) or hot water, leads to the disintegration of Sarkara (gravel)" (`SS_Chikitsa_ch07.txt`, lines 98-102). *Uttaratantra* ch.39 (fever) says that "several authorities" do not recommend "the meat of Sarasa, Krouncha, Mayuta (peacock), Kukkuta (cock) and of Tittira in cases of fever, owing to its heaviness (as regards digestion), as well as to its heat-making potency", and that the translator subscribes to this "with a certain limitation": the flesh may be given in moderate quantity at the proper time if the fever has a preponderance of deranged *Vayu* (`SS_Uttara_ch39.txt`, lines 908-917). Two other uses of the spelling are different things: in *Uttaratantra* ch.58 (suppression of urine), a compound includes "seeds of lotus (Krouncha)", with a footnote that "Dallana explains Krounchasthi as bones of a Krouncha bird, but we are not inclined to accept this explanation" (`SS_Uttara_ch58.txt`, lines 218-224, 249-250); and in ch.28 an invocation mentions a god who "pierced with a single shaft right through the heart of the mountain Krouncha" (`SS_Uttara_ch28.txt`, lines 73-76).

**Identity.** The translator glosses the Krauncha as "heron" in *Kalpasthana* ch.1, and his Vol. 3 glossary (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`, name at line 30653) prints "Krauncha (Ardea jaculator)" and, in a separate OCR column, "A kind of heron"; the pairing is by column order. The ch.46 group is of swimming birds, and for the lotus-seed compound in ch.58 the translator rejects the commentator Dallana's reading of "Krouncha" as a bird's bones. Which bird the Krauncha is, and whether the "bones of the Krauncha" in ch.7 are a bird's, rests on the translator's glosses. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Wild waterbird flesh is mostly water and muscle protein, with fat that varies by species, and supplies iron and B vitamins. Bone is mainly calcium phosphate and collagen. No analysis of the bird Sushruta calls Krauncha was read, and its species is not settled. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced record is a name in a list of swimming birds with the group's qualities, one authorities' caution against its flesh in fever, and a bone powder for urinary gravel. The Codex gives no protocol and recommends no use for gravel, fever or any other condition. The bird is not identified with confidence; herons and cranes are protected by wildlife law in many countries; and wild waterbirds are a recognised reservoir of avian influenza viruses. A reader who eats no meat has nothing to adopt.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations the texts give are the bones powdered together with Svadamshtra, Talamula, Ajamoda, Kadamba roots and Nagara and given in wine or hot water for gravel (*Chikitsasthana* ch.7, lines 98-102), and the flesh in moderate quantity in a *Vayu*-type fever (*Uttaratantra* ch.39, lines 908-917). No quantity or course is printed. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Wild waterbirds can carry avian influenza viruses and enteric bacteria such as *Salmonella* and *Campylobacter*, and thorough cooking with hygienic handling is the standard precaution. Bone powder is not a recognised treatment for urinary calculi in current medicine. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Sushruta (SS).** Printed in *Sutrasthana* ch.46 for the *Plava* group generally (lines 747-751): cooling; demulcent; spermatopoietic; destroys deranged *Vayu*; beneficial in haemoptysis; sweet in taste and digestion; laxative and diuretic. In *Uttaratantra* ch.39 the Krauncha is among birds "authorities" call heavy and heat-making (lines 908-913). The two statements pull apart (cooling for the group, heat-making in the fever passage); the file gives no reconciliation and both are recorded as printed. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The Krauncha is named in the food-and-drink chapter, whose opening lines tie food as a class to bodily strength, complexion and *ojas* (ch.46, lines 9-12). That is a claim about food, not about this flesh, and the bird has no sentence of its own there. Its other places are a poison-detection sign, a gravel remedy and a fever caution, none a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* chapter; no line read gives it that status. No *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* claim is made for it. *Sattvic*, *rajasic* and *tamasic* food lists are *Samkhya*-era material, not Sushruta, and are not used.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice. In Sushruta the Krauncha is a name in a list of water birds, a heavy flesh to be careful of in fever, and a bone powder for gravel. A cultivator can take only the caution, and with the bird unidentified there is no reason to seek it out.

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** wild waterbirds can carry avian influenza viruses and enteric bacteria; do not handle sick or dead wild birds, and cook any flesh through.
- **The text's own caution:** the flesh is called heavy and heat-making and is not advised in fever by "several authorities", with a limited exception (*Uttaratantra* ch.39).
- **Urinary calculi:** bone powder in wine is not a substitute for medical assessment of kidney stones or urinary obstruction.
- **Identity:** the bird is not fixed in the text; heron is the translator's gloss. Do not substitute on this basis. [USER: verify]
- **Legal and conservation status:** herons, cranes and other waterbirds are protected in many countries; status for any candidate species was not checked. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics and religion:** killing an animal for food or medicine is a moral question for many traditions; this entry takes no position beyond recording the text.
