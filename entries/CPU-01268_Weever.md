# Weever (sea dragon fish) — DRAKON THALASSIOS, "the sea dragon" (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 2-15)
## *The sea dragon, which the translation's gloss gives as Trachinidae, "spiny-finned fishes, Weevers"; the text itself says only "the sea dragon" and speaks of its "prickles"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Chapter 2-15 of *De Materia Medica*, headed DRAKON THALASSIOS, stands in Book 2 among the "Living Creatures" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 4976-4978; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 205, which records the translator's identification "Trachinidae — Spiny-finned fishes, Weevers"). The whole chapter is one sentence: "The sea dragon (opened and applied) is a cure for the hurt done by his prickles." So the fish is cut open and laid on the wound its own spines have made. No place of capture, season, quality, quantity or length of application is given, and the text does not say which of the fish's parts is applied, only that the animal is opened.

The chapter is short, but the sea dragon is named in two other places in the same edition, each time as the creature that does the harm and not as the remedy. In 2-24 (TRIGLA, the red mullet) the mullet "if often eaten is thought to cause dullness of sight, but cut apart whilst it is raw and applied it heals the hurt caused by the sea dragon [2-15], scorpion, and the spider" (line 5020). In 2-99 (PERI OURON, urine) a man's own water is "applied with hot cloths for the bites of sea vipers, sea scorpions, and sea dragons [2-15]" (line 5379). The sea dragon therefore appears in the book in three roles: as its own remedy (2-15), as a wound-giver cured by another fish (2-24), and as a wound-giver cured by urine in hot cloths (2-99). The same like-for-like pattern is stated for a land animal in 2-73: "The shrewmouse (cut apart and applied) is a remedy for its own bites" (line 5232). The index of this edition lists further pages for the sea dragon and for "bites of the sea dragon" that I did not trace in the text; my search of the English found the three places above. ### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, the weevers (family Trachinidae) are small fish with venomous spines on the first dorsal fin and on the gill covers; the venom is a mixture of proteins that causes intense local pain and swelling. A fish's flesh is mostly water and muscle protein with fat. Dioscorides' remedy applies the opened fish, not any extracted substance, and nothing links a constituent of the fish to the effect he claims.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records the opened sea dragon applied to the sting of its own spines. The Codex does not recommend it. A sting from a weever is a painful venomous injury that can become infected and needs prompt first aid and medical advice, and a fish laid on the wound adds bacteria. The entry exists to record what Dioscorides says. It is not a stinging-fish protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation is the one quoted: the sea dragon "opened and applied". No dose, quantity, time or repetition is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Weevers are venomous fish found in shallow sandy water, where bathers can tread on them, and the pain from their spines is severe and is managed by clinicians and by first aid. Their spines can still sting after the fish is dead, so handling one needs care. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM):** None is printed. Chapter 2-15 gives no quality (warming, cooling, drying, astringent) and no taste for the sea dragon.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. The Galenic three spirits are later than Dioscorides. What he offers a practitioner is a discipline of attention (provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, adulteration detection), and this chapter has none of it: it names no place of capture, gives no mark of a good fish and no test, and does not say how long the fish is to be applied. The one habit it models is cross-reference: the other chapters that name the sea dragon (2-24 and 2-99) point back to 2-15 in square brackets, so a reader can follow the creature as harm and as remedy. Recording is not endorsing, and nothing in the chapter is amuletic. No cultivation status is assigned.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The sea dragon is a one-line wound remedy in a book of "living creatures", and the Codex does not recommend it. The most useful thing for a cultivator to take from the chapter is to avoid the hazard: the practical lesson is to avoid the sting in the first place. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Contraindications

- **Stings:** a weever sting needs first aid and medical advice, especially if pain spreads, breathing or heart rate change, or the wound is deep.
- **Infection:** do not lay a raw or opened fish on a puncture wound; it can introduce bacteria and leave spine fragments in the tissue.
- **Handling:** the spines of a dead or landed fish can still sting.
- **Conservation and ethics:** the Codex does not recommend taking wild fish for this use.
