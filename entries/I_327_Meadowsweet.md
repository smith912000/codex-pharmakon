# I-327: Filipendula ulmaria (Meadowsweet)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-327 | Rosaceae**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Filipendula ulmaria* (L.) Maxim.
**Synonyms:** *Spiraea ulmaria* L. — the old genus name *Spiraea* is pharmacologically significant (see below)
**Family:** Rosaceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Meadowsweet, Queen of the Meadow, Bridewort, Meadow Wort, Dolloff
- German: *Echtes Mädesüß* ("true meadow-sweet"), *Wiesenkönigin* ("queen of the meadow")
- French: *Reine des prés* ("queen of the meadows")
- Welsh: *Llysiau'r frenines* ("queen's herb")
- Latin: *Filipendula* — from *filum* (thread) + *pendulus* (hanging) — referring to root tubers hanging by threads; *ulmaria* — resembling elm leaves (*Ulmus*)
- Arabic: *Ushbāt al-Murūj* (أشبات المروج) — "meadow herb"

**Etymology:**
The old genus name *Spiraea ulmaria* is pharmacologically significant because **the word "aspirin" derives from this plant**. Salicylic acid was first chemically synthesized from *Spiraea ulmaria* by Felix Hoffmann at Bayer in 1897 (though the chemical had been isolated earlier by other chemists). Bayer named the new drug **"aspirin"** — **"a"** (from acetyl) + **"spir"** (from *Spiraea*) + **"in"** (common pharmaceutical suffix). *Spiraea* derives from Greek *speira* (coil/twist) — the twisted seedpods. Every time the word "aspirin" is used, it contains a botanical reference to meadowsweet/spiraea in its third and fourth letters.

**The Aspirin Paradox:**
Meadowsweet contains **salicylates** (methyl salicylate, salicin, salicylaldehyde) — precursors and analogs of the salicylic acid used to synthesize aspirin. Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) was developed as a more GI-tolerable form of salicylic acid. The paradox: aspirin (derived chemically from the salicylate pathway discovered in meadowsweet) can CAUSE gastric ulceration and GI bleeding, while **meadowsweet itself traditionally PROTECTS the gastric mucosa**. The mucilage and tannins in whole meadowsweet appear to buffer the gastric irritation from salicylates. This whole-plant buffering effect was lost when pure salicylic acid and aspirin were isolated.

**Morphology:**
Perennial; 60–120 cm; erect; pinnate leaves with alternating large and small leaflets; distinctive almond-cream scented dense fluffy panicles of tiny cream flowers; riverbanks, wet meadows, fens; characteristic almond-vanilla scent from salicylates + benzaldehyde.

**Parts Used:** Aerial parts (flowers + leaves) — Commission E standard; harvested at or just before full flowering.

**Salicylate Tradition — Meadowsweet in Context:**
Meadowsweet's cross-traditional convergence is LOWER than willow bark (I-next) despite sharing the salicylate pharmacological class, because meadowsweet is a European plant occupying wet meadows rather than the broad-distribution riparian habitat of willows. Willow achieved broader traditional documentation because *Salix* species are present on every continent. Meadowsweet was locally important in Celtic, Anglo-Saxon, and Nordic traditions and was among the sacred herbs of the Druids (documented in classical sources as strewn over floors — the source of "strewing herb" tradition in medieval England).

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domains:**

1. **Analgesic / anti-inflammatory (salicylate)** — European folk; AT; Commission E; classic salicylate mechanism; **Level B**
2. **Anti-ulcer / gastroprotective** — European folk; Commission E; tannin + mucilage mechanism; **Level B** (paradoxical: salicylate source that protects stomach)
3. **Diuretic** — European folk; Commission E; flavonoid mechanism
4. **Astringent / anti-diarrheal** — tannin mechanism; traditional

**The Gastroprotective-Salicylate Duality:**
Meadowsweet's pharmacological profile represents one of the most elegant examples of **whole-plant buffering** in the Codex. The plant contains:
- **Salicylates** (anti-inflammatory; analgesic; antipyretic)
- **Mucilage polysaccharides** (gastroprotective; coating)
- **Tannins** (astringent; gastroprotective)
- **Flavonoids** (anti-inflammatory; anti-ulcer; gastroprotective)

The mucilage and tannins appear to protect the GI mucosa from the GI irritant effects of the salicylates — creating a self-buffered combination that is more GI-tolerable than pure aspirin. Commission E approves meadowsweet specifically for "heartburn and gastritis" — the exact indications that aspirin worsens. This whole-plant gastroprotection is not replicable by adding mucilage to aspirin because the molecular interactions within the whole plant matrix differ from simple mixture.

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Salicylate Compounds

**1. Methyl Salicylate (EO) — PRIMARY volatile**
- Characteristic wintergreen-almond odor
- Topical counter-irritant; mild analgesic
- **NOT primarily aspirin-like systemically** — methyl salicylate requires ester hydrolysis to salicylic acid

**2. Salicylaldehyde (EO)** — volatile; analgesic (precursor/adjacent)

**3. Salicin, Salicortin** — glycoside salicylates (as in willow bark); hydrolyzed to salicyl alcohol → salicylic acid in vivo
- Analgesic; anti-inflammatory; antipyretic

**4. Spirein (Rutin derivative)** — flavonoid; anti-inflammatory

### Flavonoids (Anti-Ulcer / Anti-Inflammatory)

**5. Quercetin, Rutin, Hyperoside** — anti-inflammatory; anti-ulcer (gastroprotective); antioxidant; diuretic
**6. Spiraeoside (Quercetin-4-glucoside)** — specific marker compound

### Tannins

**7. Condensed Tannins (Ellagitannins)** — astringent; gastroprotective; anti-diarrheal
- Precipitate proteins on GI mucosa → protective protein-tannin layer

### Mucilage

**8. Polysaccharide mucilage** — gastroprotective; demulcent; complements tannins

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### Salicylate Anti-Inflammatory
- **Salicin/salicortin → intestinal β-glucosidase hydrolysis** → salicyl alcohol → liver oxidation → salicylate → COX-1/2 inhibition (reversible; unlike aspirin's irreversible acetylation)
- **Anti-inflammatory; analgesic; antipyretic** — salicylate pathway
- Weaker and more reversible than aspirin's irreversible COX acetylation → less anti-platelet effect + less GI toxicity than aspirin

### Gastroprotective (Paradoxical)
- **Quercetin → PGE2 synthesis in gastric mucosa** → increased gastric mucosal prostaglandins → gastroprotective (same PGE2 that misoprostol and sucralfate provide pharmaceutically)
- **Tannins → protein precipitation on gastric mucosa** → protective barrier layer
- **Mucilage → physical coating** → reduced acid contact with epithelium
- **Combined:** salicylate anti-inflammatory + flavonoid gastroprotective + tannin barrier + mucilage coating = net gastroprotective profile despite salicylate content

### Diuretic
- **Quercetin + rutin → flavonoid aquaresis** (standard mechanism)

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**GI / Gastroprotective:**
- German Commission E approved: meadowsweet for "heartburn and gastritis"
- Animal studies: meadowsweet extract reduces ethanol and HCl-induced gastric ulceration
- Clinical: open-label studies supporting gastroprotective use
- **Evidence Grade: Level C** (Commission E approved; preclinical support)

**Anti-Inflammatory:**
- Commission E: "supportive therapy for cold-type illnesses" (analgesic/anti-pyretic reference)
- Traditional use for rheumatic pain; salicylate mechanism plausible
- No dedicated large RCTs for meadowsweet anti-inflammatory indication
- **Evidence Grade: Level C** (traditional; Commission E; mechanism-supported)

**Antimicrobial:**
- Salicylaldehyde and methyl salicylate active against multiple pathogens in vitro
- **Evidence Grade: Level D**

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Aspirin Name:**
The entire global history of aspirin — 100+ years, billions of doses, dozens of indications from pain relief to MI prevention to colorectal cancer prevention — carries the name of meadowsweet embedded in its third syllable: a**sp**irin. Every prescription, every over-the-counter bottle, every clinical trial paper contains this invisible botanical reference. The plant that gave aspirin its name is now rarely mentioned in pharmacology curricula. The chemical was separated from the plant, rendered more potent and more toxic, and the plant was forgotten behind the pharmaceutical. Meadowsweet's contribution to modern medicine is one of the most widely used but least acknowledged botanical origins in pharmacological history.

**The Strewing Herb:**
Medieval English households strewed sweet-smelling herbs over earthen and stone floors — meadowsweet was one of the most prized strewing herbs (Queen Elizabeth I reportedly favored it). The aromatic volatile compounds (methyl salicylate, benzaldehyde, salicylaldehyde) masked odors, provided mild insect deterrence, and contributed antimicrobial vapor to the air. The practice of floor-strewing was one of the oldest indoor aromatherapy/air-treatment traditions in European culture — functional pharmacology disguised as air freshening.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Commission E Standard:**
- Dried aerial parts (flowers + leaves): 4–6 g daily; tea 2–3 g per 250 mL boiling water; steep 10 minutes; cover while steeping (salicylate volatiles escape with steam)
- 3 cups daily for GI indications; 3× daily for anti-inflammatory

**Tincture:**
- 1:5 in 25% ethanol; 2–4 mL 3× daily

**Topical (Methyl Salicylate):**
- Ointments containing meadowsweet EO (methyl salicylate dominant); counter-irritant for musculoskeletal pain

### Safety Profile

**Good safety profile. Significantly more GI-tolerable than aspirin despite salicylate content.**

**Aspirin/Salicylate Allergy:**
- **ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION in aspirin-sensitive patients** — cross-reactivity with meadowsweet salicylates
- Salicylate allergy → anaphylaxis risk with meadowsweet
- Asthma patients sensitized to aspirin: AVOID meadowsweet (aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease)

**Anticoagulants:**
- Salicylates → anti-platelet effect (reversible COX-1 inhibition) + additive with aspirin/warfarin
- Monitor INR with anticoagulant co-use; separate from aspirin

**Pregnancy:**
- Salicylates: theoretical fetal risk (premature ductus arteriosus closure) at high doses; similar concerns to aspirin
- AVOID medicinal doses in pregnancy, especially third trimester

**Children — Reye's Syndrome:**
- Salicylates in children with viral illness → Reye's syndrome (aspirin warning)
- AVOID meadowsweet in children with viral illness (fever, chickenpox, flu)
- Same restriction as aspirin for pediatric use during viral illness

**Drug Interactions:**
- Anticoagulants (warfarin, aspirin): additive anti-platelet + anticoagulant → bleeding risk
- NSAIDs: additive GI effects at high doses
- Antidiabetics: salicylates may mask hypoglycemia

**Contraindications:**
- Aspirin/salicylate allergy or intolerance (ABSOLUTE)
- Children with viral illness (Reye's syndrome risk)
- Pregnancy (third trimester especially)
- Active gastric ulcer: despite gastroprotective profile, caution at high doses
