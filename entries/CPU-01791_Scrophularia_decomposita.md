# Scrophularia decomposita — Yhershingpa, gyer shing pa, "Figwort" (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary by column position; Clark 1995 gyer.shing.pa, line 1066)

## *Scrophularia decomposita, the third scientific name of the "Y" block of the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary, which column order sets against "Yhershingpa" (gyer shing pa) and, by sense, the English "Figwort"; Clark 1995 prints gyer.shing.pa with Scrophularia incisa first and no Scrophularia decomposita* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Sushruta, Ebers and Assyrian text folders on disk for *Scrophularia* found no hit. The Shennong text (Yang 1998) prints the genus once, in the name Xuan Shen "(Radix Scrophulariae Ningpoensis)" at line 2726, a different species given for a Chinese drug, and Yang's note 160 (line 2744) is his modern commentary on it; it is not read as this plant. The Dioscorides text prints *Scrophularia aquatica* and *Scrophularia maior* in a translator's SUGGESTED line for the Greek drug *galiopsis* (line 8890), which are European species and are not read as this plant. The row was added from a printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**One Tibetan drug, two rows.** This entry and **I_1356** (*Scrophularia incisa*, CPU-01792) are written from the same two passages, because the two editions give two different species for one Tibetan name. This entry takes the Men-Tsee-Khang binomial and I_1356 takes Clark's first binomial. Neither is merged into the other, and the Codex does not choose between the species.

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 392) the row is keyed by the **scientific name** "Scrophularia decomposita", with the locus "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 6044)" and the note "Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". Line 6044 prints **three** binomials in one line ("Chrysoplenium camosum Zanthoxylum armatum Scrophularia decomposita"); the key is the **third**, a later name on the line and not the first, and its two neighbours are the first two. The Tibetan name *gyer.shing.pa* is reached from this row by column position, as set out below, and the row of the sibling, in which the Tibetan name *gyer.shing.pa* is the key, is line 165 (Clark 1995, line 1066).

**Identity doubt.** The binomial is set against the Tibetan name by column position only, and the English "Figwort" fits the genus by sense and not by count. Clark's entry for *gyer.shing.pa* (line 1066) prints "Scrophularia incisa: Scrophularia ningpoensis Hemsl: Tournefortia sibirica", so its first two species are other *Scrophularia* and its third is a *Tournefortia* of a different family, and it prints no *Scrophularia decomposita*. The two editions therefore agree on the Tibetan name and on the genus (or on two of Clark's three genera) and differ on the species. Clark's preface says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" (line 83) and that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect" (line 68). The Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note (line 686) says that its botanical terms rest on the information of its own Materia Medica Department, and it names no flora. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "Y" block).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs, and the columns do not hold the same number of entries. The phonetic names are six: "Yakyima" (line 6036), "Yherma" (6037), and, on line 6038, "Yhershingpa Yugu shing Yumo dejin Yungwa". The transliterations are "gya' kyi ma" (6040), "gyer ma" (6041) and, on line 6042, "gyer shing pa yu gu shing yu mo mde 'byin yung ba". The scientific names are six: "Chrysoplenium camosum", "Zanthoxylum armatum" and **"Scrophularia decomposita"** (all on line 6044), "Sambucus adnata" (6045), then "Paraquilegia anemonoides" and "Curcuma longa" (6046). The English names for the block are three (lines 6050 to 6052): "Guinea pepper", "Figwort" and "Turmeric" (the "Dactylorhiza" and "Coriander" of lines 6048 to 6049 belong to the rows above), so the English column cannot be aligned by count. Counted in order, "Yhershingpa" is the third phonetic name, "gyer shing pa" the third transliteration and *Scrophularia decomposita* the third scientific name. The count is checked at both ends of the block, on "Yherma" against *Zanthoxylum armatum* (where Clark's *gyer.ma*, in the line for *gyer.shing.pa* itself, is paired with *Zanthoxylum*) and on "Yungwa" against *Curcuma longa* and "Turmeric". The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position only. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3448).* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225; colophon at line 3583), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428): "Yhershingpa cures hot disorder associated with small pox. Metok Serchhen treats wounds and necrotic channels. Drakya hawo is an antidote against poison and stops dysentery." The printed index lists "Yhershingpa 209" (line 7567). "Hot disorder" and "small pox" are the translators' words, kept as printed. The sentence gives no part used, no preparation and no taste.

*Clark 1995, the same Tibetan name (read through the name, not the binomial).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997), line 1066. The entry follows the entry that ends "Both Codonopsis and Leucas] cure chest fevers and common cold" (line 1065) and is followed by *me.tog.ser.chhen* (line 1067). It reads:

"gyer.shing.pa: Scrophularia incisa: Scrophularia ningpoensis Hemsl: Tournefortia sibirica: [The superior type is dense and black, whereas the inferior type grows in damp areas and has a red petiole, yellow flower and seed similar to Zanthoxylum (gyer.ma).] It cures [all types of] pox-fever."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The two types and their description are therefore the commentary layer, and so is "all types of". Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the three Latin names, which are Clark's identifications, and "It cures ... pox-fever". Clark's name index lists *gyer.shing.pa* (line 1752) and the Latin index lists *Scrophularia incisa* (line 1644) and *Tournefortia sibirica* (line 1652).

The two editions agree in kind: Clark's drug "cures pox-fever" and the Men-Tsee-Khang *Yhershingpa* "cures hot disorder associated with small pox", where "pox-fever" and "hot disorder associated with small pox" are the two translators' words. The Codex reads *gyer.shing.pa* and *Yhershingpa* as one drug from the spelling and that agreement; neither text says so. The Codex's own list of rGyud-bzhi names (`_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv`, line 289) carries "Yhershingpa" with no botanical name and the identity flagged uncertain. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Scrophularia decomposita*, *Scrophularia incisa*, *gyer.shing.pa* and *Yhershingpa* found no entry on this drug under its own name. **I_1356** (*Scrophularia incisa*) is the sibling written from the same two passages. **I_1326** (*Papaver nudicaule*, *me.tog.ser.chhen*, tag FMT) quotes Clark's line 1067 and the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence on line 3448 for the neighbouring name "Metok serchhen", and so prints the *Yhershingpa* sentence as context. **I_973** (*Zanthoxylum armatum*, tags SS, FMT) reads the same "Y" block for "Yherma" and quotes Clark's line 1066 for the seed "similar to Zanthoxylum". **I_641** (*Scrophularia ningpoensis*, Xuan Shen, tags CM, DMM, SBJ) is the entry on Clark's second binomial in other traditions, and **I_1185** (*Scrophularia aquatica*, tag DMM) and **I_715** (Common Figwort, tags AT, DMM, CM) are entries on European figworts. None is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Scrophularia* is a genus of the figwort family (Scrophulariaceae) whose species commonly carry iridoid glycosides, and that the Chinese Xuan Shen is a different species of the genus; this is a statement about the genus and not about this plant. The lines read give no composition for the Tibetan drug, and nothing is stated here about what it contains. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "cures pox-fever" (Clark, line 1066) and "cures hot disorder associated with small pox" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3448). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only through a glossary count, and they are not Codex protocols. Pox, fever and rash need medical care, and the Codex gives no protocol and no dose for them. The English "Figwort" is the name of European plants as well, and the English name is not evidence that the Tibetan drug is one of them.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used (the commentary layer describes petiole, flower and seed, and names no part as the drug), no quantity, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it cures pox-fever, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in its own words. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names (*gyer.shing.pa*, *Yhershingpa*, *Scrophularia*) found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, colophon at line 3950), and the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence extraction (lines 971, 1043, 1078, 1103, 1121 and 1144) do not include it. Its framing is therapeutic only, and the Chinese Xuan Shen's place in the Shennong text is another tradition's and is not carried over.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a pox-fever drug in one short line of each edition, with no preparation and no dose, and its Latin name is a positional choice among at least three. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Pox and fever need medical care; the identity is open among at least three species, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
