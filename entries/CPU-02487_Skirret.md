# Skirret (water parsnip) — سيون (*sīwun*, as printed; glossed in the text as *qurrat al-ʿayn*, قرة العين)

## *Sion of the Canon: an aromatic water plant that the text itself glosses as qurrat al-ʿayn, jirjir al-maʾ (water rocket) and karafs al-maʾ (water celery); the English name "skirret / water parsnip" is the spine's and is doubtful* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** CM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry.** Named only by Avicenna's *Canon* among the Arabic texts on disk. The locus is *Canon* Book II, entry *sīwun* (سيون), Arabic text line 16054 in `Sources/CM_Avicenna/text/IbnSina_Qanun_OpenITI_Shamela0010706.txt`, three lines long (16055-16057). Dioscorides has a chapter *sion* (2-154) in the English text on disk (line 5859); this entry does not cite it, and any DMM tag would be an editor's addition (see report).

**What the entry says (CM).** "It is *qurrat al-ʿayn*; it is found in standing waters, in it is a fragrance, and it has been spoken of in the chapter of *qāf*" (هو قرة العين يكون في المياه القائمة فيه عطرية وقد قيل فيه في باب القاف; lines 16055-16056). For the excretory organs: "cooked and uncooked it benefits stone, promotes urine, and benefits dysentery" (lines 16056-16057). That is all.

**The entry it points to.** The *qāf* entry *qurrat al-ʿayn* (Arabic text line 17657) says: "it is *jirjīr al-māʾ*, also called *karafs al-māʾ*, fragrant, and its plant is in standing waters" (هو جرجير الماء ويقال له أيضا كرفس الماء وهو عطر الرائحة ونباته في المياه الراكدة; lines 17658-17659); "heating and dissolving" (مسخن محلل; line 17661); it promotes menses and urine and "breaks up the stone in the kidneys, eaten raw or cooked", and helps ulcers of the intestines (lines 17661-17662).

**Identity doubt.** The spine's label "skirret (water parsnip)" is a Latin-name reading of the Greek *sion*. The Canon's own glosses (*jirjīr al-māʾ*, water rocket / cress, and *karafs al-māʾ*, water celery) describe a fragrant plant of still water and do not say parsnip. The English Dioscorides gives *sion* for a water plant with broad leaves "aromatic, eaten boiled or raw to break stones and discharge them", with urine, menses and dysentery, and its translator's list suggests Sium, brooklime and *Sium latifolium* (2-154, lines 5859-5863). The uses agree with Avicenna's, but the species cannot be fixed from the Canon. Identification: low confidence. Compare I_1192 (*Sium angustifolium*) already on the spine.

**Practitioner's note.** A watercress-like plant in the text, used for urinary complaints. There is nothing for a cultivator to do with it except to know what the Canon said.

### Contraindications

Wild plants of still water can carry parasites (liver fluke is a general hazard of raw water plants) and some water-parsnip relatives are poisonous (Apiaceae of wet ground include dangerous species). Because the species is not fixed here, no wild plant should be eaten on this entry's authority. The Canon's uses include promoting menses, so pregnancy is a general concern. [USER: verify]
