# Peucedanum ostruthium — Elaphoboskon, Silphion (Dioscorides 3-80, 3-94)

## *Peucedanum ostruthium, printed on the translators' SUGGESTED lines as "Peucedanum ostrithium, Imperatoria ostrithium — Masterwort, Broad-leaved Hog's Fennel" for 3-80, and as "Laserpitium germanicum, Osteritium [Fuchs], Imperatoria major [Bauhin], Imperatoria ostruthium [Linnaeus], Peucedanum ostruthium [in Sprague]" for 3-94* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM); this entry rests on one text, the Osbaldeston and Wood (2000) English translation of *De Materia Medica*.** The row was added because that text prints the binomial under two chapters. The binomial is the translators' SUGGESTED gloss, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. A search of the Sushruta, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Tibetan text folders for *ostruthium*, *ostrithium*, *Imperatoria* and *Peucedanum ostruthium* found no hit; the Tibetan and Chinese folders hold other *Peucedanum* species (for example the *praeruptorum* of I_1330), which are separate plants. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The row cites two chapters, and they are very different.

*3-80. ELAPHOBOSKON* (heading at line 7237, SUGGESTED line at 7238, the translators' note "produces peucedanin — see 3-92" at 7239, text at 7240 and names at 7241), in Book Three, among the roots of the *akanthoda*. This is the chapter whose SUGGESTED line gives the binomial plainly. It reads: "Elaphoboscum has a knotty stalk similar to libanotis or to marathrum [3-81]. The leaves are two fingers-breadth, very long like terminthos [1-91], broken around in a sharp way. The stalk has very many little sprigs, with pale yellow tufts similar to dill flowers [3-67], and the seed is also similar to dill. The root is about the length of three fingers, the thickness of a finger, white, sweet and edible. The new stalks are eaten [as vegetables] like other herbs. They say that deer having fed on this very herb thereby resist the bites of snakes, as a result the seed is given with wine to those bitten by snakes." The names follow: "Some call it elaphicum, others nephrium, ophigenium, ophioctonon, herpyxe or lyme; the Romans call it cervi ocellum, the Egyptians chemis, and the Africans, ascacau." The "they say" of the deer is Dioscorides', and the only medical use the chapter prints is the seed in wine for snakebite.

*3-94. SILPHION* (heading at line 7383; SUGGESTED lines 7384 to 7385; the translators' note at 7386 reading "There is evidence that the silphium of the ancients was harvested to extinction"; the second block at 7387 reading "[other usage] Silphium laciniatum — Compass Plant"; text at lines 7388 to 7407), among the roots of the *akanthoda*. The text describes the ancient drug *silphion*: "Silphium grows in places around Syria, Armenia, Media and Libya. The stalk, called maspetum, is very like ferula [3-95], but with leaves similar to apium [3-77] and a broad seed. The root is warming, hard to digest, inflative, and hurts the bladder." It then gives a long list of uses of the root, the liquid and the leaves: for scrofulous tumours and nodules in a wax ointment; for sciatica with the ointments *irinum* and *cyprinum*; as an antitoxin in a decoction against "deadly medicines"; for alopecia rubbed on with wine, pepper and vinegar; for toothache, dog bites, scorpion stings, gangrene, carbuncles, corns, lichen and polyps; for a suddenly hoarse voice; for coughs, pleurisy, jaundice, dropsy, chills, tetanus, epilepsy, the menstrual flow, the coeliac complaint, convulsions and hernia; and "it is eaten with vinegar and honey and is good for the arteries, and (especially) for cut-off voices [laryngitis]". The text also records a second plant: "There is said to be another magudaris [gift of the wise man] that grows in Libya, the root of which is similar to silphium but somewhat less thick — sharp, with a loose substance and without juice. It does the same things as silphium." The text does not say that the plant is masterwort or an *Imperatoria*, and the translators' own note says the silphium of the ancients was harvested to extinction. The SUGGESTED line is therefore a Renaissance and later guess that sits beside that remark.

*Names.* 3-80 prints the Greek name *elaphoboskon* ("deer-fodder") with its names above; 3-94 prints "silphion" with "maspetum" for the stalk, "magudarim" for the root and "maspeta" for the leaves ("Some have called the stalk sylphium, the root magudarim, and the leaves maspeta").

**The chapter around them.** 3-92 *PEUKEDANON* (heading at line 7356) has its own SUGGESTED line ("Peucedanum germanicum [Bauhin] Peucedanum officinale [Linnaeus], Selinum officinale ... Hog's Fennel, Sulphur Weed") and is treated in I_1157, which records the translators' pointer to 3-80. 3-95 *SAGAPENON* (line 7408) is the next chapter; its SUGGESTED line prints *Ferula persica*, the binomial of the Codex's I_209.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter ids 3-80 and 3-94, for *elaphoboskon*, *silphion*, *ostruthium* and *Imperatoria* found no entry on either chapter. I_1157 (*Peucedanum officinale*, 3-92) quotes the 3-80 line only as a pointer; I_870 (Agaric) quotes the 3-94 name in a simile; I_209 (Sagapenum, *Ferula persica*) matches the SUGGESTED binomial of the next chapter, 3-95, and was not checked for a chapter citation. Nothing is merged. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The composition of the roots Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, *Peucedanum* is a genus of the carrot family (Apiaceae), a family that commonly carries volatile oils and furanocoumarins, which can make the skin more sensitive to sunlight. The translators' note on 3-80 mentions peucedanin, a compound they attach to *Peucedanum* by name. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Chapter 3-80 records a sweet edible root and edible new stalks, and a seed in wine for snakebite. Chapter 3-94 records the root and juice of *silphion* for a long list of conditions and as a seasoning, and a second plant that "does the same things". The Codex gives no protocol for any of these. The identification of either chapter with masterwort is the translators', and for 3-94 the drug is recorded as harvested to extinction.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these preparations, each with its locus. Chapter 3-80 (line 7240): the seed "given with wine" to those bitten by snakes; the root eaten; the new stalks eaten as vegetables. No measure is given. Chapter 3-94 (lines 7388 to 7407): in a wax ointment, with oil, with pomegranate skin and vinegar, with wine, pepper and vinegar, with hyssop and figs boiled with posca, with honey, with a raw egg, with dry figs, with pepper and frankincense, with pepper and myrrh, with raisins, and in pills with bitter almonds, rue or warm bread. The one quantity printed is the pill: "Having made ten grains of it into a pill give it to swallow to those with tetanus, and to the opisthotonic". The ten grains are as printed (the translators' English), for that use only. No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that the Apiaceae contain both edible and very poisonous members, that no preparation applied to a poisoned arrow wound, an animal bite or a snakebite replaces medical care, and that a plant cannot be safely recognised from an ancient description alone. The identification is a translators' suggestion, and for 3-94 the plant Dioscorides describes is, on the translators' own note, a drug that was harvested to extinction, so the chapters do not show that either describes *Peucedanum ostruthium*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 3-94 prints that the root is "warming, hard to digest, inflative, and hurts the bladder", that the liquid is "inflative and sharp", and that the juice is the most effective part, then the leaves, then the stalk. Chapter 3-80 prints only that the root is "white, sweet and edible". No other warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality is printed for either.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What chapter 3-94 offers is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition, and it is a full one: the best liquid is "somewhat red and transparent (emulating myrrh), and predominant in its smell, neither scented like leek nor unpleasant to taste, and easily changing into a white colour"; the Cyrenian is told from the Median and Syrian by strength and smell; and adulteration is named: "All the juice is adulterated before it is dry, sagapenum or bean meal being mixed with it, which you shall discern by the taste, smell, sight and feel." Chapter 3-80 adds a description by stalk, leaf, tuft, seed and root, and a story of deer. Neither chapter assigns a plant any status beyond that of a food or medicinal root and juice, and the deer story is told with "they say". The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapters gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' method of judging a drug by colour, transparency, smell and taste, and of naming the adulterants (sagapenum, bean meal) that a buyer should test for. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 3-94 itself records that the liquid "causes dullness over your body" in the Cyrenian grade when tasted and that the weaker Median and Syrian grades "have a more poisonous smell"; several of its uses are by mouth (a pill for tetanus), on open wounds (gangrene, bites) or in the eye. Chapter 3-94 also lists the menstrual flow and conditions of the womb among its uses, which is a risk in pregnancy. The carrot family includes some of the most poisonous plants of the temperate flora and some that cause skin reactions in sunlight, and the identification here is a translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
