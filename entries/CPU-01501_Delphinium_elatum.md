# Delphinium elatum — Delphinion eteron (Dioscorides 3-85)

## *Delphinium elatum (the translators' suggestion, their only name on the line), marked by them "250 species in genus — poisonous"; Dioscorides' chapter is "the other delphinium"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-85. DELPHINION ETERON* ("the other delphinium"), heading at line 7289, SUGGESTED line at 7291, note at 7292, text at line 7293. It stands in Book Three, "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants".

The whole chapter reads: "The other delphinium is similar to that above, yet is much more slender in the leaves and branches. It has the same properties as that previously mentioned, but it is not altogether as effective. It is also called hyacinthum; the Romans call it bucinus."

This chapter is therefore not a free-standing description. It depends on 3-84 *DELPHINION* (line 7271; see the entry for *Delphinium oxysepalum*, I_1066), and says this plant is similar to it but "much more slender in the leaves and branches", with the "same properties" but "not altogether as effective". What 3-84 says of those properties is a seed in wine against scorpion stings, and a report that scorpions are numbed by the herb. The reading of those properties into 3-85 is Dioscorides' own cross-reference ("the same properties as that previously mentioned"), not the Codex's. The other names are *hyacinthum* (Greek) and *bucinus* (Roman), the latter paralleling *buccinus minor* printed for 3-84.

**Illustration.** Immediately after the 3-86 heading (line 7296) the translators print a figure caption "Delphinium peregrinum after FAGUET — 1894". It is not part of this chapter's text. It is dealt with in I_1067. The translators' index lists *Delphinium elatum* at page 463, which is the page where this chapter and the start of 3-86 stand. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. The translators' note calls the genus poisonous; in general terms, larkspurs (*Delphinium*) are widely known as toxic plants. No composition is asserted. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter gives no use of its own beyond "the same properties" as 3-84, with a weaker strength. The Codex gives no protocol: a scorpion sting needs medical care and the genus is, on the translators' note, poisonous.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-85 gives no preparation of its own. By its cross-reference the preparation would be that of 3-84, the seed drunk in wine, but the chapter does not say so in terms. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

No study of the plant was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] *Delphinium elatum* is a tall garden larkspur known generally to be toxic; whether it is the plant of this chapter is the translators' guess only.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. It prints a comparison of strength only: "not altogether as effective" as 3-84.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter is three sentences: a comparison with the chapter before (more slender, the same properties, less effective), and two other names. It gives no habitat, no season and no test of quality, and it assigns the plant no status. The one thing that can be said is that Dioscorides here ranks two forms of one drug by strength. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here gives a cultivator a practice. It is a note on a weaker second form of a poisonous scorpion remedy, so it is history and not a practice.

### Contraindications

The genus is marked poisonous by the translators and is generally known as toxic, so no part of any larkspur should be eaten or drunk on the strength of this entry. A scorpion sting needs medical care. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion with no description to check it against, so no wild plant should be gathered or used.
