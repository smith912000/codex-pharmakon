# Tree salt — Shing tsha (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011: "literally means tree salt"), rGyud-bzhi Explanatory Tantra ch.20

## *A salt "obtained from the decayed roots or a log of a pine tree", poisonous "if not exposed to sun", according to the Men-Tsee-Khang note; Clark 1995 prints the same Tibetan name, shing.tsha, as "Cinnamon: Cinnamomum tamala", a plant already on the spine, so the two translations disagree and the identification is unresolved (low confidence); class mineral on the spine, but the Men-Tsee-Khang note describes a plant-derived product* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** The Men-Tsee-Khang note says this salt "becomes poisonous" if it is not exposed to the sun. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should make or take this material.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The Codex's list row is `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 201, "Shing tsha, , mineral, rGyud-bzhi, Explanatory Tantra ch.20 (Efficacy of Medicinal Substances); Four Tantras of Tibetan Medicine (English trans.)", flagged as an uncertain identification. That row gives a name and a chapter only. Two texts were read, and they do not agree on what the name means.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`). The Explanatory Tantra's salty-taste group lists "Shing tsha" with a footnote marker 4 (line 3211): "Gyam tsha, Gya tsha, Che nyang tsha, Ra tsha, Kharu tsha, Tsabru tsha, Ze tsha, Wa tsha, Zae tsha, Thai tsha, Shing tsha, Yabak khyara, Bultog and other similar substances are the group of medicinal substances having a salty taste." The note (line 3294, page 193) reads: "Shing tsha literally means tree salt. It is obtained from the decayed roots or a log of a pine tree. If not exposed to sun it becomes poisonous." The neighbouring notes in the same list describe *Thai tsha* as "artificial ash salt ... made by burning different kinds of woods" (line 3288), so wood-derived salts are a recognised type in this list. In the salt-medicine summary a group is named (lines 3621-3626): "Sedu, Phowari, Pipiling, Men ga, Tsitaka, Kakola, Sugmel, Shing tsha, Jamdre, Jitanga, Dali, Zira nagpo, Lalaphue, E mong, Subka, Che tsha, Tsabru tsha, Gya tsha, Gyam tsha, Ra tsha and Thai tsha are the group of medicines that cure cold disorders associated with baekan." The index lists "Shing tsha 193, 202, 221" (line 7493). No separate action for Shing tsha is printed in the salt-medicine summary (line 3400) in the passages read.

*Clark 1995* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). The same Tibetan spelling appears in Clark's entry at line 916: "shing.tsha: Cinnamon: Cinnamomum tamala: [has sixteen names and increases (digestive) heat. It grows in thick forest, has a hard trunk, small leaves and both thick and thin types of bark. The thin type is hotter than the thick type, which has balanced power. Cinnamon has hot, sweet, astringent and salty tastes.] It cures cold disorders of the stomach and liver and wind disorders." Here *shing.tsha* is a tree medicine, with a salty taste among its four. Clark's salt list (line 767) does not contain it: "rock salt, wood salt, horn salt, black 'sanchal' salt, white mineral salt, mirabilite, soda salt (saltpetre), white rock salt, ash salt, aragonite (Tartar salt) and salsoda (sodium bicarbonate)".

**The conflict.** Men-Tsee-Khang explains the name as "tree salt" and places it among the salts in the salty-taste list; Clark identifies the name as cinnamon, a bark. The two readings share a tree origin and a place in the cold-disorder group; they differ in what the substance is. The cinnamon reading is already carried on the spine at CPU-00008 (Cinnamon, which lists rGyud-bzhi among its traditions); this entry records the Men-Tsee-Khang "tree salt" reading separately and does not decide between them. Which is correct is not settled by the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

**Related wood salt.** Clark has a separate entry for "lche.myang.tsha: Wood Salt: [is (derived from) the branch of a tree, grows in hot areas, for example southern valleys, and has a reddish hue. This and rock salt are the superior types of salt]. Wood salt is also [applied in the] same [way as rock salt and] is beneficial for the eyes" (line 929). The Men-Tsee-Khang list has "Che nyang tsha" (line 3211), which "has the same potency as Gyam tsha, and treats eye problems" (line 3400). These are a different entry from Shing tsha in both texts; the spine note that wood salt was folded into this row is not borne out by the lines read here. [USER: verify]

**What is not given.** In the passages read, no taste beyond "salty" (the list), no dose, no preparation and no formula is given for tree salt.

**Identity.** The Men-Tsee-Khang note describes the product of a pine log or decayed root that must be exposed to the sun to be safe; this is an organic, wood-derived salt, not a mineral. The class on the spine (mineral) follows the list row and is probably wrong if the note is right; the Codex files it in Volume II pending a decision. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None is stated in the texts. As a general fact, a salt "obtained from" wood or roots would be a plant-ash or resin-derived product whose composition cannot be inferred from the note. No composition is asserted.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Possibly toxic.** The Men-Tsee-Khang text places tree salt among the salty-taste substances and, by the group list, among the medicines for cold baekan disorders. It records that the salt "becomes poisonous" if it is not exposed to sun. The Codex states no use, supplies no protocol, and does not recommend the preparation of any wood or root product for ingestion.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation-related statement is the note's source (decayed roots or a log of a pine tree) and its one condition: it must be exposed to sun, or it "becomes poisonous" (line 3294). How the salt is extracted or made is not given. No form, route, measure or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] As general facts, decayed wood can carry moulds and bacterial toxins, and pine-derived products are not safe to ingest unprocessed; the note's warning about sun exposure is not explained in the text and is not interpreted here.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT).** The salt is placed in the salty-taste group (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3211) and, with the other members of the group listed at lines 3621-3626, among the medicines that cure cold disorders associated with baekan. The Explanatory Tantra states that hot, sour and salty tastes cure baekan disorders and that salty taste pacifies wind (*rlung*) disorders (line 3224); that is said of the tastes in general. No potency is printed for this salt itself. No classification is imported from other systems.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The tantra lists it among the salty-taste medicines. The one substance of the stone section called "unsurpassed as an essence-extraction substance" is *brag.zhun* (Clark line 870); nothing like this is said of tree salt, and no *bcud len* use was found. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to take from it for cultivation. It is a wood-derived salt the Men-Tsee-Khang note warns can turn poisonous, whose very identity is disputed between two translations. It is not a recommended practice. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Possibly TOXIC.** The Men-Tsee-Khang note says it "becomes poisonous" if not exposed to the sun. Do not make or ingest.
- **Identity disputed:** "tree salt" (Men-Tsee-Khang) versus cinnamon bark (Clark); nothing should be bought or used on the strength of the name.
- **Cold baekan disorders:** medical conditions; use proper care.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
