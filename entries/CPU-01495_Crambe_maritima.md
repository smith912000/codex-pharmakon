# Crambe maritima — Krambe thalassia (Dioscorides 2-148)

## *Crambe maritima (the translators' suggestion; "Sea Kale, Sea Cabbage")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The line names one species only: "Crambe maritima — Sea Kale, Sea Cabbage". The translators' index lists it at page 271.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *2-148. KRAMBE THALASSIA* at line 5783, SUGGESTED line at 5785, text at line 5786. The chapter stands in Book Two under the running head "Lachana: Vegetables" (line 5792).

The whole chapter reads: "That which is called sea crambe is altogether different from the cultivated, with many thin leaves similar to the round aristolochia. Every one of them springs from reddish branches out of one stalk (similar to cissus). It has white juice but not in any great abundance. It is salty to the taste and somewhat bitter with a fatty substance. The whole herb is an enemy to the stomach, sharp, and eaten boiled excessively loosening to the bowels. Some (because of its sharpness) boil it together with fat from rams."

So the chapter is a warning more than a remedy. It describes many thin leaves like those of the round aristolochia, each springing from a reddish branch off one stalk, with a little white juice. It gives the taste (salty, and somewhat bitter with a fatty substance) and then three statements: the whole herb is "an enemy to the stomach, sharp", it is "excessively loosening to the bowels" when eaten boiled, and some boil it with ram's fat "because of its sharpness". It gives no therapeutic use, no habitat and no other names. The plate printed after the chapter (line 5788) is captioned "Brassica oleracea after FAGUET — 1888", so it shows the cabbage species and does not illustrate the translators' sea kale.

*The chapters around it.* Chapter 2-146 *KRAMBE EMEROS* (line 5762, "Crambe that is sown or set") is the long chapter on the cultivated kind, which is "good for the bowels" when eaten slightly boiled, with a long list of uses. Chapter 2-147 *KRAMBE AGRIA* (line 5779, SUGGESTED line "Brassica oleracea — Wild Kale [Mabberley]") is the wild kind, which grows "most commonly in steep coastal places", and is "similar to that which is sown but is whiter, rougher and bitter". The sea crambe of 2-148 is "altogether different from the cultivated". The next chapter, 2-149 (line 5793), is a beet.

**Overlap.** The spine's existing cabbage entry I_448 (*Brassica oleracea*, tags AT, CM, DMM, EP, SBJ, SS) treats *krambe* under Dioscorides without a chapter number, and so presumably rests on 2-146 and 2-147. Chapter 2-148 is a distinct chapter with a distinct suggested species and is not merged into it. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a sea-coast herb that is salty, bitter and sharp, bad for the stomach and strongly loosening to the bowels, and boiled with ram's fat by some to blunt its sharpness. These are the text's claims, and they are cautions, not recommendations. The Codex gives no protocol, and the identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

2-148 gives one preparation: "eaten boiled", and, by "some", "boil it together with fat from rams". No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition, and it does so by contrast: a plant is known by its leaves and branches, its juice and its taste, and it is distinguished from the cultivated crambe of the neighbouring chapters, which is described as good for the bowels when eaten slightly boiled (line 5764). It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The text's own verdict is that it is an enemy to the stomach, so the lesson is abstentive: this is a plant Dioscorides describes as harmful to the stomach.

### Contraindications

The chapter itself says the herb is "an enemy to the stomach, sharp" and "excessively loosening to the bowels". Anyone with a gastrointestinal complaint should not take it on the strength of this text, and no safety information for pregnancy was read. The identification is the translators' suggestion, so no wild plant should be gathered or eaten on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
