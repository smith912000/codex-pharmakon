# Calamagrostis arenaria — Kalamagrostis (Dioscorides 4-31), "reed grass"

## *Calamagrostis arenaria* (printed by the Dioscorides translators as the first suggested binomial for 4-31, glossed "Sea Sand Reed"; the same line also gives *Phragmites australis* / *P. communis*, "Common Reed") | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry.** This plant is named only by Dioscorides (DMM). It was added 2026-10-09 from the extraction list (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) at the locus 4-31, line 8285. The chapter gives almost nothing, so this is a short entry.

**Identity note.** The binomial is the translators' (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) suggestion and not Dioscorides' identification. The SUGGESTED line (line 8286) reads: "Calamagrostis arenaria — Sea Sand Reed Phragmites australis, Phragmites communis — Common Reed". Two different grasses are offered, and the chapter has no description that could choose between them. The second, *Phragmites australis*, has its own entry in the Codex (I_619, Common Reed, which is a Chinese-materia-medica entry and does not cite this chapter). [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), among the grasses that follow 4-30 AGROSTIS.

*4-31 KALAMAGROSTIS* (heading at line 8285; text at line 8287). The whole chapter reads: "Reed grass is bigger in every respect than gramen, but eaten, it is a killer of labouring beasts (especially that which grows in Babylon by the wayside)." That is all. The chapter gives no description beyond the comparison in size, no medicinal use for people, no preparation, no dose and no other names.

*Comparison chapter read.* "Gramen" is the grass of the preceding chapter 4-30 AGROSTIS (heading at line 8266), which the translators gloss as *Stellaria holostea* (greater stitchwort) with "other usage" *Agrostis alba* and *A. palustris* (white bent grass). It has "little branches full of joints creeping on the earth", "sweet, knotty roots", "the sharp leaves hard and broad like a little reed, nourishing for cows and labouring cattle". Its root is "applied to heal wounds", and a decoction is "good for griping, painful urination, and ulcers around the bladder, and it breaks urinary stones". It is also called "aegicon, or amaxitist", and the Romans call it "gramen". These are the uses of 4-30. They are not attributed to this row, which is defined by Dioscorides only as the larger plant that harms the beasts that eat it.

### Active Components

Nothing is known from the sources read. The two grasses suggested are both members of the grass family (Poaceae), and grasses as a group are generally not recognised as a source of strongly active medicines. No composition for the plant meant by Dioscorides was read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records no medicinal use. The only use of the information is as a warning that a large reed-like grass, "especially that which grows in Babylon by the wayside", was believed to kill labouring animals that ate it. The Codex gives no protocol for it.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and this chapter offers neither a status nor a frame: no description to recognise the plant by, no season, no assay, no preparation. The only information is a comparison in size with *gramen* and the warning that the grass kills labouring beasts when eaten. No cultivation framing is attested.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here gives a cultivator a practice. The plant is not securely identified and Dioscorides gives no use for people. The abstentive answer is the correct one.

### Contraindications

The chapter's only statement is that the plant, "eaten, ... is a killer of labouring beasts". This is a statement about animals, and it is reported here as the historical record and not as established toxicity. No human use is recorded, and none is suggested.
