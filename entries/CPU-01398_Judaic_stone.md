# Judaic stone — Ioudaikos lithos (the translation prints "Judaican stone"), Dioscorides 5-155
## *A white, acorn-shaped, finely lined stone found in Judaea, tasteless when dissolved, taken in warm water for painful urination and for stones in the bladder; the translator gives no identification ("UNKNOWN")* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-155, *Ioudaikos lithos* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 10637; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 928, translator identification "UNKNOWN", class guess mineral; list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 881, "Ioudaikos Lithos", mineral, flagged as an uncertain identification; the book index sends the reader to page 821). The whole chapter was read. In the translation the stone is called "Judaican stone", not "Judaic". The chapter reads (line 10639):

"Judaican stone is found in Judaea, shaped like a glans [acorn], white, with a very handsome shape, and lines parallel to one another as if made by turning. When it is dissolved, it yields no taste. An amount like a cicer [2-126], dissolved like a cream on a whetstone and taken with three cupfuls of warm water as a drink can help painful urination, and break stones in the bladder."

The chapter thus gives a place (Judaea), a shape (like a *glans*, which the translator brackets as "acorn"), a colour (white), a surface (parallel lines "as if made by turning", as on a lathe), a sensory test (dissolved, it "yields no taste"), a use (painful urination; breaking stones in the bladder), and a portion size and method of preparation (see Preparation & Dosing). It gives no season, no burning method and no adulterant. The whole chapter is a single paragraph, and the following chapter (5-156, *amiantos*) is on another stone and does not mention it.

**The reference to cicer.** "Cicer [2-126]" points to chapter 2-126 (line 5572), headed *Erebinthos*; the translator's suggestions for that chapter are "Cicer arietinum ... Chick-pea, Gram". That is the translator's identification of the reference, not a statement of this chapter. The chapter does not give a count or weight; the measure is a size, "an amount like a cicer".

**Identity.** The translator gives no identification ("UNKNOWN"), and the Codex list marks the identification uncertain. The chapter's clues (an acorn-shaped, white, tasteless stone with parallel lines, from Judaea) do not name a mineral; the text does not say what it is. Which material Dioscorides meant is not settled by the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records an unidentified white, acorn-shaped stone, ground on a whetstone and drunk in warm water, for painful urination and bladder stones. The material cannot be identified and bladder stones and painful urination are conditions that need medical care (infection, obstruction, kidney damage). The Codex supplies no modern protocol.

---

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation in 5-155 is the following: an amount "like a cicer [2-126]" is "dissolved like a cream on a whetstone" and "taken with three cupfuls of warm water as a drink". The chapter gives the size of the portion (that of a cicer), the method (rubbing the stone on a whetstone to a cream), and the vehicle (three cupfuls of warm water). It does not say how often, for how many days, or what size the cupful is. No further dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

What the herbal offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, adulteration detection), and this chapter shows part of it: a named place (Judaea), a visual description (shape, colour, parallel lines) and a taste test (dissolved, it yields no taste), together with a measure and method of preparation. It gives no season and no adulterant. The chapter is therapeutic and carries no cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to take from it for cultivation beyond the author's habit of recognition: where it comes from, what it looks like, and what it tastes of when dissolved. The stone itself is unidentified, and its use is for a urinary complaint. It is not a recommended practice. No dose beyond the one quoted in Preparation & Dosing is given in the sources retrieved. ### Contraindications

- **Unidentified material:** the identification is uncertain; an unknown stone should not be ground and swallowed.
- **Grit and contamination:** a scraping from a whetstone mixes stone from the whetstone itself with the drug.
- **Urinary symptoms:** painful urination, blood in the urine or suspected bladder stones need medical assessment; delay can lead to kidney damage.
