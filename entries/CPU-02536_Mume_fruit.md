# Mume fruit — 梅实 *meishi* (Shennong bencao jing)

## *The fruit of the mume or Chinese plum-apricot, Prunus mume (Siebold & Zucc.) Siebold & Zucc., our identification (high confidence) from the Chinese name; our note records it as the dried fruit (the later "wumei"), but the text itself says only "mei fruit" and, in the Mingyi layer, "dry it by fire"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Chinese / Shennong bencao jing (SBJ).** The mume fruit is named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk. The identification is ours. The source is Sun Xingyan's 1799 reconstruction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt`); the entry stands in the middle classic (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, middle classic, entry 梅实 *meishi*, same file, line 2833). The preface's middle-class list ends its fruit group with it (line 1677), and the file's own heading after the entry reads "above, fruit, middle grade, one kind; same as the old" (上果，中品一种。旧同; line 2843).

**The Shennong text proper** (line 2833): "Flavour sour, balanced. It governs bringing qi down; removes heat, vexation and fullness; settles the heart; pain in the limbs and body, one-sided withering and numbness (偏枯不仁), dead flesh; removes blue-black marks (青黑志), and malignant disease (恶疾). It grows in river valleys." (味酸，平。主下气，除热、烦、满，安心，肢体痛，偏枯不仁，死肌，去青黑志，恶疾。生川谷。) "Blue-black marks" (志) are skin marks or moles; "malignant disease" is the classic's term and is not identified. [USER: verify]

**The Wu Pu layer** (line 2837): "*Meishi* (the Daguan *bencao* writes 'kernel', 核) brightens the eyes and benefits qi" (明目，益气; quoted from the *Yulan*), and "does not hunger" (不饥; quoted from the Daguan edition's quotation of Wu's *Bencao*). This is Wu Pu's wording, in a different layer from the paragraph above, and it supplies a life-nourishing flavour that the Shennong paragraph does not have.

**The Mingyi bielu layer** (line 2839): "Grows at Hanzhong. Gather in the fifth month; dry by fire." (生汉中。五月采，火干。) So the later layer gives the harvest month and the drying method.

**Sun Xingyan's note (案, line 2841).** Sun quotes the *Shuowen* (the character *mou* is defined as a sour fruit, 酸果也), the *Erya* with Guo Pu ("like the apricot, the fruit sour"), the *Zhou li* (the keeper of the *bian* vessels offers dried fruit) with Zheng's gloss that the dried form is dried *mei*, and the *Mao shi* commentary: "*mei* is sun-dried as a preserve, put into soups, and people hold it in the mouth to sweeten the breath" (梅暴为腊，羹臛中，人含之，以香口). Several characters are dropped in the file.

**Preface.** The middle class "mainly nourishes the nature, to correspond to man; toxic or non-toxic, to be weighed as appropriate; for one who wishes to check disease and fill out the thin, take from the middle classic" (line 1677).

### Active Components

The text names no constituent. In general terms, the flesh of *Prunus mume* is notably sour because of organic acids (citric and malic acid among them), and, as with other *Prunus* fruits, the kernel (the seed inside the stone) contains amygdalin, a cyanogenic glycoside. These are general statements of plant chemistry, not findings of the text.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The classic records the fruit for downward qi, heat, vexation and fullness, settling the heart, limb pain, one-sided withering and numbness, dead flesh, dark marks on the skin and "malignant disease". The Codex does not turn these into modern indications. One-sided withering and numbness can describe a stroke, which is an emergency. The Sun note records an everyday use: dried mume held in the mouth to sweeten the breath, and added to soups.

### Preparation & Dosing

The Mingyi layer: gather in the fifth month, dry by fire (line 2839). The Sun note, quoting the *Mao shi* commentary: sun-dried as a preserve, added to soups, held in the mouth (line 2841). The Shennong paragraph gives no preparation and no dose. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Dried mume ("wumei") is a recognised drug in Chinese pharmacy today and the fruit is eaten as a pickle and as a flavouring across East Asia; that is a general statement. Whether it affects the conditions the text names has not been tested in sources retrieved for this entry. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SBJ (Shennong).** One flavour and one nature are printed: sour (酸), balanced (平). Middle class by placement. No further quality is given.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(b) Framed.* The entry stands in the middle classic, which the preface (line 1677) describes as for nourishing the nature and checking disease, with drugs to be weighed as appropriate. The Shennong paragraph has no *jiu fu* ("taken for long") clause. Wu Pu's layer adds "brightens the eyes, benefits qi, does not hunger", which has a life-nourishing flavour, but it is Wu Pu's wording (3rd century), not the Shennong paragraph, and the entry therefore stays at (b). The status is the middle class's: a drug whose use is weighed against the complaint.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator may treat this as a food and a seasoning, which is what Sun's note shows the fruit being used as; the classic gives it no cultivation role beyond its grade. As a drug it is a course remedy for a defined complaint, with no dose in the text.

### Contraindications

- **Kernel.** The kernel of *Prunus* fruits contains a cyanogenic glycoside; do not eat crushed kernels as a remedy. The classic says nothing about the kernel; the Wu Pu variant "kernel" (核) is a textual note, not a recommendation.
- **Stroke-like symptoms.** "One-sided withering and numbness" in the classic may correspond to emergencies today; seek care.
- **Pregnancy, children, medicines.** The text says nothing; no use is supported. [USER: verify]
