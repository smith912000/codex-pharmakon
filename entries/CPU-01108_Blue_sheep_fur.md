# Blue sheep (bharal) fur — gnah.wa, "Burrhel sheep" (Clark 1995) / "Bharal/Blue sheep" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *The fur of the Himalayan blue sheep or bharal; Clark prints* Pseudo nayaur Hodgson *among several identifications, the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints* Pseudois nayaur | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

This entry is one line of one text, in two translations.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The locus is the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, in Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, line 1195). Section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" names thirteen kinds of animal medicine, of which hair is the tenth (line 1148). Under the heading "Hair" Clark prints: "gnah.wa: Burrhel sheep: Ovis nahur Rupicapra rupicapra: Chamois: Pseudo nayaur Hodgson: [has eight names]. Its fur neutralizes poison." The identification is therefore not single in Clark: he lists burrhel sheep (*Ovis nahur*), the chamois (*Rupicapra rupicapra*, a different animal) and *Pseudois nayaur* (the blue sheep, "bharal") after the one Tibetan name. The Tibetan name appears in his index (line 1710) and the spine's source row is `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 372, "Blue sheep fur". The spine's name follows the third identification.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3559) says: "Bharal/Blue sheep fur cures poisoning." Its glossary of animals lists "Bharal/Blue Sheep Pseudois nayaur" (line 7052). Both translations agree on the action. Which species the Tibetan word covers is a question about the Tibetan names, not about the action. [USER: verify]

Neither text gives a method, a form (burnt, powdered, worn or applied), an amount, or the kind of poison. In the same "Hair" passage the peacock's crown feather also "cures poisoning" (Clark line 1193), a separate item that is not carried over.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, fur is keratin, a structural protein, with lipid from skin glands and some water. Nothing about it is known to neutralise poison.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources record one action for this material in a list of animal parts and give no outcome. Poisoning is a medical emergency, and fur does not treat it. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources give no preparation and no amount. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Animal fur is not an antidote to any poison; for poisoning, the accepted steps are poison-centre advice, supportive care and, for specific poisons, a specific antidote. Fur can carry parasites, fungi and bacteria from the animal. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** none printed. Both translations give an action only ("neutralizes poison"; "cures poisoning"). Neither prints a taste, post-digestive taste or potency, and the passages read do not assign the fur to one of the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* The fur sits inside the Explanatory Tantra's section on medicines of animal origin, in the hair passage, as a drug with a stated action on poison. No passage read assigns it a cultivation status, and none gives it a role in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any subtle-body practice.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here makes blue sheep fur a substance for cultivation. It is one line in a catalogue of animal parts. A cultivator takes nothing from this entry, and anyone who suspects poisoning seeks medical help. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Poisoning:** a suspected poisoning needs emergency care; fur is not a treatment.
- **Hygiene and infection:** wild-animal fur can carry parasites, fungal spores and zoonotic bacteria, including anthrax spores in some regions; do not use raw, unwashed fur on skin or swallow it.
- **Conservation and law:** the blue sheep is a wild animal of the Himalaya and the Tibetan plateau, and in many places its hunting and the trade in its parts are regulated or banned. [USER: verify]
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** nothing in the sources.
