# Quassia / Amargo (Bitter-wood) — Spanish *Amargo* · Sranan/Surinamese *Kwasi bita* · Sanskrit (adopted) *Bhūnimba*-analogue

## *Quassia amara* L. | Family: Simaroubaceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM · CM · SS · BM · (S. America — Indigenous Guianan/Surinamese)

**Energetic Classification:** Cooling, Drying (bitter tonic — cold and dry, Galenic reading)

**Training Phase:** Purification

### Origin & Tradition

Quassia is the one entry in this cluster that the classical Old-World authors never saw. It is a New World bitter, native to the Guianas, Suriname, and northern Brazil, and it enters the written materia medica only in the eighteenth century — yet it enters through a genuinely documented act of indigenous transmission, which is why it earns a place beside Dioscorides and Sushruta rather than merely after them. The genus *Quassia* was named by Linnaeus after **Graman Quassi** (Kwasímukámba), an enslaved and later freed Surinamese healer of West African descent who used the wood as a febrifuge and made its virtue known to European colonists in the 1750s. This is one of the few binomials in botany that commemorates an African-descended traditional practitioner, and the naming is real and well-attested.

**Indigenous South American / Surinamese:** The Guianan and maroon traditions used the intensely bitter heartwood (*kwasi bita*) as a febrifuge, an anthelmintic (to expel intestinal worms), a stomachic to restore appetite, and a tonic in convalescence. The wood was often prepared as "bitter cups" — vessels turned from quassia wood in which water or wine was left to stand and draw out the bitter principle. This is the foundational tradition; everything downstream is European adoption of it.

**Dioscorides / Galenic framing (adopted):** Quassia has no genuine Dioscoridean entry — it was unknown to antiquity. It was, however, rapidly absorbed into the *Galenic system of bitters*: European pharmacy classified it as a cold, dry, bitter tonic in the same doctrinal slot as gentian and wormwood, "strengthening the stomach" by stimulating the bitter response. We flag the tradition tag DMM here as a *doctrinal adoption*, not a historical attestation — the plant was slotted into the Dioscoridean framework of bitters after the fact.

**Avicenna (Qanun) framing (adopted):** Likewise, quassia entered Unani practice only in the colonial and post-colonial period, where it took the classical Unani role of a *muqavvī-e-meda* (stomachic) and bitter febrifuge — again a doctrinal placement into an existing category rather than an entry Avicenna himself wrote.

**Badianus Codex / New World context:** The Badianus Codex (1552, the Aztec herbal of Martín de la Cruz and Juan Badiano) is genuine and is the emblematic New World indigenous herbal; quassia is not in it (it is a South American, not central Mexican, plant), but it is cited here as the tradition-family marker for indigenous American botanical medicine into which quassia belongs.

**Sushruta / Ayurveda framing (adopted):** Quassia entered Indian bitter-tonic practice by analogy to indigenous bitters like *kirata-tikta* (Swertia) and neem, occupying the same *tikta rasa* febrifuge/anthelmintic niche.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Quassia is the pure archetype of **the bitter** — one of the most intensely bitter substances in the plant kingdom, detectable to the human tongue at extreme dilution. Across every tradition that adopted it, bitterness itself is the doctrine: the bitter that "wakes the stomach," expels the parasite, and drives out fever. This is not superstition. The bitter taste receptors (T2R family) that quassin activates are now known to line not just the tongue but the gut, driving digestive secretion and motility — the ancient "stomachic" reading is a direct description of a receptor-mediated reflex. And the same quassinoid scaffold that makes the wood bitter is what kills the malaria parasite and the intestinal worm. Symbol, taste, and pharmacology are here a single fact.

### Active Components

- **Quassinoids** — the defining constituents, a class of highly oxygenated degraded triterpene lactones. The principal bitter principles are **quassin** and **neoquassin**; more pharmacologically potent members include **simalikalactone D (SkD)**, plus picrasin B, H, I, and J. Quassin is among the bitterest natural substances known.
- **Canthin-6-one and β-carboline alkaloids** — contributing antimicrobial/antiparasitic activity.
- **Traces of tannins and volatile constituents.**

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Quassia is the **Purification-phase bitter scourge**. Where Utpala cools passively, Quassia purifies actively — it is the drug of expulsion and of the reawakened digestive fire. In the Kenetica curriculum it is used to break stagnation: the loss of appetite, sluggish digestion, and parasitic or microbial burden that blunt a practitioner's capacity to extract nourishment. Its teaching function is the discipline of bitterness itself — the deliberate acceptance of an unpleasant stimulus that produces order downstream. The bitter cup is used as a morning stomachic before food to "wake the fire," and as a targeted anthelmintic course. It is a short-arc, corrective agent, never a daily tonic — its potency and toxicity make it a scalpel, not a food.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- **Houël et al. (2009), *Journal of Ethnopharmacology*** — analysis of *Quassia amara* young-leaf herbal tea identified multiple quassinoids (simalikalactone D, picrasin B/H/I/J, neoquassin, quassin) and showed that the antimalarial activity *and* the cytotoxicity of the preparation were attributable chiefly to simalikalactone D; the authors explicitly cautioned against recommending the tea for malaria until human trials of SkD are done. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2009.07.037
- **Mishra et al. (2009), *Experimental Parasitology*** — purified quassin and neoquassin, and crude bark extract, showed in-vitro antiplasmodial activity against chloroquine-sensitive and -resistant *Plasmodium falciparum* (crude bark IC50 ≈ 0.0025 µg/mL; quassin IC50 ≈ 0.06 µg/mL; neoquassin ≈ 0.04 µg/mL) and combined additively — non-antagonistically — with artesunate. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.exppara.2009.12.007

Evidence grade: **preclinical, with a genuine ethnobotanical signal.** The antimalarial and antiparasitic activity of the quassinoids is well demonstrated in vitro and in animal models, and the traditional febrifuge/anthelmintic use is one of the better-corroborated in the New World materia medica. However, the same molecule that carries the activity (SkD) carries the cytotoxicity, and there are no controlled human efficacy/safety trials — so the honest grade stops short of clinical.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM (adopted framing):** Cold, bitter; would map to "clears heat, dries damp, kills parasites" — the class of bitter-cold antiparasitics.
- **Ayurveda (adopted framing):** Rasa *tikta* (bitter), virya *shita* (cooling), vipaka *katu* (pungent); *krimighna* (anthelmintic), *dīpana/pācana* (digestive-kindling), *pitta*-neutral to reducing, strongly *kapha*-reducing.
- **Galenic/Unani (adopted framing):** Cold and dry; bitter stomachic and febrifuge (*muqavvī-e-meda*, *dāfi'-e-humma*).

### Contraindications

- **Toxicity — flag heavily:** quassinoids are cytotoxic. Simalikalactone D, the principal antimalarial constituent, is also the principal cytotoxic constituent (Houël et al. 2009). High or prolonged dosing is unsafe. This is not a gentle tonic.
- **Pregnancy — contraindicated.** Quassia and quassinoids have reported effects on fertility and reproductive tissue and a traditional reputation as an antifertility agent; avoid entirely in pregnancy and while trying to conceive.
- **GI irritation / vomiting:** large doses irritate the gastric mucosa and can cause nausea and vomiting.
- **Cardiac caution:** high doses of quassin have been reported to affect cardiac function; avoid in cardiac patients.
- **Drug interactions:** additive with antimalarials (artesunate additivity is documented) — relevant only in supervised research settings, not self-treatment. Do not self-treat malaria with quassia; it is not an established substitute for standard antimalarial therapy.
- **Not for children except under supervision** given the narrow margin.
