# Senecio dianthus — Nampar, "mam par", "White ragwort" (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary by column position; Clark 1995 yu.gu.shing, line 1019, and chapter 21, line 1212)

## *Senecio dianthus, as the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints it on line 5525 between the transliteration "mam par" and the English "White ragwort", the fourth of six names of its "N" block; Clark 1995 prints Senecio dianthus Franch ("white type") as the third of five binomials for yu.gu.shing (line 1019) and again, by binomial only, in the chapter 21 class of poison-neutralizing medicines (line 1212)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Shennong (Yang 1998), Sushruta, Ebers and Assyrian text folders on disk for *Senecio* found no hit in the primary texts. The Dioscorides text prints *Senecio* in translators' SUGGESTED lines for Greek drugs (line 8901, *Senecio vulgaris* for *erigeron*; line 8693, *Senecio doronicum*; line 9129, *Senecio thapsoides* for *cacalia*), which are European species and are not read as this plant. The row was added from a printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` the binomial has **two** source rows. The batch locus is line 323: keyed by the **scientific name** "Senecio dianthus", locus "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 5525)", with the note "Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". The second is line 138, keyed by the **Tibetan name** *yu.gu.shing*, locus "Clark 1995 (line 1019)", translator identification "Veronica longiflora; Veronica incana; Senecio dianthus Franch (white type); Cacalia quinquelobus; Sambucus adnata Wall (black type of elder)", note "key uses the first". In Clark's string *Senecio dianthus* is therefore the **third** name, a later name and not the first, and that row is keyed by the Tibetan name. In the glossary it is the only binomial on its line. Clark's chapter 21 list (line 1212) has no row of its own.

**Two Tibetan names for one binomial.** The two editions place *Senecio dianthus* under **different** Tibetan names. Clark has it under *yu.gu.shing* (line 1019), and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary sets it against "Nampar" (line 5503) while setting "Yugu shing" against *Sambucus adnata* (see I_1353). The Codex does not decide whether *Nampar* and *yu.gu.shing* name one drug or two. The chapter 21 lists of both editions (below) are the one place where the editions agree on what the drug does, and they agree by role and not by name.

**Identity doubt.** The binomial is set against the Tibetan name by column position only. The glossary's transliteration is printed "mam par", which the Codex reads as the same name as the phonetic "Nampar" (the transliteration is not corrected). A different Tibetan drug whose name begins in the same way exists in Clark, *rnam.par.rgyal.wa* ("victor", line 937, a fruit with a "neck"), and the Codex does not pair it with "Nampar". Clark's preface says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" (line 83) and that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect" (line 68). The Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note (line 686) says that its botanical terms rest on the information of its own Materia Medica Department. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "N" block).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the letter at line 5500. The phonetic names are six: "Naga gesar Naga pukhpa" (line 5501), "Nalaesham" (5502), "Nampar" (5503), "Naram" (5504) and "Ngoga" (5505). The transliterations follow: "na ga ge sar" (5507), "nA ga pu+Sh+pa" (5513), "na le sham" (5517), "mam par" (5523), "na rams" (5529) and "sngo sga" (5535). Each is followed by its scientific name and English name: "Bombax ceiba" and "Silk-cotton tree" (5509, 5511), "Bombax ceiba" and "Silk-cotton tree" again (5513, 5515), "Piper nigrum" and "Black pepper" (5519, 5521), **"Senecio dianthus"** and **"White ragwort"** (5525, 5527), "Plantago lanceolata" and "Ribwort plantain" (5531, 5533), and "Dracocephalum heterophyllum Dragonhead" (5537). Counted in order, "Nampar" is the fourth phonetic name, "mam par" the fourth transliteration, *Senecio dianthus* the fourth scientific name and "White ragwort" the fourth English name. The count is checked on "Nalaesham" against *Piper nigrum* and "Black pepper" (third) and on "Naram" against *Plantago lanceolata* and "Ribwort plantain" (fifth). The binomial is not on the same line as the name, so it remains a pairing by order, though in this stretch the transliteration, the binomial and the English name follow each other without a gap. *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, chapter 21 (lines 3604 and 3605).* In the chapter "The Group and the Method of Compounding Medicine" (contents, line 229; chapter 20 closes at line 3583), the group of medicines that cure poisoning begins, across lines 3604 and 3605: "La tsi, Bongnga karpo, Bongnga serpo, Bongnga marpo, Khungdher karpo and mukpo, Pawo karpo and serpo, Tsad, Nampar Guthup, Tak- / sha, Havo, Yungwa, ..." and ends "... are the group of medicines that cure poisoning". The text prints "Nampar Guthup" with no comma, and the index lists both "Guthup 207, 220" (line 7381) and "NamparGuthup 220" (line 7449). Whether "Nampar" and "Guthup" are one drug or two is not settled by the lines read: chapter 20 names a "Guthup" separately (line 3433: "Tsed, Guthup and Dhumtak are antidotes to poison"), and the glossary prints "Nampar" separately (line 5503). The Codex does not say which is meant. No sentence in the Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 20 efficacy runs names "Nampar" alone. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, chapter 21, the class of poison-neutralizing medicines (line 1212).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 21 ("The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines", heading at lines 1205 and 1206): "The class of medicines which neutralize poisons consists of Musk, Red, Yellow and White Aconites, White and Brown Uncaria, (white) Phytolacca esculenta (var. Houtte), (a type of Pokeberry), (yellow) Scutellaria baicalensis, Pleurospermum hookeri, Senecio dianthus, Scutellaria, Oxytropis chiliophylla, Corallodiscus kingianus, Turmeric, Gentiana urnula, Fern, Tibetan tamarisk, Gentiana algida, Pterocephalus hookeri, Wild Rose and the cambium (middle bark) of Indian Barberry." The list names substances by English or Latin name and not by Tibetan name, so the binomial *Senecio dianthus* is Clark's identification and the Tibetan name behind it is not printed. Clark's Latin index lists *Senecio dianthus* (line 1644).

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1019).* Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry follows *spang.rgyan.dkar.po* (line 1018) and is followed by *rtsad* (line 1020). It reads:

"yu.gu.shing: Veronica longiflora: Veronica incana: Senecio dianthus Franch (white type): Cacalia quinquelobus: Sambucus adnata Wall (black type of elder): [has nine names. The brown type is said to be dar.ya.kan, and it grows in very hot areas. The white type has a large lobed leaf, pale green on top and brown beneath. It has small, slightly funy flowers, bitter taste, cool power and subdues spirits by its pungent odor. The plains-growing black type has dandelion-like dark green leaves. Both types] heal wounds, cure fever caused by poisoning [and dispel spirits]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The nine names, the three types, the leaves, the taste, the power and the action on spirits are therefore the commentary layer. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the five Latin names with Clark's "white type" and "black type" labels, and "heal wounds, cure fever caused by poisoning". The "white type" label is the only description that attaches to *Senecio dianthus*, and its content is in the brackets: a large lobed leaf, bitter taste, cool power, a pungent odor that "subdues spirits". Clark's name index lists *yu.gu.shing* (line 1749).

The three passages agree on one thing: the drug has a poison role. Clark's chapter 21 class names *Senecio dianthus* among the poison neutralizers, Clark's line 1019 has the *yu.gu.shing* drug cure "fever caused by poisoning", and the Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 21 group (if "Nampar" is read alone) names it among the medicines that cure poisoning. These are three different pairings of name and binomial, and the Codex reads them as consistent in role only. [USER: verify]

I_1190 (*Senecio vulgaris*, tag DMM) and I_843 (*Senecio jacobaea*, tags CM, DMM) are entries on European species in other traditions. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Senecio* is a very large genus of the daisy family (Asteraceae) and that many of its species contain pyrrolizidine alkaloids, which damage the liver; this is a statement about the genus and not about this species. The other genera Clark prints for *yu.gu.shing* (*Veronica*, *Cacalia*, *Sambucus*) belong to other families, so the Tibetan drug is not shown to be a *Senecio* at all. The lines read give no composition for it, and nothing is stated here about what it contains. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug among the poison neutralizers (Clark, line 1212), a *yu.gu.shing* drug that will "heal wounds, cure fever caused by poisoning" (Clark, line 1019) and a group of medicines that "cure poisoning" (Men-Tsee-Khang, lines 3604 to 3605). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only through a glossary count and Clark's identifications, and they are not Codex protocols. Poisoning is an emergency and is not treated with a herb from a catalogue. Because many *Senecio* species are liver-toxic, the genus is the wrong one to gather on the strength of a positional identification.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used (the commentary layer describes leaf and flower, and names no part as the drug), no quantity, no frequency and no duration. The poison-neutralizing class and the poison group are lists of names, and they give no compound, no method and no dose. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** The Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3604 to 3605, 5503, 5523, 5525, 5527) print no taste, power or post-digestive taste for "Nampar". Clark's line 1019 prints "bitter taste, cool power" for the white type, both inside the brackets, so they belong to the commentary layer and attach to a Tibetan name that the Men-Tsee-Khang edition does not give to this binomial.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica and compounding chapters give this drug by what it does: it belongs to the poison-neutralizing class (Clark, line 1212) and cures poisoning (Men-Tsee-Khang, lines 3604 to 3605), and the *yu.gu.shing* drug heals wounds and cures fever caused by poisoning. In the commentary layer it also "subdues spirits by its pungent odor", an action against spirit-caused disorders, one of the tradition's own divisions of disease (Clark, line 128), and not a cultivation status. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the binomial and the names found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, colophon at line 3950), and the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence extraction (lines 971, 1043, 1078, 1103, 1121 and 1144) do not include it. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a poison drug named in two chapter 21 lists and in one chapter 20 entry under a different name, with no preparation and no dose, and the genus is a liver-toxic one. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Poisoning, fever and wounds need medical care; the Tibetan name is open, the identity rests on a column count and on Clark's list of five, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. It is a general fact that many *Senecio* species contain liver-toxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids and that these are a recognised hazard in herbal products; this is a statement about the genus and not about the Tibetan drug. No conservation information and no safety information for pregnancy were read. [USER: verify]
