# Meum bunius — Bounion (Dioscorides 4-124)

## *Meum bunius, the one name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-124; "Coriander-leaved Bawd-money"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 9145) reads "Meum bunius — Coriander-leaved Bawd-money", one name and no alternative. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-124. BOUNION*, heading at line 9144, in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"). The whole chapter (line 9146) reads: "Bunium sends out a quadrangular stalk of a good length and a finger's thickness, and leaves similar to smallage [celery — old use] but much thinner, closer to those of coriander. The flower is like dill [3-67]; the seed has a sweet scent and is smaller than that of hyoscyamus [4-69]. It is urinary and warming, draws out the afterbirth, and is properly used for the spleen, kidneys and bladder. It is used with honey and water — moist, dry, or juiced with the roots, stalks and leaves. It is also called aton, actine, or anemosphoron, the Magi call it paradacry, the Egyptians, erxoe, the Romans, scopa regia, the Africans, zigar, and some, thepso."

Dioscorides describes the plant by the stalk (four-sided, "of a good length", a finger's thickness), by the leaf (like smallage but much thinner, closer to coriander), by the flower (like dill) and by the seed (sweet-scented, smaller than henbane's). He then gives its qualities and uses together: it is "urinary and warming", it draws out the afterbirth, and it is "properly used for the spleen, kidneys and bladder". The way of use is stated in one sentence: with honey and water, and the plant taken "moist, dry, or juiced with the roots, stalks and leaves". The chapter prints other names as names only: the Greek *aton*, *actine* and *anemosphoron*, the Magian *paradacry*, the Egyptian *erxoe*, the Roman *scopa regia*, the African *zigar*, and *thepso*. It gives no use or belief attached to the Magi's name.

**The chapters it points to.** 3-67 *ANETHON* (heading at line 7124) is the translators' dill: "Anethum is eaten as a vegetable", its decoction "draws down milk, soothes griping and gaseousness", and it is also called by the Magi *genitura cynocephali*; that chapter is the plant to which the flower of this one is compared, and it prints Magian names of its own. 4-69 *UOSKUAMOS* (heading at line 8618) is the translators' henbane, to whose seed the seed of this plant is compared for size.

**The next chapter and other "meum" chapters.** The next chapter, 4-125 *PSEUDOBOUNION* (heading at line 9147), is the "false bunium", whose leaves are "similar to bunium [above]" and which is treated in I_1030; it carries the cross-reference "see 2-136, 4-124". It is a different drug with different uses (griping, painful urination, pains of the side, tumours). The chapters 1-3 *MEON* (line 3564) and 3-63 *TORDULION* (line 7094) print the translators' "Athemanta meum" ("Bald-money, Meu, Spignel, Bear Root"), a different "meum". The index separates them: "Meum bunius 675" (line 16607), against "Meum 5" and "Meum athemanticum 5, 439". And 5-56 *OINOS BOUNITES* (line 10070) is a wine with a different "bunium", the translators' "Napus-Bunias sativas ... Brassica campestris var rapa ... Land Cress — Bunium Wine", which the Codex does not read as this plant. A search of the entries folder for "4-124" found I_1030 (*Bunium bulbocastanum*, 4-125), which cites it as a pointer, and no existing entry on this chapter.

### Active Components

The composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, the genus *Meum* belongs to the carrot family (Apiaceae), whose members are commonly aromatic through volatile oils. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a sweet-scented herb with fine leaves, used with honey and water as a urinary and warming remedy that draws out the afterbirth and serves the spleen, kidneys and bladder. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol for any of them. A remedy said to draw out the afterbirth is a remedy for childbirth, and the text gives no dose. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation: "It is used with honey and water — moist, dry, or juiced with the roots, stalks and leaves." It gives no quantity for any of these. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Greek name *bounion* is used in the same book for two plants (4-124 and 4-125) and, in a wine, for a third, so a modern fact about one species named *Meum* would not by itself bear on the remedies recorded.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one quality, that the plant is "warming". It also calls the plant "urinary". It prints no cooling, drying, moistening or astringent quality, and no taste; the seed has "a sweet scent".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant (a four-sided finger-thick stalk, thin coriander-like leaves, a dill-like flower, a sweet-scented seed smaller than henbane's) and how it is told from its neighbour, since the next chapter describes a plant by its likeness to this one. He records the Magi's name without comment, and recording is not endorsing. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a warming urinary drug. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a urinary and warming herb used with honey and water, with no stated quantity. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' method of recognising a plant by comparison with three others, and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Dioscorides gives the plant as a drug that "draws out the afterbirth" and as a urinary, so it should not be self-treated, and no safety information for pregnancy was read beyond that statement. Disorders of the spleen, kidneys and bladder need medical assessment, and a home preparation must not replace it. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion that the text itself does not confirm, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
