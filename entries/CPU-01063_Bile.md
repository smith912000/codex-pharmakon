# Bile — Chole Pasa (Gall from various animals) / Cow's, Ox, Bullock's Bile / gi.wam (Animal Bile)
## *Gall, the bile held in the gall bladder of an animal (ox, bull, cow, hog, elephant and others as named in the texts)* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP, DMM, FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The English text on disk is C. P. Bryan's 1930 *The Papyrus Ebers*, a translation with summaries, not a transliteration, and the Egyptian words are not printed. In Chapter VII, "Organic Remedies", Bryan lists the animals "whose identity is not in doubt" (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1392: "Ox Cow Calf Mouse...") and the parts the physicians took, among them "Blood Bile Bones Marrow Liver" (line 1415). He adds that the Cow "gave her bile, brain, blood, fat, flesh, horns, liver, milk, spleen, and tallow" (line 1429). The bile appears in these compounds as Bryan renders them: a wormcake of "Herbs-of-the-Field and Natron, baked into a cake with Cow's Bile" against the tapeworm (line 1962); a bath, "Ox-bile, Boiled Milk, Honey, Wonderfruit", strained and poured on the anus for a day, listed under "Another to cool after the other medicines" (lines 2054-2070); a vulval douche changed from garlic and cow-horn to "Bile-of-the-Cow, Cassia, and Oil" and then, "the Cow was discarded for the Hog", to "Fresh Dates and Hog's Bile" (lines 2556-2557); a salve of "Sea-salt, Honey, and Cow's Bile" for the uxedu-disease complicated by the uha-disease, later repeated with Cow's Brain in its place (line 3280); a poultice of saltpetre, teun-plant, Wasp's Blood, Ox Bile, sea-salt and bean-meal, "crush and poultice therewith for four days", for a growth in the neck (lines 3796-3812); and two cosmetic uses, "a Face-cream of Bullock's Bile and Ostrich Egg, beaten up with Fresh Milk" (line 1380) and a remedy of Bullock's Bile, whipped ostrich egg, oil, dough, natron and resin for the ageing face (lines 4118-4122). Bryan also prints "Bile-of-Many-Crabs" among the hair-dye remedies (line 4055). The only place the spine's "ox bile" row (`_sources/ebers.csv` line 76, "Cattle gallbladder (ox bile)", source field "Ebers; sae.saw-leipzig.de") can be checked on disk is these Bryan lines; the website named in that row was not read.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-96, *Chole Pasa*, translator's suggestion "Gall from various Animals" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 5352). The chapter begins with how to prepare and keep it: take new gall, "bind the mouth [of the gall bladder] with a linen thread, put it into boiling water, and then let it remain for as long as it would take to travel three furlongs [3/8 mile]", then take it out and dry it "in a shady dry place"; gall meant for eye medicines is bound with linen thread and put into a glass bottle containing honey, the thread tied to the mouth of the bottle, corked and stored. Then: "All kinds of gall are sharp and warming, varying in strength depending on their source." Those "more effective" are the galls of sea scorpions, the callionymus fish, sea turtles and hyenas, with partridge, eagle, white hens and wild she-goats; "A bull's gall is more effective than that from a sheep, swine, or goat, as well as that from a bear." Uses named include eye troubles (liquids and darkness of the eyes that has recently begun, *argema*, coarse eyelids), a laxative effect in children with a wool lock applied to the perineum, bulls' gall with honey rubbed on for tonsillitis, ears (purulent, cracked, noisy) with goats' or women's milk or leek juice, spreading ulcers, genital and scrotal pains, leprosy and dandruff with nitre or fuller's earth, bear gall in a linctus for "falling sickness", tortoise gall in the nostrils for tonsillitis, and "ointments that are rubbed about the body to prevent poisoning".

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** In the Explanatory Tantra ch.20 as translated by Clark (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`), bile is the fifth of the "thirteen medicines of animal origin" (horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings, whole body; lines 1147-1148). Under "Bile" (lines 1177-1178): "All the various types of bile constrict the mouths of the channels, halt necrosis, stimulate flesh growth, neutralize poison and are beneficial in eye disorders." In the resins section the entry *gi.wam*, "Animal Bile" (line 880), "has seven names. The superior type is said to be obtained from the liver of an elephant, but the bile duct of a bull, pig, etc. is also a common source. The brown type is greatly superior to the yellow type. It cures infectious fever, poisoning, liver fever and vessel organ fever." Clark's bracketed note at line 872 records that some scholars take the series "from animal bile to utpala" as the section on resins. Chapter 21 lists "Bile of elephant and so forth" among the standard cures for fevers and for infectious fevers (lines 1208 and 1211). The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`) gives the same: "Giwam cures infectious hot disorder, poisoning, hot disorder of the liver and the vessel organs" (line 3381), "All kinds of bile stops bleeding. They also heal necrosis and muscle tissues, act as an antidote against poisons, and are beneficial to eye problems" (line 3545), and Giwam in the group "that in general cure general hot disorder" (line 3591) and in the group for contagious disorders (line 3601). The two versions differ in one point: Clark's "constrict the mouths of the channels" is Men-Tsee-Khang's "stops bleeding".

### Varieties

**Ox bile** (EP; Bryan 1930, lines 1380, 1962, 2054-2070, 2556-2557, 3280, 3796-3812, 4118; `_sources/ebers.csv` line 76). Bryan prints the substance as "Ox-bile", "Ox Bile", "Cow's Bile", "Bile-of-the-Cow" and "Bullock's Bile". Whether these are one Egyptian word or several cannot be told from the translation. The uses are those listed in the Egyptian paragraph above: tapeworm cake, anal bath, vulval douche, a salve, a neck poultice and a face cream. A second source animal appears beside it in the same passages, the hog, "Hog's Bile" (line 2557).

*Synthesis of the varieties.* Three texts separate bile by the animal it came from, and each does so on its own ground. Dioscorides ranks the galls by animal (the sea scorpion, callionymus, sea turtle and hyena "more effective"; the bull's gall above sheep, swine, goat and bear; sheep and bear galls "somewhat weaker") and then gives one set of uses for all. Clark's Tibetan text ranks the source (elephant "superior", bull and pig as common sources) and the colour (brown "greatly superior" to yellow). Bryan's Ebers gives cow or ox first and the hog once, and sets no ranking. The cow, ox or bull (the texts print the animal differently) and the pig appear in all three, side by side and not as one thing.

### Active Components

Bile is a secretion of the liver stored in the gall bladder. In general it contains bile acids and their salts (the conjugated forms act as detergents that emulsify fats), the pigments bilirubin and biliverdin, cholesterol, phospholipids, water and electrolytes. The composition and the main bile acids differ between species, which is one reason the texts' preference for particular animals cannot be tested against a single chemistry. No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources record bile as a topical and internal drug for eyes, ulcers, poisoning and fevers, and in Egyptian cosmetic and cleansing compounds. The Codex has no protocol for it and none is offered. The reasons are plain: raw animal gall is a possible source of infection and, taken by mouth, of poisoning (see Contraindications); the sources give no dose; the animals named include protected species; and sourcing it raises ethical and legal questions. The reader should treat the entry as a record of what three traditions wrote, and of how carefully Dioscorides tells the reader to process and store it.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation given with method is Dioscorides 2-96, quoted above: bind the neck of the new gall bladder with linen thread, hold it in boiling water for the time taken to travel three furlongs, remove it and dry it in a shady dry place; for eye medicines, store the bound gall in a glass bottle of honey, thread tied to the bottle's mouth and corked. The compounds in Ebers are given with "Bile" as one named ingredient (Bryan, lines above), with the proportions only where Bryan printed them: the anal bath in the ratio 4 : 5 : 4 : 4 for ox-bile, boiled milk, honey, wonderfruit (lines 2054-2070; the numbers are as OCR'd and may be misread). No dose is given for Dioscorides's or the Tibetan uses in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Bile acids are the body's own fat-emulsifying agents, which is a general, well-established fact. Ursodeoxycholic acid, a bile acid first obtained from bear bile, is today a licensed medicine for some gallstone and liver conditions; that concerns a purified compound and does not validate raw-gall remedies. Bile contains no substance that is established as an antidote for poison or a cure for infectious fever. No clinical studies of the traditional uses were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** "All kinds of gall are sharp and warming, varying in strength depending on their source" (2-96).

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The translated line for bile (Clark 1177-1178) prints actions only, not taste or potency. For *gi.wam* Clark prints the superior and the brown types and the fevers and poisoning it cures (line 880), and no taste or potency. The chapter's groupings are by indication, hot disorders and contagious disorders (Men-Tsee-Khang 3591, 3601). No further quality is claimed here.

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The corpus is organised by complaint, and Bryan's translation prints no energetic qualities for bile.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* The Ebers material read records what was done for a complaint, such as a tapeworm, a sore anus or a neck growth, or for the face, with bile as one ingredient. It records nothing about what the substance does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. The papyrus joins spell and drug in one act (*heka*); Bryan reports no recitation with the bile compounds read above, and since he summarises, that does not show there was none. No deity is attributed. The identity of the Egyptian word behind "Ox Bile" is Bryan's rendering, and the spine row rests on a web source not read. [USER: verify]

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. What chapter 2-96 offers is a discipline of attention: how to prepare gall (bind, boil, dry in shade), how to store it for eye medicines (honey, glass bottle, thread), and the ranking of the galls by source animal. Nothing in it assigns bile a status beyond the therapeutic.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Bile is one of the thirteen animal medicines of the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica and *gi.wam* is listed among the resins. Both are given by their action on disorders (poisoning, fevers, eye disorders, bleeding, necrosis). The translated passages read name no essence-extraction (*bcud len*) use of any animal substance in lines 1147-1199; the essence-extraction references in Clark's chapter 20 that I found attach to a mineral exudate (*brag.zhun*, "unsurpassed as an essence-extraction substance", line 870) and to plants (for example *ra.mnye*, line 1144). No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing to cultivate with bile. All three traditions treat it as a drug for a complaint, the Greek text mostly as a processed topical, the Tibetan text as a medicine for fevers, poisoning and the eyes, and the Egyptian text as an ingredient in compounds. It is not food, not a tonic and not safe to improvise. The one thing a cultivator can take from it is Dioscorides's habit of attention to how a substance is gathered, prepared and kept.

### Contraindications

- **Infection and toxicity.** Raw gall from an animal can carry pathogens. In clinical toxicology, ingestion of raw gall bladders (reported with some fish and other animals) is a recognised cause of serious kidney and liver injury. [USER: verify specifics]
- **Dose unknown.** None of the three traditions' texts, as retrieved, gives a dose for internal use.
- **Eyes and wounds.** Dioscorides's eye uses and the Egyptian and Tibetan wound and eye uses are not safe to copy; animal gall in the eye or an open wound is an infection risk.
- **Legal and conservation.** Several animals named for gall (bear, hyena, elephant, sea turtles, eagle) are protected in many places; trade in their parts is restricted or illegal.
- **Ethics.** Obtaining gall means killing the animal.
