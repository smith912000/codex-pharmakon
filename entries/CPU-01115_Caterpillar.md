# Caterpillar — Kampai (Dioscorides 2-64) / erucae
## *Caterpillars, which Dioscorides says "breed on vegetables"; the translator's heading is "Caterpillars" and no species or binomial is printed* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The English is the 2000 edition of Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). The entry is Book 2, chapter 64, headed *KAMPAI*, with the translator's heading "Caterpillars", under "Living Creatures" (line 5191; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` locus "2-64 (line 5191)"). The chapter is one sentence long, and I read all of it: "The erucae [caterpillars] which breed on vegetables rubbed with oil on anyone are said to protect him from the bites of poisonous beasts."

Three points about that sentence. First, the claim is reported, not endorsed: the construction is "are said to protect". Dioscorides does not say that he has tried it. Second, the sentence is awkward in the English ("rubbed with oil on anyone"); it reads as caterpillars, rubbed with oil, applied on a person as a protection against the bites of venomous animals. The text does not say whether the caterpillars are crushed, whether they are alive, or how often, and I do not supply it. [USER: verify] Third, no species is named. "Caterpillars which breed on vegetables" is a habitat, not an identification, so the entry cannot say which insects are meant.

The same book names a different caterpillar: the Buprestis chapter (2-66, line 5201) says the *bupressedes* "are types of cantharides [2-65] and erucae [caterpillars] of the pine tree", that they are "POISONOUS" (the chapter's own marker), and that their "common strength" is "antiseptic, anti-ulcerous and heating", so that they are mixed into medicines for diseases of the cornea, leprosy and wild impetigo. That is a poisonous insect's preparation, and it is kept in the Codex under its own entry (see the cross-reference below); it is not the vegetable caterpillar of 2-64. In the milk chapter, the passage printed under 2-77 (line 5246) lists *pityocampa* [pine grub or pine caterpillar] among the "deadly medicines" (with cantharis, salamander, buprestis, hyoscyamus, dorycnium, aconitum and ephemerum) whose ulcers and inflammation new milk relieves, "For this cow's milk helps best". That line places the pine caterpillar among the dangerous substances; it gives no use for it.

One further name should not be confused with this entry: "Common Caterpillar" is the translator's English name for a plant, *Skorpioides* (4-195, line 9751), which the editor identifies as *Scorpiurus vermiculata*; the chapter says that, smeared on, it helps "those bitten by scorpions". That is a herb, not an animal.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, caterpillars are the larval stage of moths and butterflies. Their bodies are mostly water, protein and fat. Some species carry irritant or venomous hairs and spines; some store plant toxins from their food. The text does not say which caterpillars are meant, so no more specific statement is possible.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records one reported use: caterpillars rubbed with oil on a person to protect him from the bites of venomous animals. The Codex does not recommend it. There is no way to tell from the text which insects are meant, the claim is hearsay in the text's own words, and some caterpillars cause painful or dangerous skin reactions on contact.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation is the one in the sentence above: the caterpillars "rubbed with oil on anyone". The English gives no amount, no number of caterpillars, no oil, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. There is no established evidence that rubbing a caterpillar on the skin protects against bites. Contact with some caterpillars causes dermatitis, and a few species cause serious reactions. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** 2-64 prints no temperature, moisture, taste or other quality. Nothing is printed to classify. The only quality word for any caterpillar in the book is the "antiseptic, anti-ulcerous and heating" of the pine-tree *erucae* in the Buprestis chapter (2-66), which is a different material.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. The chapter is one reported protective use against venomous bites; it assigns no cultivation status, and it contains none of the provenance, season, sensory assay or adulteration detail that is the discipline of attention elsewhere in this author. The sentence's "are said to" records a report while distancing the author from it. Recording is not endorsing. The nearest the text comes to a protective, amuletic use is this reported anti-venom rub, and it is presented as a report.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The honest lesson of the chapter is how thin the sourcing can be: a single sentence, an unnamed insect, a use that the author does not vouch for. Leave caterpillars alone. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Contact reactions:** some caterpillars have hairs and spines that cause rash, pain, eye injury and, in a few species, severe systemic reactions; rubbing them on the skin is unsafe.
- **Ingestion:** do not eat or apply unidentified insects; some store toxins from their food plants.
- **Poisonous caterpillars of the text:** the pine-tree caterpillars of the Buprestis chapter are marked "POISONOUS" by the book itself; they are not the caterpillars of 2-64 but should not be mistaken for them.
- **No protection from bites:** a person bitten by a venomous animal needs proper medical care, not a rub.
- **Ethics and conservation:** a number of butterfly and moth species are protected; do not collect caterpillars in the wild.
