# Sharabha flesh — Sharabha (also printed S'arabha), venison of the Janghala group, Sushruta Samhita
## *The flesh of the animal Bhishagratna prints as "Sharabha" among the deer-like animals of Sutrasthana ch.46; his Vol. 3 glossary reads "A species of deer of the size of a camel (fabulously supposed to have 8 legs)", and the spine list flags the identity as uncertain* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The Sharabha is named in the "Meat Group" of *Sutrasthana* ch.46, the chapter on food and drink (*Annapana-Vidhi-madhyaya*), in Bhishagratna's English (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt`; `_sources/sushruta.csv` line 149 lists "Sharabha", with no gloss, source "Sutrasthana ch.46 (Annapanavidhi); Bhishagratna trans. vol.1", flagged id_uncertain). The chapter says the flesh of high-ground animals is "generally used as food", each succeeding kind superior to the one before it (lines 385-394), and divides the high-ground (*Jangala*) animals into eight sub-groups, the first being the *Janghala* (lines 403-410). Its members are listed: "the Ena (black deer), the Harina (red deer), the Rishya (blue deer), the Kuranga (antelope), the Karala, the Kritamala, the Sharabha, the Shvadanstra, the Prishata, the Chitrila (Spotted deer), the Charushka, the Mriga-matrika, etc." (lines 413-426). The group text is: "These species of venison have a sweet and astringent taste, are light, keen, pleasant (palatable), laxative, and diuretic in their effect. They subdue the Vayu and the Pittam" (lines 426-430). The chapter then takes Ena, Harina, Kuranga and Mriga-matrika singly (lines 431-447) but gives nothing by name for the Sharabha.

Other places. *Uttarasthana* ch.39 (fever): "Some authorities are of opinion that Ena, Prishata, S'arabha, Kala-puchchha, Kuranga, Mriga-matrika (different kinds of deer) or S'as'a (hare) may be prescribed as diet for a fever-patient accustomed to the use of animal food" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/uttara/SS_Uttara_ch39.txt` lines 904-908). The translator's parenthesis "(different kinds of deer)" there covers the whole list. In the after-drinks of ch.46, "The Asava (wine) of the Pippali should be used after a meal of the long thighed venisons" (lines 2835-2837).

**Identity.** The chapter text gives no gloss for the Sharabha. Bhishagratna's Vol. 3 glossary has an entry printed "S'arabha" with the gloss, by column order in an OCR table, "A species of deer of the size of a camel (fabulously supposed to have 8 legs)" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt` lines 30917 and 30950-30952). That gloss describes a fabulous animal, so the Sharabha of the text may not be an animal that can be named from the lines read; the group in which it is listed is a group of venisons, and the translator in Uttara ch.39 calls the whole list "different kinds of deer". The spine list gives no gloss and flags the identity as uncertain. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Venison is muscle meat: protein, with fat that varies with the animal, and iron and B vitamins as in other red meats. Wild game can carry parasites and bacteria. No analysis specific to this entry was read, and the animal's identity is not settled. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records the Sharabha as a venison in the best-ranked class of flesh, and as one of the meats some authorities allowed a fever patient who already ate meat. Nothing in the sources supports taking it as a medicine, and the animal cannot be identified. The Codex does not recommend the flesh for any complaint and gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives no preparation for the Sharabha by name. The only related instruction is the general after-drink for the long-thighed venisons: Pippali wine (ch.46 lines 2835-2837). Uttara ch.39 allows the list of meats it names, this one among them, as a diet for a fever patient accustomed to animal food, without a quantity. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Sushruta (SS).** Printed in *Sutrasthana* ch.46 for the *Janghala* group as a whole (lines 426-430): sweet and astringent in taste; light, keen, pleasant, laxative and diuretic; subduing *Vayu* and *Pittam*. The individual properties the chapter prints belong to Ena, Harina, Kuranga and Mriga-matrika (lines 431-447); none is printed for the Sharabha by name. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The Sharabha is named in the food-and-drink chapter, whose opening lines tie food as a class to bodily strength, complexion and the *ojas* principle (ch.46, lines 9-12). That is a claim about food in general, not about this flesh. The chapter ranks the high-ground animals highest among the classes of flesh (lines 385-394), a ranking of foods and not a cultivation grade. In the lines read the Sharabha appears only in the group list and in a fever diet; neither is a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* passage. No *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* claim is made for it. *Sattvic*, *rajasic* and *tamasic* food lists are *Samkhya*-era material, not Sushruta, and are not used.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice. In Sushruta this is a name in a list of venisons and in a list of fever-diet meats, and it cannot be identified with confidence. A person who eats game may read the group classification as the tradition's description of the food; a person who does not has nothing to adopt.

### Contraindications

- **Identity:** the animal is unglossed in the chapter and described as fabulous in the glossary; do not substitute a species on this basis. [USER: verify]
- **Food safety:** wild game can carry parasites and bacteria; cook through, and do not eat the flesh of a sick animal.
- **The text's own cautions:** the fever diet is allowed only for a patient already accustomed to animal food, and by "some authorities" (Uttara ch.39). The group properties in ch.46 describe the food; they are not a safety clearance.
- **Legal and conservation status:** hunting and trade in deer and similar game are regulated in most countries. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics and religion:** killing an animal for food is a moral question for many traditions, and meat is restricted in some; this entry takes no position beyond recording the text.
