# Hog Fennel / Athamanta cervaria — Seseli Peloponnesiakon (Dioscorides 3-62)

## *Athamanta cervaria, one of several binomials the Dioscorides translators suggest for chapter 3-62 ("Hog Fennel, Wild Celery")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). **Named only by Dioscorides in this row.** The binomial is the translators' suggestion, not Dioscorides' own identification.

*3-62 SESELI PELOPONNESIAKON* (heading at line 7090). The SUGGESTED line (lines 7091 to 7092) reads: "Dauci tertium genus, Seseli Peloponnesiacum [Fuchs], Peucedanum cervaria [in Sprague], Daucus montanus apii [Bauhin], Athamanta cervaria [Linnaeus] — Hog Fennel, Wild Celery". *Athamanta cervaria* is the last of five names in that list, and the translators do not decide between them.

The whole chapter is short (line 7093): "That which grows in Peloponnesus has leaves similar to hemlock but broader and thicker, and a stalk bigger than the Massiliense [3-60], similar to a ferula. On the top of this is a broad tuft, in which is a broader seed with a sweet scent and more fleshy. It has the same strength [as those above]. It grows in rough, moist and hilly places. It also grows in Ida."

The chapter therefore gives a description, a habitat (rough, moist, hilly places; Peloponnesus and Ida) and a pointer back to the kinds listed before it, with no use of its own. The neighbouring chapter that precedes it, 3-60 SESELI MASSALEOTIKON (heading at line 7072), prints: "The seed and root are warming: taken as a drink they cure slow painful urination and orthopnoea [form of asthma]. They are good for urinary constriction and epilepsy, induce the menstrual flow, are abortifacient, and are effective for all disorders within. They cure old coughs, and taken as a drink with wine the seed helps digestion and dissolves griping." And 3-61 SESELI AITHIOPIKON (heading at line 7076) says of its seed that it "produces similar effects". Which of these "those above" means is not stated by the text, so the strength of 3-60 is reported here as the nearest printed account and not as established for 3-62. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Plants of the carrot family (Apiaceae), to which *Athamanta* belongs, are generally known to contain essential oils, and some contain furanocoumarins. The constituents of the plant Dioscorides meant were not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Chapter 3-62 records no use of its own. It records a plant of the *seseli* kind, with a sweet-scented seed, "of the same strength" as the kinds before it. The uses printed for the neighbouring kind (urinary disorders, asthma, epilepsy, menstrual flow, abortion, coughs, digestion) are the text's claims for that kind and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** no quality is printed for 3-62 itself. It "has the same strength [as those above]", and the nearest chapter, 3-60, calls the seed and root "warming". This is reported as a pointer and not as a printed quality of 3-62.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and the chapter assigns the plant no status. What it offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: how to tell this *seseli* from its relatives by the broader leaf like hemlock, the stalk like a ferula, the broad tuft and the fleshy, sweet-scented seed, and where it grows (rough, moist, hilly places in Peloponnesus and on Ida). That is provenance and sensory recognition only. The identification with *Athamanta cervaria* is the translators' suggestion. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in this chapter gives a cultivator a practice. It is a description for recognition, with its uses left to the chapters before it. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. The neighbouring chapter 3-60 records the seed and root as an emmenagogue and abortifacient, so any such plant must be avoided in pregnancy on the text's own showing. Umbellifers include dangerous look-alikes (the text itself compares the leaf to hemlock), and the identification here is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
