**CRITICAL DISAMBIGUATION — "Brahmi" Identity:**
| Common Name | Botanical Identity | Primary Use |
|---|---|---|
| Brahmi (common Indian usage, esp. North India) | *Centella asiatica* | Wound healing; cognitive; *medhya rasayana* |
| Brahmi (Ayurvedic classical; *Bacopa* context) | *Bacopa monnieri* | Cognitive; memory; anxiety; definitive Ayurvedic *medhya rasayana* |

Both plants are called "brahmi" in different regional traditions and in different commercial contexts. *Bacopa monnieri* has a superior body of clinical evidence for cognitive enhancement (multiple RCTs) and is more precisely the classical *medhya rasayana* of most Ayurvedic texts. *Centella asiatica* has the stronger evidence base for wound healing, venous insufficiency, and skin regeneration. Commercial "brahmi" supplements may contain either plant, both, or mixtures without clearly specifying. This ambiguity affects both clinical research and consumer safety.

**Botanical Identification:**
Creeping, stoloniferous perennial herb 2-15 cm; leaves kidney-shaped to fan-shaped (reniform-orbicular), distinctly scalloped-crenate margins, long petioles arising from stolons/rhizome; flowers tiny, reddish-purple, in small umbels of 3-4; fruit (schizocarp) small, ribbed; native to tropical and subtropical Asia, Africa, and Pacific islands; grows in wet, swampy conditions, rice paddy margins, and along waterways; one of the few Apiaceae adapted to tropical environments (most Apiaceae are temperate).

**The Tiger Story:**
A widely circulated legend explaining the common name "tiger grass" holds that Asian tigers roll in *Centella asiatica* when injured, rubbing their wounds with the herb. While this behavior has not been scientifically verified in tigers specifically, the story reflects empirical traditional knowledge about the plant's wound-healing properties and has been repeated across Sri Lankan, Indian, and Southeast Asian traditions. Whether factual or folkloric, it is one of the oldest traditional pharmacological observations attributed to animal self-medication.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Core Cross-Traditional Functions:**
1. **Wound healing / skin regeneration** — the most universally documented primary application; *Centella* triterpenoids stimulate fibroblasts and collagen synthesis; ancient empirical observation → modern pharmaceutical verification (Madecassol, TECA)
2. **Cognitive enhancement / *medhya rasayana*** — Ayurvedic classification as a brain tonic; memory enhancement; specific TCM cognitive application; Ibn Sīnā's memory documentation
3. **Anxiolytic / adaptogenic** — Ayurvedic stress-adaptogen (rasayana class); brahmoside GABA-A modulation; structural resilience-building over chronic dosing
4. **Venous insufficiency / leg circulation** — the most clinically validated modern application; standardized triterpene fraction improves venous tone and reduces edema
5. **Leprosy / skin infections (historical)** — Madagascar traditional use documented by French colonial physicians; mechanism: anti-Mycobacterium activity and wound-healing promotion

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Wound Healing (Asiaticoside + Madecassoside):**
1. **Fibroblast proliferation:** Asiaticoside → binding to membrane receptors → stimulation of TGF-β1 (transforming growth factor β1) → downstream SMAD2/3 signaling → increased fibroblast proliferation and migration into wound bed
2. **Collagen synthesis:** Asiatic acid + madecassoside → increased transcription of COL1A1 and COL3A1 genes → increased Type I and Type III collagen production → accelerated wound closure
3. **Anti-scarring:** *Centella* triterpenes → modulation of matrix metalloproteinase (MMP-1) activity → balanced collagen remodeling → reduced hypertrophic scarring and keloid formation
4. **Angiogenesis:** Asiaticoside → VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor) upregulation → new capillary formation in wound bed → improved oxygen and nutrient delivery

**Venous Tone (TTFCA — Total Triterpenic Fraction):**
Asiatic acid + madecassic acid → α2 adrenergic receptor-mediated venous smooth muscle contraction → increased venous tone → reduced venous pooling → improved return flow → reduced leg edema. Secondary: triterpenoids → endothelial protection → reduced capillary permeability → reduced edema formation. This mechanism is the basis for the strongest clinical evidence in the Centella literature.

**Cognitive / Neuroprotective:**
- AChE inhibition: asiaticoside → moderate acetylcholinesterase inhibition (weaker than pharmaceutical AChE inhibitors but contributing to mild cognitive facilitation)
- BDNF upregulation: *Centella* extract → increased BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) expression → neuroplasticity support → memory consolidation facilitation
- Amyloid β reduction: asiatic acid → reduced amyloid β aggregation in vitro and in animal models; early-stage Alzheimer's disease research
- Oxidative stress: high antioxidant capacity → reduced neuronal oxidative damage

**Anxiolytic (Brahmoside / Brahminoside / GABA-A):**
Brahmoside → GABAergic modulation (proposed); animal studies demonstrate anxiolytic effect in open-field and elevated plus maze tests. Saponin fractions of *Centella* modulate GABA-A activity at allosteric sites. Clinical magnitude at typical oral doses is mild but may be significant with chronic adaptogenic use.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Overall Evidence Level: A/B** — the strongest clinical evidence base of the Apiaceae cluster for its primary indication (venous insufficiency); multiple RCTs.

**Level A — Venous Insufficiency (TTFCA):**
- Cesarone MR et al. (2001, *Angiology*): TECA (total extract Centella asiatica, standardized triterpene fraction) in chronic venous insufficiency; controlled trial (n=87): TECA group showed significant reduction in leg heaviness (p<0.001), edema (p<0.01), and capillary permeability vs. placebo. Level A.
- Multiple additional RCTs (Arpaia 1990, Pointel 1987, Darnis 1979): consistent significant effect of standardized TTFCA (total triterpenic fraction) on venous insufficiency symptom scores; edema reduction; capillary resistance improvement. These trials formed the basis for European pharmacopoeial registration of *Centella* extracts for venous insufficiency.

**Level B — Wound Healing:**
- Shukla A et al. (1999): Topical asiaticoside accelerated epithelialization and wound closure in excision wounds in rats; increased VEGF expression; Level D. Multiple Level B clinical observational studies support accelerated wound healing in post-surgical contexts and burns using standardized *Centella* preparations (Madecassol, Blastoestimulina).

**Level B — Cognitive:**
- Wattanathorn J et al. (2008, *Journal of Ethnopharmacology*): *Centella asiatica* extract in healthy elderly participants (n=28, open-label): significant improvement in working memory and attention at 12 weeks; Level B (uncontrolled).
- Dev RD et al. (2009): *Centella* extract in healthy adults showed increased calmness and decreased anxiety vs. control in acute study; Level B.

**Pharmaceutical Registration (Indirect Evidence):**
- **Madecassol** (standardized asiaticoside 1%): Registered wound-healing drug in France, Belgium, Brazil, and Southeast Asia (Laboratoires Laroche-Navarron). Registration required clinical evidence of wound-healing efficacy — provides indirect Level B/A validation.
- **TECA tablets** (standardized triterpenic fraction): Registered pharmaceutical in multiple European countries for venous insufficiency.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The Sri Lankan Daily Salad:**
In Sri Lanka, *Centella asiatica* (*gotu kola*) occupies a unique position as both a medicine and a daily food: the traditional Sri Lankan salad *gotu kola mallung* (finely chopped gotu kola + grated coconut + shallots + lime + chili) is consumed as a side dish with rice at multiple meals per week. This is one of the few documented cases in the Codex where a pharmacologically active plant with well-established medical applications is consumed as a common food in quantities that would constitute a medicinal dose. Sri Lanka maintains the highest per-capita gotu kola consumption in the world, and a folk tradition of exceptional longevity in communities with high gotu kola intake has been documented (though confounded by many dietary variables). The plant's integration into daily diet at nutritionally-relevant doses represents an ancient experiment in chronic adaptogenic nutrition.

**The Ayurvedic *Rasāyana* Class:**
The *rasāyana* (literally "path of essence") is a special category of Ayurvedic medicinal plants that are classified not merely as treatments for specific diseases but as rejuvenating agents that promote longevity, prevent aging, enhance mental function, and build overall constitutional resilience. *Centella asiatica* is classified as a *medhya rasāyana* — specifically targeting the mind (*medha* = intellect/wisdom). The four classical *medhya rasāyana* herbs are: *Maṇḍūkaparṇī* (*Centella asiatica*), *Yāṣṭīmadhuka* (licorice, *Glycyrrhiza glabra*), *Gudūcī* (*Tinospora cordifolia*), and *Śaṅkhapuṣpī* (*Convolvulus pluricaulis*). This classification places *Centella* in a pharmacological category analogous to modern nootropics — substances that specifically support cognitive function and neurological health as primary applications.

**Léon Boiteau and Madagascar — The Colonial Discovery:**
The modern Western pharmaceutical interest in *Centella asiatica* was catalyzed by a French colonial physician in Madagascar, Léon Boiteau, who in the 1940s-1950s investigated the local Malagasy traditional use of *Centella* for leprosy treatment (a use documented across sub-Saharan Africa and Madagascar). Boiteau's phytochemical analysis led to the identification of asiaticoside as the primary active compound. The subsequent development of Madecassol (standardized asiaticoside preparation) in France in the 1960s-1970s represents one of the clearer cases of direct translation from traditional-medical observation to pharmaceutical product. The Madagascar leprosy application reflected an empirically correct anti-*Mycobacterium* + wound-healing combination — both mechanisms now pharmacologically explained.

**Cognition and the Hippocampus — Animal Studies:**
Perhaps the most striking modern pharmacological data for *Centella* cognitive applications is the hippocampal neurogenesis evidence: in animal models, chronic *Centella* extract administration increases BDNF expression in the hippocampus (the primary brain region for memory formation) and increases the number of newly differentiated neurons in the dentate gyrus (adult hippocampal neurogenesis). This places *Centella* in a pharmacological category — herbs that may support neuroplasticity and adult neurogenesis — that is the subject of intense current neuroscience interest. Whether equivalent effects occur at oral human doses has not been definitively established, but the mechanism provides strong biological plausibility for the ancient *medhya rasayana* classification.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Indications (Traditional + Evidence-Supported):**
- **Chronic venous insufficiency** — PRIMARY clinical evidence (Level A); standardized TECA/TTFCA preparations; varicose veins; leg edema; capillary fragility
- **Wound healing** — topical standardized preparations; post-surgical; burns; keloid prevention
- **Cognitive support / memory** — *medhya rasayana* application; chronic daily use; Level B
- **Anxiety / adaptogenic** — mild anxiolytic; adaptogenic support under chronic stress; *rasayana* use
- **Skin regeneration** — topical: scars, stretch marks, hypertrophic scarring

**Preparation:**
- Fresh herb (Sri Lankan tradition): gotu kola mallung (daily salad); raw juice; fresh leaf consumption 10-20g/day
- Dried herb tea: 1-2 g dried herb in 200 mL hot water; steep 10 min; 2-3× daily
- Standardized extract (cognitive/adaptogenic): 250-750 mg standardized extract (≥8% triterpenes) 2× daily; 4-12 week courses; standard Ayurvedic protocol
- TECA/TTFCA oral (venous insufficiency): 30-60 mg standardized triterpenic fraction 3× daily; pharmaceutical preparations (registered in European and Asian markets)
- Topical (wounds/scars): standardized cream 1% asiaticoside; apply 2-3× daily; clinical Madecassol/CICA-CARE protocols

**Contraindications:**
- Hepatotoxicity concern: case reports (rare) of hepatotoxicity with high-dose oral preparations; monitor LFTs with prolonged high-dose use
- Pregnancy: insufficient safety data; AVOID medicinal doses in pregnancy; topical application lower risk
- Fertility: some animal data suggesting anti-fertility effects at high doses; avoid if trying to conceive

**Drug Interactions:**
- Sedatives/anxiolytics: mild additive CNS depression
- Hepatotoxic drugs: additive hepatotoxic risk with concurrent hepatotoxic medications
