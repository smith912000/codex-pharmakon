# I-360 — Anethum graveolens · Dill

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Anethum graveolens* L.  
**Family:** Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)  
**Common names:** Dill (EN); Dill / Dillkraut (DE); aneth (FR); eneldo (ES); aneto (IT); anethon [ἄνηθον] (GR, classical); shibbit / shabath [شبت] (AR); soa / suwa [सोया] (HI); shi luo [時蘿] (TCM); shi luo zi [時蘿子] (TCM seeds); luk-pa (TIB)

**Note on Indian dill:** Indian dill (*Anethum sowa* / *A. graveolens* var. *sowa*) is sometimes listed as a distinct variety or species; pharmacologically equivalent to European dill (*A. graveolens*) with minor compositional differences (higher dill apiole, different carvone ratio). Most pharmacological literature uses both interchangeably.

**Parts used:** Seeds/fruits (highest essential oil; medicinal primary); leaves (culinary fresh and dried; "dill weed"); umbels (fresh for pickling); root (historical minor)  
**Habitat & distribution:** Annual or biennial; native to Southwest Asia and the Eastern Mediterranean (Iran, Afghanistan, India regions); naturalized across Europe, North America, South America; one of the most extensively cultivated aromatic herbs globally; thrives in warm, well-drained soils; strongly aromatic at all stages

## 3. Convergence Analysis

Dill's 6/9 convergence — with clear confirmation across Greek, Arabic, Chinese, Egyptian, and Assyrian traditions — reflects one of the most consistent single-indication profiles in the Codex: **carminative and digestive antispasmodic**. Every confirming tradition independently emphasizes the ability of dill seeds to relieve intestinal gas, abdominal cramping, and digestive discomfort. The mechanistic explanation (carvone and anethole smooth-muscle relaxation) was established in the 20th century; the empirical observation was stable across 4,000 years.

The **Biblical mention of dill** (Matthew 23:23 — Jesus criticizes the Pharisees for tithing "mint and dill and cummin" while neglecting "justice and mercy and faithfulness") establishes the economic value of dill in 1st-century CE Palestine as a tithed commodity — alongside the practical acknowledgment that these herbs were deliberately cultivated as both flavoring and medicine.

Dill's cross-traditional application as a **sleep promoter and calming herb** (Ibn Sīnā specifically describes shibbit as promoting sleep) has no modern mechanistic explanation comparable to its carminative effects, but has persisted in European folk tradition ("dill water" for restless infants). The primary modern product derived from this tradition — "gripe water" — contains dill seed water as its historically central ingredient, though contemporary formulations vary widely in actual dill content.

## 4. Phytochemical Composition

**Essential oil (seeds), principal constituents:**

| Compound | Concentration | Notes |
|----------|--------------|-------|
| (R)-(+)-Carvone | 40–60% (seeds) | Primary active in seeds; dill/caraway enantiomer |
| α-Phellandrene | 15–35% | Fresh "dilly" aroma character |
| Limonene | 5–15% | Citrus note; minor bioactivity |
| Dill apiole | 2–10% (higher in Indian var. *sowa*) | Pharmacologically relevant at high doses; related to parsley apiole |
| Dillapiole | Variable | Insecticide; minor therapeutic significance |
| (E)-Anethole | Trace | Lower than fennel; some overlap of mechanisms |

**Carvone enantiomer significance:**
- **(R)-(+)-Carvone** (dextratory) = dill and caraway (*Carum carvi*) — distinctive dill/rye bread aroma
- **(S)-(-)-Carvone** (levorotatory) = spearmint (*Mentha spicata*) — minty aroma

Same molecular formula (C₁₀H₁₄O), different 3D arrangement, different sensory receptor binding (different olfactory receptors; different biological activity profiles). This enantiomeric distinction in a single major volatile accounts for why dill/caraway, despite being in completely different plant families from spearmint, produces a categorically different sensory experience and pharmacological profile despite sharing a nominal "carvone" constituent.

**Leaf composition (dill weed — fresh):**

| Compound | Content / Notes |
|----------|----------------|

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Carminative / Spasmolytic:**
Carvone and dillapiole → calcium channel blockade in intestinal smooth muscle → reduced tone → relaxation of intestinal spasm → release of entrapped gas → carminative effect. Confirmed in isolated rat ileum preparations and in vivo rat intestinal motility studies. The spasmolytic effect is quantitatively similar to papaverine at equivalent tissue concentrations in some preparations.

**Galactagogue:**
The mechanism of dill's galactagogue effect is not well characterized. Proposed pathways: (1) mild estrogenic activity via anethole trace content; (2) dopamine receptor modulation affecting prolactin; (3) indirect → improved maternal well-being and reduced digestive discomfort → improved let-down reflex. Traditional use consistent but mechanistic basis weaker than for fennel.

**Sedative / Anxiolytic:**
Traditional sleep-promoting use: carvone has been reported to have CNS depressant activity in rodent models (reduced spontaneous motor activity, increased pentobarbital sleeping time). Mechanistic studies suggest possible GABA-A modulation (minor, not confirmed at therapeutic doses). Clinical significance uncertain. Infant "dill water" for colic calming may work primarily via carminative mechanism rather than true sedation.

**Antimicrobial:**
Carvone, phellandrene, and dillapiole inhibit *Salmonella*, *E. coli*, *S. aureus*, *Aspergillus flavus*, *Candida albicans* in vitro. Relevant to traditional use for digestive infections. Dill's combination of carminative + antimicrobial properties makes it particularly appropriate for post-infective IBS presentations.

**Hypoglycemic:**
Dill seed extract reduces blood glucose in streptozotocin-diabetic rats (multiple studies); mechanism involves inhibition of α-glucosidase activity (reduced carbohydrate digestion rate) and possible insulin secretagogue effects. Human RCT data: pilot studies only (Level C evidence); no large RCT.

**Anti-inflammatory:**
Flavonoids (quercetin, kaempferol) and carvone → NF-κB inhibition; COX-2 inhibition (modest). In vitro and animal data; human anti-inflammatory clinical evidence not established.

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**Commission E approved (Germany):**
Not specifically Commission E approved for dill alone (contrast with fennel). However, dill appears in the Commission E monograph on "Caraway fruit" (caraway has same carvone composition), and dill seed preparations carry analogous traditional use recognition under ESCOP and EMA/HMPC.

**ESCOP (European Scientific Cooperative on Phytotherapy) monograph:**
Dill fruit recognized for: **dyspeptic complaints** (flatulence, mild spasm) — consistent with carminative clinical evidence.

**Level B:**
- **Infantile colic:** "Gripe water" containing dill seed water — multiple clinical series and at least two RCTs show reduction in colic episodes vs placebo; however, product composition variability (different gripe water formulations contain different amounts of actual dill extract vs simethicone vs other ingredients) makes evidence attribution complex. Dill seed 0.1% emulsion (analogous to Alexandrovich fennel protocol): positive in small pilot trials.
- **Digestive complaints (adults):** Institutional clinical series (German-language primary) support carminative use

**Level C:**
- Hypoglycemic (humans): pilot studies with mixed methodology
- Galactagogue: consistent traditional use; no robust RCT specific to dill alone
- Sleep promotion: folk use with no controlled study

**Level D:**
- Anti-tumor mechanisms in vitro: carvone induces apoptosis in some cancer cell lines; no clinical cancer use data
- Antifungal: in vitro Candida inhibition (therapeutic relevance not established)

**Gripe water history:**
Original "gripe water" (William Woodward, UK, 1851) contained dill seed water (carminative), sodium bicarbonate (antacid), alcohol (5–20%), and sugar. The alcohol content was later removed. Modern gripe water products vary enormously — some contain dill extract, others use simethicone or have replaced all herbal ingredients. The brand name persists long after the dill-water origin has been obscured from consumer awareness in many markets.

## 7. Phenomenological Record

**Biblical tithes and Rabbinic law:**
Matthew 23:23 explicitly names dill (*anethon* in the Greek New Testament) alongside mint and cummin as tithed commodities — establishing that first-century Judean practice required one-tenth of dill production as Temple tribute, confirming both its agricultural economic importance and its everyday presence in the kitchen garden. The Mishnah (Talmudic compilation) elaborates that dill's leaf, seed, and stem were each subject to different tithing rules, demonstrating the sophistication with which rabbinic law categorized the plant's multiple commercial-culinary forms.

**Dill and the evil eye (European and Middle Eastern folk use):**
In numerous European (Germanic, Slavic, Scandinavian) and Middle Eastern traditions, dill was hung in doorways, placed in cradles, and used in charms to protect against the evil eye and malevolent spirits. The aromatic volatile oils almost certainly played a role in the practical protective function (insect repellent, air antimicrobial) that was subsequently mythologized. Norse tradition specifically associated dill with protective magic (from the Old Norse *dill* = "to soothe or calm" — possibly the etymological origin). Viking warriors reportedly bathed wounds in dill decoctions — the antimicrobial activity of carvone providing real wound-protective benefit that may have established the herb's reputation for protective power.

**Dill in Eastern European culinary pharmacology:**
The preserved/pickled cucumber tradition across Poland, Russia, Ukraine, Hungary, and Scandinavia is, at its pharmacological core, a delivery mechanism for dill's carminative principles alongside the probiotic effects of lacto-fermented cucumber. The traditional practice of adding dill umbels (not just leaves) to the pickle brine specifically captures the seed's higher carvone content at the fermentation stage. The "pickle season" — late summer dill harvesting timed to cucumber maturation — was a major annual household pharmacological event in pre-industrial Central and Eastern Europe, preparing the fermented, carminative-rich winter food supply.

**Sanskrit dill and Ayurvedic dietary science:**
In Ayurvedic cooking, dill (*shatapushpi*) is classified as both a *dipana* (digestive fire kindler) and a *pachana* (digestive — completes improperly digested food). These categories map precisely onto the dual carminative-spasmolytic mechanism: kindling digestive fire = increasing gastric acid and enzyme secretion; pachana = reducing gas from incompletely digested carbohydrate fermentation in the colon. The Ayurvedic dietary science that produced this classification operated without chemical knowledge but with extraordinary precision about functional outcome.

## 8. Practical Application

**Therapeutic dose ranges:**

| Form | Dose | Indication |
|------|------|-----------|
| Dill seed infusion (tea) | 1 tsp seeds/cup, 2–3×/day after meals | Carminative; digestive; IBS |
| Dill seed tincture (1:5, 45% ethanol) | 1–2 mL TID | Digestive; carminative |
| Dill seed gripe water (infant) | 5–10 mL per dose (standardized preparations only) | Infantile colic |
| Fresh dill (dill weed) | 10–30 g/day culinary | Nutritive; mild carminative |
| Dill seed powder | 1–2 g/day | Digestive tonic; mild hypoglycemic (adjunct) |

**Preparation principles:**
Freshly crush seeds before infusion — carvone is volatile; pre-ground seeds lose potency. Use freshly boiled water but do not continue boiling the infusion (volatile oil loss). Cover infusing vessel. For infant colic, use pre-made standardized preparations (consistent dosing) rather than home infusions where concentration is variable.

**Contraindications:**
- **Pregnancy (medicinal doses):** Emmenagogue and uterotonic at therapeutic doses (contains dill apiole, which is related to parsley apiole); culinary use as flavoring generally safe
- **Apiaceae allergy:** Cross-reactivity with carrot, celery, anise, fennel, parsley; anaphylaxis risk in sensitized individuals

**Drug interactions:**
- Sedative/hypnotic medications (benzodiazepines, barbiturates): theoretical additive CNS depression at high doses (evidence weak; clinical significance uncertain)
- Antidiabetic medications: additive hypoglycemic potential at medicinal doses; monitor blood glucose

**Safety profile:**
Culinary use (dill weed, seeds as seasoning): GRAS; extremely safe. Seed preparations at therapeutic doses: well tolerated; mild GI effects possible. Essential oil (concentrated): not for internal use without professional guidance; dill apiole hepatotoxic at high doses. Gripe water (infant): use only standardized commercial preparations; do NOT give home-prepared strong infusions to infants.

**Energetic tier:** ◉ Qi (digestive carminative primary); minor ☿ Shen (sedative folk use — mechanistically uncertain)  
**Optimal form:** Fresh seed infusion (tea) for carminative use; standardized gripe water for infantile colic; fresh dill weed in cooking for daily nutritive-carminative maintenance dose
