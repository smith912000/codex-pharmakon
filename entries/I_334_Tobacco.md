# I-334: Nicotiana tabacum (Tobacco)

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary name:** Tobacco  
**Botanical name:** *Nicotiana tabacum* L.  
**Family:** Solanaceae  
**Synonyms:** Common tobacco, cultivated tobacco, Virginia tobacco; also *N. rustica* (Aztec tobacco — higher nicotine; ritual use)  
**Traditional names:** Tabako (Arawak/Taíno — origin of all European words); Cohoba (Taíno — snuff form); Piciétl (Nahuatl — *N. rustica*); Sayri (Quechua); Tütün (Turkish/Ottoman); Tabac (French); Tabak (German/Dutch/Russian); Tambaku (Hindi तम्बाकू)  
**Etymology:** "Tobacco" derives from Arawak *tabako* — the Y-shaped pipe used to inhale smoke, not the plant itself; the word transferred to the plant in Spanish

**Botanical description:** Robust annual herb 1–3 m; large, ovate-lanceolate leaves 30–60 cm, sticky-glandular; flowers tubular-funnel-shaped, pale pink to red, 5-lobed, in terminal panicles; capsule ovoid, 2-celled, containing thousands of minute seeds. *N. rustica* (smaller, yellow flowers) contains 2–14% nicotine vs. *N. tabacum* at 1–4%.

**Part used:** Leaves (cured/dried); historically also sap, poultice, root in indigenous medicine

## 3. Convergence Analysis

**Pattern:** Tobacco represents the sharpest divergence in the Codex between traditional pharmacological status and modern medical significance. In indigenous American traditions, tobacco is a sacred healer and ritual substance — not a recreational drug. Pan-South American evidence places tobacco use at least 5,000–6,000 BCE (ancient nicotine residue in ritual pouches found in Bolivia; Dillehay et al. 2010). The Maya, Aztec, and Amazonian traditions all distinguish between medicinal/ritual use (intermittent, high-dose, supervised) and the chronic daily use that European colonizers developed.

The Aztec codices (*Codex Vindobonensis*, *Florentin Codex* compiled by Sahagún) describe yetl/piciétl application for headache (nasal insufflation), toothache (chewed leaf poultice), skin sores (topical), and fatigue. *N. rustica* (higher nicotine) was the ritual species; *N. tabacum* (milder) was the domestic/medicinal species. The distinction between the two species is culturally encoded.

The 16th-century European encounter created the catastrophic public health trajectory: Jean Nicot de Villemain (French ambassador to Portugal) sent tobacco to Catherine de' Medici for her migraines (1560) — the genus *Nicotiana* and the alkaloid "nicotine" both bear his name. Within 50 years, tobacco was a global addiction-generating commodity, fundamentally altering world economics, the slave trade, and eventually causing the largest preventable mortality epidemic in modern medical history.

### Primary Alkaloids

|----------|-------------------|-------|
| Nicotine | 1.0–4.0% (*N. tabacum*); 2–14% (*N. rustica*) | PRIMARY — nAChR agonist; the addictive component |
| Nornicotine | 0.1–0.5% | Minor metabolite/biosynthetic intermediate; also nAChR active |
| Cotinine | Trace (primary in vivo metabolite) | Major urinary metabolite; biomarker of tobacco exposure; CYP2A6 substrate |
| Anabasine | 0.2–0.7% | nAChR agonist; implicated in smoking cessation product contamination |
| Myosmine | Trace | Weak nAChR activity |
| NNN, NNK, NNAL | Present | Tobacco-specific nitrosamines (TSNAs) — PRIMARY CARCINOGENS in cured tobacco |

### Combustion Products (not present in leaf — formed on smoking)
- **Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs):** benzo[a]pyrene, pyrene → DNA adducts → lung carcinogenesis
- **Nitrosamines:** NNK → lung carcinogen (4-(methylnitrosamino)-1-(3-pyridyl)-1-butanone)
- **Carbon monoxide:** binds hemoglobin → tissue hypoxia
- **Acrolein, formaldehyde, acetaldehyde:** mucosal irritants; respiratory carcinogens
- **Polonium-210 (²¹⁰Po):** radioactive; from phosphate fertilizer on tobacco; lung dose ~100 mSv/year in pack-a-day smoker
- Total identified compounds in tobacco smoke: >7,000; ~70 established carcinogens

### Non-alkaloid leaf constituents
- Chlorogenic acid, rutin, caffeic acid (antioxidant — irrelevant to clinical tobacco effects)
- Solanesol: polyprenol; potential antimicrobial (experimental only)
- Stigmasterol, β-sitosterol: phytosterols

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

### Primary: Nicotinic Acetylcholine Receptor (nAChR) Agonism

**Nicotine mechanism — the most studied natural alkaloid in pharmacology:**

Nicotine binds **nicotinic acetylcholine receptors** (ligand-gated ion channels): heteromeric α4β2 (predominant brain subtype; responsible for addiction and cognitive effects) and homomeric α7 (inflammation modulation; cognitive effects). Unlike muscarinic antagonists (belladonna family), nicotine is a **cholinergic AGONIST**.

**CNS effects:**
- **Dopamine release in nucleus accumbens** via α4β2 in VTA → reward, motivation, craving; the mechanistic basis of tobacco addiction
- **Cortical arousal, attention enhancement:** α4β2 prefrontal → cognitive enhancement (acute); basis of the "concentration aid" perception
- **Mood regulation:** serotonin and noradrenaline release; acute mood improvement
- **Appetite suppression:** hypothalamic nAChR activation

**Peripheral effects:**
- **Adrenal medulla stimulation (α3β4):** catecholamine release → HR↑, BP↑, peripheral vasoconstriction
- **Neuromuscular junction (α1β1γδ):** at toxic doses — muscle fasciculations → paralysis (Creveld syndrome of nicotine poisoning)
- **Ganglionic nAChR (α3β4):** initial stimulation then block at overdose

**Receptor desensitization + tolerance:**
- Rapid desensitization after binding → tolerance develops within hours of first cigarette
- Upregulation of nAChR density: smokers have more brain nAChRs than non-smokers — contributes to withdrawal on cessation
- The "first cigarette of the day" restores receptor sensitivity after sleep-imposed abstinence — a molecular basis for morning craving

### Addiction neuropharmacology
The most addictive quality of tobacco: nicotine bolus delivery via inhalation reaches brain in **7–10 seconds** — faster than IV injection route; cigarette design optimized for rapid delivery. This pharmacokinetic profile (fast onset, fast decay, ~30-minute half-life) creates the 20-minute craving cycle encoding typical smoking frequency.

## 6. Clinical Evidence

### Nicotine Replacement Therapy (NRT) — Level A

**Cochrane review (Hartmann-Boyce 2018; updated 2022):** 117 RCTs, n>65,000; all NRT forms (patch, gum, lozenge, inhaler, nasal spray) increase quit rates ~50–70% vs. placebo (absolute quit rate improvement ~5–10% at 6 months).

- **Nicotine patch (transdermal):** slow-release; prevents morning craving; 14–21 mg/24h starting dose; taper over 8–12 weeks; Level A
- **Nicotine gum/lozenge (polacrilex):** 2–4 mg; buccal absorption; episodic craving management; Level A
- **Nicotine inhaler:** lung absorption (peripheral, not deep lung); mimics hand-to-mouth habit; Level A
- **Nicotine nasal spray:** fastest NRT delivery; for heavy addiction; Level A

**Combination NRT (patch + rescue form):** Cochrane: superior to single NRT form (OR 1.34); now standard recommendation

### Varenicline (Chantix/Champix) — Level A
Partial α4β2 nAChR agonist: reduces craving (partial agonist effect) + blocks nicotine binding (competitive). Cahill 2016 Cochrane: superior to NRT and bupropion. Neuropsychiatric safety concern (black box 2009) largely resolved (FDA 2016 trial EAGLES n=8,000: no significant neuropsychiatric difference vs. NRT in stable psychiatric patients).

### Bupropion (Zyban) — Level A
Dopamine/noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor + weak nAChR antagonist; Hughes 2014 Cochrane: approximately doubles quit rates vs. placebo; Level A

### Tobacco-associated disease reduction (Level A — epidemiological):
- Smoking cessation at any age reduces mortality; at age 30 cessation returns near-normal life expectancy
- Lung cancer: 10-fold lifetime risk reduction 10–15 years after cessation
- CV disease: CV risk returns to near-baseline within 1–2 years of cessation

## 7. Phenomenological Record

### Sacred Tobacco in Indigenous American Tradition
Across 500+ documented indigenous North American nations, tobacco (*N. rustica* primarily) is sacred: the most important plant in the Lakota, Anishinaabe, Haudenosaunee (Iroquois), Potawatomi, and Cherokee traditions. Tobacco is offered to spirits, passed in the pipe ceremony (chanupa), mixed with kinnikinnick (herbal mixture for communal pipe), and understood as a messenger between human and spirit worlds. This is categorically not recreational use: sacred tobacco is used intermittently, ceremonially, and often in specific social contexts with spiritual meaning.

The confusion of sacred tobacco with commercial tobacco represents a profound cultural category error that has contributed to smoking rates in indigenous communities exceeding 40% (vs. ~15% general US population) — a public health tragedy rooted in the collision of two radically different tobacco relationships.

### Jean Nicot and the Medical Introduction
1560: Jean Nicot, French ambassador to Lisbon, obtained tobacco from a Flemish trader who had brought it from Florida. He sent seeds to Catherine de' Medici for her migraines, attributing it with "the wound herb" (based on observed healing of a man's ulcers — likely an anti-infective or cicatrizant effect from nicotine's antimicrobial properties). Nicot published "The Miraculous Herb" in 1571. The genus *Nicotiana*, the alkaloid *nicotine*, and the concept of tobacco as medicine all bear his name — a historical irony given the ensuing mortality.

### The Tobacco Economy and the Atlantic Slave Trade
Tobacco cultivation in Virginia (from 1612 — John Rolfe's first successful crop) became inseparable from the Atlantic slave trade: tobacco was the first globally traded agricultural commodity, and its labor requirements drove demand for enslaved people from West Africa. By 1700, Virginia exported 20 million pounds of tobacco annually; the tobacco economy directly funded the colonial infrastructure that shaped American history. The intersection of *N. tabacum*, addiction pharmacology, and racial capitalism constitutes one of the most consequential historical chains in recorded history.

### The Tobacco Industry Scientific Fraud
1950s–1990s: the most extensively documented case of corporate scientific fraud in history. Brown & Williamson, Philip Morris, RJ Reynolds, and other tobacco companies:
- Internally accepted since 1953 (Sloan-Kettering studies) that cigarettes cause lung cancer
- Publicly denied it until 1998 (Tobacco Master Settlement Agreement)
- Manipulated nicotine delivery to maximize addiction (ammonia processing to free-base nicotine)
- Funded the "controversy" strategy (later adopted by fossil fuel industry)
- Documents revealed in the MSA established the standard framework for studying industry scientific manipulation

### Nicotine and Parkinson's Disease — Epidemiological Paradox
A consistent epidemiological finding since 1959 (Doll & Hill, incidentally): smokers have ~40–50% lower lifetime risk of Parkinson's disease. Multiple mechanisms proposed: nicotine's neuroprotective effect on dopaminergic neurons in substantia nigra (via α7 nAChR); MAO-B inhibition by tobacco smoke constituents (MAO-B is also the target of selegiline). Clinical trials of nicotine for Parkinson's have yielded mixed results; the epidemiological protection likely involves non-nicotine tobacco constituents. Regardless, the risk-benefit calculus is clear: no one should smoke to prevent Parkinson's.

## 8. Practical Application

### NO MEDICINAL USE OF CRUDE TOBACCO — APPLICATIONS ARE PHARMACEUTICAL (NICOTINE-BASED) ONLY

**Medicinal tobacco smoking is contraindicated in all modern guidelines.** The therapeutic agent is nicotine; the delivery vehicle (combustion) produces the harm. The ratio of nicotine benefit (modest cognitive effect, anti-Parkinson's signal) to tobacco harm (cancer, COPD, cardiovascular disease) is catastrophically unfavorable.

### Nicotine Replacement Therapy Protocol (for smoking cessation)
**Combination NRT (first-line) — most effective non-prescription approach:**
1. Nicotine patch (long-acting, steady-state): 21 mg/24h (>10 cigarettes/day) or 14 mg/24h (≤10/day) for 6 weeks → 14 mg 2 weeks → 7 mg 2 weeks
2. Plus short-acting rescue NRT: 2 mg gum/lozenge (smoke <25/day) or 4 mg (>25/day) PRN craving
3. Start day of quit; continue patch through sleep if vivid dreams tolerable (or remove at bedtime)

**Prescription options (most effective):**
- Varenicline (Chantix/Champix): 0.5 mg OD days 1–3, 0.5 mg BD days 4–7, 1 mg BD thereafter for 12 weeks
- Bupropion SR (Zyban): 150 mg OD 3 days, then 150 mg BD for 7–12 weeks; start 1 week before quit date

**Combination (most effective overall):** Varenicline + NRT > varenicline alone

### Drug Interactions (nicotine — physiological effects on CYP and cardiac systems)
- **Polycyclic hydrocarbons in tobacco smoke** (not nicotine itself) induce **CYP1A2**: increases metabolism of clozapine, olanzapine, theophylline, caffeine → dose may need increasing in smokers; reduction on cessation
- **Nicotine** (not nicotine replacement): increases insulin resistance; smokers may have higher insulin requirements
- **NRT + cardiovascular disease:** transient HR↑ and BP↑ from NRT; caution in recent MI (< 2 weeks), serious arrhythmia, unstable angina

### Indigenous Tobacco Protocol Note
The Codex documents traditional use without endorsement. Sacred tobacco use in indigenous ceremonial contexts represents a pharmacologically and culturally distinct practice from commercial tobacco. The Codex acknowledges the indigenous tradition while reiterating that medicinal tobacco smoking is contraindicated in all modern clinical settings.
