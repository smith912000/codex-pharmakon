# I-400 | Betony / Wood Betony
**Betonica officinalis** L. (syn. *Stachys betonica* Benth., *Stachys officinalis* (L.) Trevis.) | Family: Lamiaceae

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted binomial (disputed):** *Betonica officinalis* L. (1753) / syn. *Stachys betonica* Bentham / *Stachys officinalis* (L.) Trevisan
**Note on nomenclature:** Taxonomic authorities remain divided — many current databases (Plants of the World Online, GBIF) place this species in *Stachys* (*S. betonica* or *S. officinalis*). The name *Betonica* is retained here as it is: (1) the older Linnaean name under which the herb was pharmacopoeially studied; (2) still in common use in herbal medicine literature; (3) recognizes the distinct pharmacological identity separate from other *Stachys* species.
**Family:** Lamiaceae (Labiatae)
**Tribe:** Lamieae

**Common names:**
- English: betony, wood betony, common betony, bishopswort, betonica
- French: bétoine officinale, bétoine des bois
- German: Heil-Ziest, Echte Betonie, Betonie
- Italian: betonica, betonica officinale
- Spanish: betónica, bretónica
- Welsh: cribau Sant Ffraid ("St. Bridget's comb" — shape of flower spike)
- Latin (classical): *betonica*, *vettonica*

**Etymology:**
*Betonica* derives from Gaulish *bettona* (via Latin) or possibly from the Vettones — a Celtic tribe of western Iberia whom Pliny cited as discovering the plant's virtues. The Italian province of Betona in Umbria is also proposed. *Officinalis* indicates pharmacopoeial recognition.

**Alternative etymology — most cited:**
Pliny (*Naturalis Historia* XXV.46): "The Vettones, a Spanish people, were the first to discover this plant, and therefore named it Vettonica." Whether this reflects genuine discovery attribution or Roman myth-making about barbarian botanical knowledge, the attribution gave betony its name and pharmacological prestige.

**Botanical description:**
Perennial herb, 20–60 cm. Stems erect, simple (rarely branched), quadrangular, hairy. Leaves basal (forming rosette) and stem (few pairs), opposite, oblong-cordate, 3–7 cm, crenate margins, hairy; basal leaves long-petiolate, stem leaves sessile. Flowers bright reddish-purple (rarely white), bilabiate, in dense terminal spike. Calyx 5-toothed, with long-pointed teeth. Nutlets 4. Characteristic of woodland edges, meadows, chalk grasslands.

**Conservation status:**
*B. officinalis* / *S. betonica* has declined significantly in northern Europe due to habitat loss (woodland clearing, agricultural intensification). Listed as vulnerable or near-threatened in several European countries. Conservation has pharmacological significance — collecting from wild populations problematic; cultivation required for commercial supply.

The decline of betony's pharmacological reputation after the Renaissance (as Galenic medicine was replaced by chemical medicine) makes it a case study in pharmacological knowledge discontinuity.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Roman primacy — Antonio Musa's *De Herba Betonica*:**
The existence of a dedicated monograph (not merely an entry in a larger text) by the personal physician to Emperor Augustus makes betony unique among European herbs of the classical period. Musa's 47 uses include: head pain, eye complaints, liver, spleen, stomach disorders, chest pain, gout, joint pain, intestinal worms, convulsions, fevers, and venomous bites. This comprehensive list — while undoubtedly containing some pharmacological stretching toward panacea — anchors betony in serious Roman medical practice.

The 47 uses are potentially classifiable by modern mechanism: many "head complaints" align with possible migraine or tension headache through alkaloid/flavonoid activity; "liver and stomach" through bitter tonic mechanisms; "epilepsy" through possible stachydrine or alkaloid CNS effects.

**Medieval European consensus:**
Medieval herbals universally continue Roman betony veneration:
- *Pseudo-Apuleius Herbarius* (4th–5th century CE): 29 betony uses
- *Macer Floridus* (c. 1070): extensive betony coverage for head complaints, worms, digestion
- Hildegard von Bingen (*Physica*, c. 1150): betony for headache and emotional disturbance

The consistency across 1,500 years of European botanical literature suggests genuine pharmacological activity underlying the hyperbolic panacea claims.

**Modern reappraisal:** Contemporary Western herbalism (Hoffmann, Menzies-Trull, McIntyre) has partially rehabilitated betony as a primary nervine for nervous headache, anxiety, and digestive conditions — uses mechanistically supported by stachydrine and alkaloid content.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Alkaloids (primary pharmacological class):**
- **Stachydrine** (proline betaine): quaternary ammonium compound; **shared with motherwort** (*Leonurus cardiaca*, I-394); vasodilatory; cardiac; anti-platelet; moderate concentration (~0.3–0.8% in aerial parts); likely responsible for many traditional cardiovascular claims
- **Betonicine** (4-hydroxystachydrine): hydroxylated stachydrine; similar activity
- **Turicin**: stachydrine-related alkaloid
- **Trigonelline** (nicotinic acid betaine): alkaloid also found in fenugreek; hepatoprotective; glucose metabolism

**Iridoids:**
- **Harpagide**, **8-acetylharpagide**: iridoid glucosides; anti-inflammatory; anti-pyretic; pain-relieving
- **Aucubin**: iridoid; hepatoprotective; anti-inflammatory
- **Catalpol**: neuroprotective iridoid (also in plantain, I-403)

**Phenolics:**
- **Rosmarinic acid** (0.5–1.5%): dominant non-volatile; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory
- **Betonynoside A and B**: phenylethanoid glycosides unique to betony; anti-inflammatory
- **Acteoside** (verbascoside): anti-inflammatory phenylethanoid
- **Chlorogenic acid**, **caffeic acid**: standard hydroxycinnamic phenolics
- **Luteolin**, **apigenin**: anti-inflammatory flavones
- **Hyperoside**: anti-anxiety flavonoid glycoside

**Tannins:**
- Condensed tannins (proanthocyanidins): astringent; hemostatic; anti-diarrheal
- High tannin content explains bitter, astringent taste and traditional use for gastric acidity

**Essential oil (low, 0.1–0.3%):**
- Minor volatile content; not pharmacologically primary

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Neurological mechanisms:**
1. **Stachydrine CNS effects:** Stachydrine demonstrates mild sedative activity in animal models; mechanism involves modulation of acetylcholine release and possible GABA-A enhancement; explains traditional nervine application
2. **Hyperoside GABA-A modulation:** The anxiolytic flavonoid hyperoside (quercetin-3-galactoside) acts as GABA-A positive allosteric modulator — contributes anxiolytic/sedative profile
3. **Rosmarinic acid GABA-T inhibition:** Standard Lamiaceae mechanism; synergistic with hyperoside on GABA system
4. **Catalpol neuroprotection:** Neuroprotective iridoid glycoside — reduces neuroinflammation, protects dopaminergic neurons in Parkinson models; may contribute to traditional headache use

**Cardiovascular mechanisms:**
1. **Stachydrine vasodilation:** Via NO pathway activation; reduces peripheral vascular resistance; mild antihypertensive
2. **Stachydrine anti-platelet:** Inhibits ADP-induced platelet aggregation; anti-thrombotic potential
3. **Tannin hemostasis:** Condensed tannins constrict capillaries; traditional hemostatic use for minor bleeding
4. **Stachydrine cardiac (shared with motherwort):** Mild negative chronotropy; same mechanism as in *Leonurus* (I-394)

**Anti-inflammatory / analgesic mechanisms:**
1. **Iridoid (harpagide) anti-inflammatory:** COX inhibition and NF-κB suppression; similar to harpagophytum (devil's claw) iridoids but weaker
2. **Rosmarinic acid NF-κB:** Standard Lamiaceae mechanism
3. **Betonynoside A/B:** Unique to betony; anti-inflammatory mechanism not fully characterized; cytokine suppression demonstrated in vitro

**Bitter/digestive mechanism:**
1. **Tannin-driven bitter tonic:** High tannin + iridoid combination stimulates gastric acid secretion, improves gastric motility, reduces gastric acidity (paradoxically — tannins reduce irritation when acidic secretion is already appropriate); explains traditional stomach tonic use

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Level A:** No modern human RCTs for *Betonica officinalis*.

**Level B — In vitro / animal / pilot:**
- **Stachydrine cardiovascular:** Animal studies confirm vasodilatory and anti-platelet effects (shared mechanism with motherwort); clinical extrapolation from motherwort research
- **Neuroprotection:** Animal models of Parkinson's disease; catalpol-containing betony extracts reduced dopaminergic neuron loss and improved motor performance
- **Antimicrobial:** In vitro studies confirm activity against common pathogen panel; rosmarinic acid and tannin contribution

**Historical pharmacological evidence:**
The 47-use Roman monograph of Antonio Musa, combined with 1,500 years of consistent European clinical application, constitutes the most extensive traditional clinical evidence base for any European herb despite the absence of modern RCTs. This asymmetry between traditional documentation depth and modern clinical study is one of the most striking examples in European pharmacognosy.

**Level C — Traditional evidence (particularly rich):**
- **Nervous headache:** Most consistent single indication across Roman and medieval texts; stachydrine + hyperoside + rosmarinic acid combination mechanistically plausible
- **Digestive disorders:** Bitter tonic, carminative, antispasmodic — tannin + iridoid mechanism supports
- **Bleeding (topical):** Tannin hemostatic; traditional application to wounds
- **Liver and kidney support:** Iridoid hepatoprotective; traditional "liver herb" claim
- **Respiratory:** Medieval expectorant use; possible rosmarinic acid mechanism

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Antonio Musa's *De Herba Betonica* (c. 30 BCE):**
This monograph, written by the personal physician of Emperor Augustus (the same physician credited with treating Augustus's illness with cold water baths — an early evidence of water therapy), is the most significant work ever devoted to a single European herb. Musa lists 47 uses and prefaced the work: "Before all the plants that our nature produces, betony occupies the first place." He recommends keeping it in a garden for health protection and carrying it when traveling as universal protection against venomous animals, disease, and evil spirits.

The 47 uses, while reflecting Roman encyclopedic tendency to comprehensiveness, are consistent with the stachydrine alkaloid and iridoid pharmacology: cardiovascular (stachydrine), nervous system (sedative alkaloids), digestive (bitter tannins + iridoids), hemostatic (tannins), anti-inflammatory (harpagide, rosmarinic acid), antimicrobial (rosmarinic acid, tannins). A modern pharmacologist reviewing the 47 uses could mechanistically justify approximately 30–35 of them through betony's known chemistry.

**The Anglo-Saxon herbal tradition:**
The *Lacnunga* (Old English medical text, c. 10th century): "Betony is good against foul temptations of evil and against nightmares... one can recognize many useful properties of this plant." This double utility — medical and spiritual — reflects the unified medicine-religion framework of Anglo-Saxon healing. Betony's reputation for protection against evil spirits follows logically from its nervine properties: the calming of "frenzied minds" and nightmares (sedative effects) was interpreted spiritually.

**Medieval English proverb:**
The proverb *"Sell your coat and buy betony"* — documented in multiple medieval English agricultural texts — reflects betony's position as the single most important medicinal herb in English folk pharmacology. The instruction to spend scarce resources on betony above clothing encodes pharmacological priority: betony was considered essential for health, especially headache, one of the most common and debilitating chronic conditions in pre-analgesic Europe.

**Victorian decline:**
By the 19th century, betony had largely fallen from medical favor in orthodox European medicine. The *British Pharmaceutical Codex* removed it, and most Victorian physicians who mentioned it did so dismissively as a "superstitious herb." This decline represents a pharmacological knowledge loss — the active stachydrine alkaloids and iridoids were not identified until the 20th century, by which time betony had already been relegated to folk medicine.

**20th–21st century rehabilitation:**
British herbalists (David Hoffmann, Anne McIntyre, Christopher Menzies-Trull) rehabilitated betony as a primary herb for nervous headache and anxiety within the tradition of Western medical herbalism. The reemergence of betony in contemporary phytotherapy represents a reversal of Victorian dismissal — the pre-scientific traditional evidence is being reevaluated through the lens of now-identified phytochemistry.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Therapeutic applications:**

*Nervous headache and anxiety (Level C, strong mechanistic basis):*
- **Tincture (1:5, 25–40% ethanol):** 2–4 mL three times daily; primary recommended preparation — alcohol extracts stachydrine and flavonoids efficiently
- **Infusion:** 2–4 g dried herb in 200 mL; steep 10–15 minutes; 2–3 cups daily; milder than tincture
- Applications: tension headache, stress-related headache, anxiety with nervous tension, mild insomnia
- Typical 4–6 week course for chronic headache; acute doses effective for situational tension headache
- Comparison to analogues: gentler than valerian, more specific to headache than passionflower; unique combination of stachydrine + hyperoside makes it pharmacologically distinct

*Digestive tonic (Level C):*
- Tincture or infusion as bitter digestive tonic; 2–4 g infusion before meals for dyspepsia, poor appetite, gastric irritation
- High tannin content helpful for diarrhea and GI hyperacidity
- Traditional post-illness convalescent herb to restore digestive function

*Topical hemostatic (Level C):*
- Dried leaf powder applied to minor cuts and wounds; tannin astringent effect reduces bleeding
- Strong cold infusion (5–8 g/200 mL, cold infusion to maximize tannin extraction) as compress for minor wounds

**Culinary integration:**
- Betony has a mildly bitter, aromatic taste; historically used in herbal ales and wines (betony ale, betony wine)
- Young leaves usable in salads in very small amounts
- Betony tea (1–2 g dried herb, 1 cup) as general health tonic — traditional practice
- NOT a primary culinary herb; primarily medicinal

**Safety:**
- Long-standing safe use record in European tradition
- Mild laxative effect at high doses (tannin and alkaloid combination) — dose reduction resolves
- **Pregnancy:** Stachydrine uterotonic (shared with motherwort); avoid therapeutic doses in pregnancy; culinary/tea amounts uncertain
- **Breastfeeding:** No documented adverse effects but limited data; caution at therapeutic doses

**Drug interactions:**
- **Antihypertensives:** Stachydrine vasodilation; additive BP reduction; monitor
- **Anticoagulants:** Stachydrine anti-platelet; mild additive effect; monitor with warfarin
- **Sedatives:** Additive CNS depression; mild but monitor combination with benzodiazepines or opioids

**Dose references:**
- Dried herb (infusion): 2–4 g three times daily
- Tincture (1:5, 25–40% ethanol): 2–4 mL three times daily
- Traditional dose range: 3–12 g dried herb daily (Roman sources; high by modern standards)
- Maximum modern recommended: 3–6 g dried herb equivalent daily

**Note on availability:** Commercial *Betonica officinalis* products are less common than most Lamiaceae herbs due to the plant's decline. Verify source — some commercial "betony" products contain *Pedicularis canadensis* (wood betony in North American terminology — a COMPLETELY DIFFERENT plant with different pharmacology). European betony (*Betonica/Stachys officinalis*) and North American "wood betony" (*Pedicularis* species) are pharmacologically unrelated despite sharing the common name.
