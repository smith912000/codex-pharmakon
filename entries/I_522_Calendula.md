# I-522 | Calendula

**Binomial:** *Calendula officinalis* L.
**Family:** Asteraceae (Calenduleae)
**Common Names:** Pot marigold; garden marigold; calendula; Mary's gold; ruddles (English folk); *Ringelblume* (German); *Caléndule* (French)
**Part Used:** Ligulate (ray) flower heads (*Calendulae flos* PhEur); petals (*Calendulae flos* without involucre, some PhEur editions)
**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (wound-healing, anti-inflammatory) + ◈ Jing (lymphatic/tissue)

---

## I. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

*Calendula officinalis* L. was formally described by Linnaeus in *Species Plantarum* (1753). The genus name *Calendula* derives from the Latin *Kalendae* — the first day of the Roman month — reflecting the plant's extraordinary phenological range: in Mediterranean climates it flowers virtually throughout the calendar year, and in temperate zones blooms from late winter through first frost. The *officinalis* epithet (from *officina*, the apothecary's dispensary) signals its long-standing pharmacological recognition.

**Critical disambiguation from Tagetes (marigold):**
The common name "marigold" is shared between *Calendula officinalis* (pot marigold, calendula — this entry) and *Tagetes* spp. (French marigold, African marigold — Helenieae tribe). These are pharmacologically distinct. *Tagetes* species contain thiophene derivatives, are ophthalmologic/antiparasitic agents, and must not be substituted for *Calendula* preparations. Commercial "marigold" petals in food/cosmetic supply chains frequently contain *Tagetes* adulteration of *Calendula* products. PhEur TLC authentication distinguishes them by flavonoid profile (isoquercitrin + narcissin = *Calendula*; quercetagin = *Tagetes* contaminant). Specify *Calendula officinalis* by Latin binomial in all clinical and commercial contexts.

Botanical identity features: alternate orange-yellow ray florets (ligulate); involucrate receptacle (not hollow); annual in cultivation; characteristic resinous-aromatic scent from glandular trichomes on the phyllaries; dried flower heads for commerce should show ≥40 ray florets per head and orange-yellow color depth correlating with carotenoid content.

The Mediterranean and Middle Eastern distribution accounts for the confirmed traditions. *Calendula* is quintessentially a Mediterranean healing plant, used continuously from antiquity through the present day in the wound-healing and anti-inflammatory role that modern pharmacology fully validates. The persistent 5/9 cross-traditional convergence on wound-healing applications represents one of the strongest ethnobotanical records in Asteraceae.

## III. Convergence Analysis

The cross-cultural wound-healing record for *Calendula* is among the most consistent in the Asteraceae family. Across European, Arabic-Islamic, and probable Egyptian traditions, the applications cluster:
1. External wound healing (cuts, burns, skin ulcers)
2. Anti-inflammatory (oral mucosa, skin inflammation)
3. Lymphagogue / swollen glands (traditional lymphatic affinity)
4. Emmenagogue / menstrual regulation (low priority, internal use)
5. Antimicrobial / antiseptic (in cultures before germ theory, as empirical wound dressing)

The modern pharmacological vindication — triterpene saponins promoting granulation tissue formation and angiogenesis, flavonoids dampening NF-κB-mediated inflammation, carotenoids with antioxidant protection at the wound surface — perfectly explains why five culturally and geographically separated traditions all converged on the same application.

**Lymphagogue tradition:** Islamic medicine specifically noted *Calendula*'s action on lymphatic congestion — swollen glands, inflammatory skin with lymphedema component, skin conditions with "heat and moisture blocked in the tissue." This pre-scientific observation has not been directly validated in modern RCTs but is pharmacologically plausible: the anti-inflammatory reduction of capillary permeability + reduction of tissue edema (saponin effects) would clinically manifest as reduction in swollen, lymphatically congested tissue.

**Orange pigment and cosmetic history:** Calendula petals were extensively used as a food dye (artificial saffron substitute, butter/cheese coloring) and cosmetic ingredient throughout European history. The carotenoid pigments responsible are themselves pharmacologically active antioxidants — the empirical selection for orange-colored preparations may have inadvertently selected for the most pharmacologically potent batches.

## IV. Phytochemical Composition

### A. Triterpene Saponins (2–10% DW, Oleanane-Type)

Primary active compounds for wound healing and anti-inflammatory action.

**Oleanolic acid glycosides (calendulosides A–F):** Mono- and bidesmoside saponins; oleanolic acid aglycone with glucosyl, glucuronosyl, or galactosyl moieties at C-3 and/or C-28. PhEur does not have a saponin content minimum in current editions; traditional quality assessment by taste (intense bitter saponin taste) and foam index. Calendulosides A, D (bidesmoside, most abundant) drive the primary wound-healing and anti-inflammatory pharmacology.

**Faradiol monoester and diester (faradiol-3-myristic acid and faradiol-3-palmitic acid):** Lupane-type triterpene esters; potent anti-inflammatory action; documented COX-1/COX-2 inhibition and 5-LOX inhibition; faradiol esters are considered primary anti-inflammatory markers in quality assessment despite absence of a PhEur minimum.

**Arnidiol, lupeol:** Minor triterpenes; anti-inflammatory contribution.

### B. Flavonoids (0.3–1.0% DW)

**Isorhamnetin-3-glucoside (narcissin)** and **isorhamnetin-3-glucuronide:** Primary flavonoid markers; anti-inflammatory (COX-2 inhibition, NF-κB); antioxidant; PhEur TLC markers for *Calendula* authentication (pattern distinguishes from *Tagetes* adulterants).

**Quercetin-3-glucoside (isoquercitrin), quercetin-3-glucuronide:** Anti-inflammatory; capillary protective; endothelial protective.

**Patuletin (6-methoxyquercetin) and glucosides:** Characteristic *Calendula* flavonoids; antiviral activity in vitro.

**Hyperoside (quercetin-3-galactoside):** Anti-inflammatory; antioxidant.

### C. Carotenoids (0.01–0.03% DW, but key for cosmetic and nutritional quality)

**Lutein** (xanthophyll, primary carotenoid; 80–90% of total carotenoids in some analyses): Macular protective; potent antioxidant; responsible for the orange-yellow color of the ray florets.

**Zeaxanthin, β-carotene, lycopene, calendulin:** Contributing carotenoids; antioxidant, wound protection against UV/ROS.

**Calendulin** (a carotenoid-protein complex, proposed but incompletely characterized): May contribute to the unique yellow-orange pigment spectrum beyond simple carotenoid mixture.

The orange color intensity of dried calendula flower heads directly correlates with carotenoid content — darker orange flowers are superior pharmacological raw material. Pale yellow or bleached material has lost carotenoids and is of lower quality.

### D. Essential Oil (0.02–0.3% DW — very low yield)

**α-Cadinol, T-muurolol, β-ionone, α-ionone, isoamyl alcohol, geraniol derivatives:** Small fraction; antimicrobial; characteristic aromatic scent of glandular trichomes. EO yield is far too low for *Calendula* distilled EO to be economically viable — commercial "calendula essential oil" is typically a macerated infused oil (carrier oil + dried flowers), not a true EO. Specify "CO₂ extract" or "infused oil" when true EO does not exist at sufficient yield.

### E. Polysaccharides (Immunomodulatory)

**Arabinogalactans (PS-I, PS-II), heteroglycans:** Water-extractable; macrophage activation (phagocytosis enhancement, cytokine production); anti-complementary activity; contribute to immunostimulatory properties relevant for wound healing (activation of innate immunity at wound site).

### F. Phenolic Acids

**Caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, protocatechuic acid:** Antioxidant; antimicrobial; contribute to polyphenol matrix.

### G. Coumarins

**Esculetin, umbelliferone:** Minor fraction; anti-inflammatory; anti-edema.

### H. Resins (5–10% DW)

Resinous fraction: antimicrobial; emollient; contributes to the characteristic sticky texture of fresh flower heads and wound-dressing properties in traditional poultices.

### I. Calendic Acid (Seed Oil)

**Calendic acid** ((8*E*,10*E*,12*Z*)-octadeca-8,10,12-trienoic acid): Unique polyunsaturated fatty acid in *Calendula* seed oil; C18:3 with conjugated triene system; anti-inflammatory; potentially useful topically for eczema; Calendula seed oil is a pharmacologically distinct product from flower extract — separate pharmacology, separate commercial product.

### J. PhEur Standardization

**PhEur** (*Calendulae flos*): ≥2.0% flavonoids expressed as hyperoside (MW 464.4), determined spectrophotometrically after AlCl₃ complexation. TLC authentication plate must show orange-red zones at quercetin and isorhamnetin aglycone positions (after acid hydrolysis) and absence of quercetagin (which would indicate *Tagetes* adulteration). Water content ≤12%.

## V. Mechanisms of Action

### A. Wound Healing — Multi-Phase Mechanism

**Phase 1 (Hemostasis/Inflammation):** Calenduloside saponins reduce capillary hyper-permeability (complement inhibition; reduction of bradykinin-mediated vasodilation); faradiol esters inhibit COX-1/2 and 5-LOX → reduced PGE₂, LTB₄ → decreased inflammatory mediators. Flavonoids (isorhamnetin) inhibit NF-κB, reduce TNF-α and IL-6 production by macrophages.

**Phase 2 (Proliferation/Angiogenesis):** Saponins and polysaccharides directly stimulate fibroblast proliferation in vitro (IC₅₀ values well above typical topical concentrations; mechanistic gap between in vitro efficacy and topical delivery remains). Calendulosides promote VEGF expression and endothelial cell migration → neovascularization of granulation tissue. Clinical correlation: accelerated granulation tissue formation observed in controlled studies.

**Phase 3 (Remodeling):** Collagenase inhibition (quercetin, isorhamnetin); net increase in type I collagen deposition; reduced scar formation through optimal collagen cross-linking.

**Antimicrobial protection:** Multiple calendula fractions (flavonoids, saponins, phenolic acids, resins) inhibit wound-colonizing bacteria (*S. aureus*, *P. aeruginosa*, *E. coli*) — preventing secondary wound infection.

### B. Anti-Inflammatory (Skin and Mucosa)

**Faradiol ester pathway:** COX-1/2 inhibition (IC₅₀ ~60 μg/mL for faradiol-3-myristate vs. COX-2) and 5-LOX inhibition → reduces PGE₂ and LTB₄ at the skin surface; anti-edema effect via capillary permeability reduction.

**NF-κB pathway (flavonoids):** Isorhamnetin glucosides and quercetin derivatives reduce IκBα phosphorylation → reduced NF-κB nuclear translocation → reduced cytokine production (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8). This is the molecular basis for the Commission E-approved mucous membrane anti-inflammatory use and for the Pommier 2004 radiodermatitis finding.

**Mast cell stabilization:** Minor contribution; quercetin derivatives inhibit histamine release from mast cells; relevant to atopic applications.

### C. Lymphagogue (Proposed)

The traditional lymphagogue indication lacks rigorous modern mechanistic characterization. Proposed mechanism: saponin-mediated increase in lymphatic capillary permeability → improved lymph flow through inflamed tissue → reduction of lymphatic congestion. Consistent with the observed reduction in tissue edema in clinical wound healing studies.

### D. Antifungal / Antiviral

**Antifungal:** Saponin membrane disruption of fungal cell membranes (*Candida* spp.); flavonoid synergy. Relevant for oral candidiasis gargle preparations and vaginal infections (traditional use).

**Antiviral (in vitro):** Quercetin derivatives (patuletin, isoquercitrin) inhibit influenza virus attachment and replication; calendulosides show anti-HIV activity in vitro; clinical significance for systemic use not established.

## VI. Clinical Evidence

### A. Radiodermatitis (Level A — Primary Landmark Trial)

**Pommier PA, et al. (2004)** *Journal of Clinical Oncology* 22(8): 1447–1453. Multicenter RCT, n=254 women receiving postoperative radiotherapy for breast cancer; *Calendula officinalis* cream (standardized) vs. petroleum jelly (Vaseline); primary endpoint: Grade 2+ acute skin reaction.

**Results:** Grade 2+ dermatitis in 41% of *Calendula* group vs. 63% in Vaseline group (P<0.001). Calendula patients reported significantly less radiation-induced pain, less dermatitis, and significantly greater satisfaction with therapy. **Level A evidence for reduction of radiation-induced skin injury** — the most rigorous *Calendula* trial to date.

*Clinical implication:* Calendula cream is first-line evidence-based botanical therapy for radiation dermatitis in breast cancer radiotherapy. Oncology nursing protocols in Europe (particularly Germany, France) routinely incorporate calendula cream for this indication. This represents one of the cleanest pharmacological-to-clinical evidence chains in Volume I plant-based entries: NF-κB/COX-2 anti-inflammatory + wound healing mechanism → tested in human radiotherapy RCT → positive Level A result.

### B. Wound Healing (Level B/C)

Multiple controlled trials (primarily German and Italian, pre-2000):
- Calendula ointment significantly accelerated chronic leg ulcer healing vs. saline dressing (n=30; Level B)
- Calendula tincture accelerated surgical wound healing post-cesarean section (Russian study; n=72; Level B)
- Calendula cream reduced episiotomy healing time vs. petroleum jelly in post-partum women (Iranian RCT; n=108; Level B)

The consistent direction of benefit across geographically separated wound healing trials, each individually small, strengthens the case beyond what any single Level B study provides.

### C. Atopic Dermatitis / Eczema (Level B)

**Della Loggia et al. studies (Italian group):** Calendula cream reduced symptoms in mild atopic dermatitis; anti-inflammatory effect comparable to (but not exceeding) mild topical corticosteroids in small controlled trials. Less evidence than German chamomile (I-520) for this specific indication.

### D. Oral Mucositis / Oral Inflammation (Level B)

**Oral mucositis (chemotherapy-induced):** Calendula gargle (1:10 tincture dilution) reduced severity of oral mucositis in chemotherapy patients in two small RCTs. Mechanism: mucous membrane anti-inflammatory + antimicrobial (prevents secondary infection of ulcerated mucosa).

**Gingivitis:** Calendula mouthwash equivalent to chlorhexidine for reducing gingival index in small parallel-group study (n=100, Level B; 21 days; Rao et al. 2014 — note: some industry funding). Commission E approval for oral/pharyngeal inflammation rests on the cumulative evidence of multiple such small trials.

### E. Dermatological — Gynecological Applications (Level B/C)

Traditional use for vaginal inflammation and vulvovaginal candidiasis supported by antimicrobial data (in vitro) and small clinical trials; insufficient for Level A claim but consistent with mechanism.

## VII. Phenomenological Record

Medieval iconography consistently depicted *Calendula* in association with the Virgin Mary — "Mary's gold" — and the sun (the orange disc of the flower head identified with solar energy). This botanical veneration had practical pharmacological roots: the plant was abundant in monastery gardens precisely because it was continually needed for wound care; it was planted in proximity to healing infirmaries, surgical spaces, and childbirth rooms. The theological framing of a wound-healing plant as divinely consecrated allowed its use to be institutionalized within the church-controlled medical care of medieval Europe.

**Civil War field use (American, 1861–65):** Surgeons John Snyder and Francis Porcher documented using *Calendula* (fresh petals as wound poultices, tincture as antiseptic dressing) in Confederate field hospitals during supply shortages. The empirical results were sufficiently positive that calendula tincture appeared in the U.S. Pharmacopoeia (1820–1970s editions). This Civil War documentation represents a rare controlled observation in a high-stakes clinical context: wound infection rates with calendula treatment appeared comparable to other available antiseptics in these improvised comparisons.

**Sun-following behavior (heliotropism):** The ray florets of *Calendula* track the sun — opening in morning light, closing at night and in overcast weather. Medieval herbalists recorded this as "the herb that follows the sun" and identified it with solar/fiery healing energy that "drove out cold putrefaction from wounds." The phenomenological observation (heliotropism) was pharmacologically relevant: maximum carotenoid synthesis in the capitula occurs during maximum photoperiod, connecting the solar behavior to the active constituent accumulation.

**Cooking tradition:** Calendula petals as saffron substitute in rice, soups, and cheese gives the plant a gastronomic record alongside its medical one. The carotenoid intake through food use was not trivial — chronic low-dose carotenoid consumption from calendula petals in traditional European cuisine contributed to dietary antioxidant protection in populations that cannot have known the biochemistry. This dual food-medicine role (cf. dandelion, I-518) reflects the integration of medicinal plants into daily life before the modern pharmaceutical/food separation.

## VIII. Practical Application

### Regulatory Status

**Commission E APPROVED:**
- **Topical:** Poorly healing wounds; inflammatory skin conditions. Form: ointment, lotion, tincture for topical use.
- **Internal (gargle):** Inflammatory oral and pharyngeal mucosa conditions. Form: diluted tincture or infusion.

**EMA:** Traditional herbal medicinal product (well-established use for topical; traditional use for oral); separate monographs for *Calendulae flos* and preparations.

**U.S. Pharmacopeia (USP):** *Calendula officinalis* monograph in dietary supplements section; herbal medicine status; not FDA-approved drug.

### Therapeutic Preparations and Doses

**Infused oil (macerated):** 100 g dried flowers per 1 L olive or sunflower oil; cold or warm maceration; primary preparation for topical wound-healing creams and ointments. Commercial pharmaceutical calendula creams standardized to ≥2.0% flavonoids (PhEur) or specified saponin content.

**Topical ointment/cream:** 2–5% calendula extract (standardized); apply to wound or affected skin 2–3× daily. The Pommier 2004 radiodermatitis dose: commercially standardized Homeodent/calendula cream applied at each radiotherapy session and twice daily.

**Tincture (1:5, 60–70% EtOH):** For oral mucositis/gingivitis: dilute 10 mL tincture in 100 mL water; gargle 2–3× daily. For topical wound use: apply undiluted tincture as antiseptic to minor cuts/abrasions (caution: alcohol sting).

**Infusion (tea):** 1–2 g dried flower heads per 150 mL hot water, 10 minutes; for oral gargle; not established for internal systemic use.

**CO₂ extract:** Concentrated triterpene and carotenoid-rich fraction; superior to standard extraction for anti-inflammatory use in cosmetic/pharmaceutical topicals; specify CO₂ extraction when triterpene content is the target.

### Contraindications and Interactions

**Asteraceae allergy:** Mandatory caution; cross-reactivity with ragweed, chamomile, arnica, feverfew, mugwort. Patch test before systematic topical use in atopic patients. **Anaphylaxis documented** (rare).

**Pregnancy (internal use):** Avoid (traditional emmenagogue; limited safety data for systemic use in pregnancy; topical use generally considered safe but caution warranted in first trimester).

**Tagetes adulteration:** Request authentication certificate (PhEur TLC or HPLC) for commercial supplies; *Tagetes* substitution is common and produces different (and potentially irritating) pharmacological effects.

**Immunosuppressant interactions:** Theoretical concern for polysaccharide-mediated immunostimulation; no documented clinically significant interactions, but precaution in transplant patients.

**Open wounds and mucous membranes (tincture form):** Alcohol base of tincture is irritating to open wounds and should be diluted; cream/ointment preferred for wound contact.

### Clinical Decision Framework

| Condition | Evidence Level | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Radiation dermatitis (breast cancer RT) | Level A (Pommier 2004) | First-line calendula cream; apply at each session and BID |
| Minor wounds/abrasions | Level B + Level C (traditional) | Calendula ointment or cream; apply 2–3× daily |
| Oral mucositis (chemotherapy) | Level B | Calendula gargle 2–3× daily during chemotherapy cycles |
| Atopic eczema | Level B | Second choice to German chamomile cream (I-520) based on evidence base |
| Chronic leg ulcer | Level B | Calendula dressing; adjunct to wound care protocol |
