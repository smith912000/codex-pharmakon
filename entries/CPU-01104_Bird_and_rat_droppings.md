# Bird and rat droppings — "Bird and Rat droppings" (Clark 1995) / "The droppings of birds and rats" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *The dung of birds (species not given) and of rats (species not given); no Tibetan names or binomials are printed for either* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

This entry is one clause of one text, in two translations. Pigeon droppings, vulture droppings and the droppings of other named animals sit in the same passage and are separate items in the text; they are not written up here.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The locus is the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, in Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, line 1199). The chapter's section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" names thirteen kinds of animal medicine, of which droppings are the twelfth (line 1148: "horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body"). Under the heading "Droppings" the list runs through vulture, pig, human, horse, rabbit, dog and wolf, and reads: "Bird and Rat droppings: both drain pus [accumulations]." It goes straight on to "phug.ron: Pigeon droppings: Columba rupestris rupestris Pallas: suppurate swellings", so the pigeon is named separately and the "bird" of this line is not the pigeon. Clark's index lists "Rat droppings" (line 1636).

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3568) has the same item: "The droppings of birds and rats drain pus." Both translations agree on the action. Neither names a bird species or a rat species, and neither gives the Tibetan words. Which birds and which rats the Tantra meant is not stated in the lines read. A related item sits in the section on skins, in both translations: "Rat and Mouse skin: drains all types of pus accumulations" (Clark line 1185), "Rat skin drains out pus" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3555). The Tantra thus names rat skin and rat and bird droppings for the same action. This is a statement of what the text lists, not an explanation of why.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, bird droppings are a mix of faecal matter and uric acid (birds excrete nitrogen mainly as uric acid, not urea), and rodent droppings are digested food residue with gut bacteria. Both carry the microbial load of the animal's gut and its surroundings.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The texts record one action ("drain pus") and no method, and the Codex recommends none. Wild animal droppings are a recognised source of disease (see Contraindications), and applying them to a pus-filled swelling is the opposite of wound care. Collections of pus need clean drainage and medical attention.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources give no preparation, no form (fresh, dried, burnt, as a poultice or a fomentation) and no amount; the pigeon-dropping item beside this one adds a fomentation, but that is stated for pigeon only and is not carried over. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Bird droppings can carry the fungus that causes histoplasmosis and the one that causes cryptococcosis, and the bacteria *Salmonella* and *Chlamydia psittaci*. Rodent droppings and urine can carry hantaviruses, *Leptospira*, *Salmonella* and other pathogens, and dust from dried droppings can be inhaled. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** none printed. The two translations give an action only ("drain pus"). Neither prints a taste, post-digestive taste or potency for droppings, and the passages read do not assign them to the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* The droppings sit inside the Explanatory Tantra's section on medicines of animal origin, as one of the thirteen kinds, with a stated action on pus. No passage read assigns them a cultivation status, and none gives them a role in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any subtle-body practice.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here makes droppings a substance for cultivation, and the safe reading of this entry is to leave the material alone. It is a record of what one text listed for pus. A cultivator takes nothing from it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** the principal risk. Bird and rodent droppings carry pathogens that can cause serious infection through broken skin, the eyes, the mouth or inhaled dust. Do not handle dry droppings without protection, and do not apply them to a wound or swelling.
- **Medical care:** an accumulation of pus (an abscess) can lead to spreading infection and sepsis, and needs medical drainage and, often, antibiotics.
- **Ethics and law:** wild birds and some rodents are protected in some places; this entry names no species.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** several of the pathogens named above are dangerous in pregnancy; avoid contact.
