# Silver Lime / Silver Linden

## *Tilia tomentosa* Moench | Family: Malvaceae (formerly Tiliaceae)

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Cool–Moist, relaxing/diaphoretic — *genus-level attribution (tilleul);* not independently graded for *T. tomentosa*
**Training Phase:** Foundation — Signature Study

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Tilia tomentosa* Moench — silver lime, silver linden — is a large deciduous tree of the mallow family (Malvaceae; the old family Tiliaceae is now sunk into it), native to south-eastern Europe and south-western Asia and widely planted as an urban street and park tree. Its common name comes from the dense white-felted (tomentose) underside of the leaves, which flashes silver in wind. Like all limes it bears fragrant, pale-yellow flower clusters in high summer, each subtended by the characteristic strap-shaped pale bract; **the flower-with-bract inflorescence is the medicinal part** recorded in pharmacopoeias.

Silver lime sits comfortably inside the classic **"tilleul"** tradition — the gentle European lime-flower tisane taken for colds, feverish chills, restlessness and mild nervous tension. Its close congeners *Tilia cordata* (small-leaved lime) and *Tilia platyphyllos* (large-leaved lime) are already in the codex and are the species most often meant by "lime flower / linden" in the European herbal record. The honest position for *T. tomentosa* is that it is a **genuine member** of the same medicinal genus that shares the tisane use — and, unusually for the three plants in this batch, it also carries a body of **species-specific** pharmacology (below). So the borrowing runs less deep here than for the sage or the vervain: much of the classic linden sedative/diaphoretic pharmacology is properly **genus-level**, but silver lime has been directly studied.

**DMM (Dioscorides / Greek herbal stream).** Dioscorides describes *philyra* — the lime/linden tree — noting uses of its bark, leaves and inflorescence. The classical *philyra* is the lime genus broadly; it cannot be pinned to *T. tomentosa* as a distinct drug, and the northern-European medicinal limes (*T. cordata*/*platyphyllos*) are the more usual referents. **[USER: verify]** — treat the DMM tag as genus-level attestation for *Tilia*, inherited by silver lime through shared genus and identical use, not as a species-specific classical record.

**CM (Avicenna, *Canon of Medicine*, Unani).** The lime/linden appears in the broader materia medica as a cooling, relaxing, sweat-promoting remedy for febrile and nervous complaints. As with the Greek stream, a specific *T. tomentosa* attestation cannot be substantiated; the humoral reading is a genus-level one. **[USER: verify].**

### Active Components

*T. tomentosa* flower/inflorescence chemistry is characterised and typical of the genus:

- **Flavonol glycosides** — quercetin and kaempferol derivatives are the signature bioactives of silver-lime flowers, explicitly linked to its intestinal-relaxant and neurological activity.
- **Tiliroside** — the kaempferol-3-*O*-(6″-*p*-coumaroyl)-glucoside characteristic of *Tilia*, reported at genus level (flavonoid/coumaroyl-glycoside class).
- **Volatile oil** — a fragrant flower oil (the aroma of the tisane); farnesol-type and monoterpene constituents at genus level.
- **Mucilage** — the demulcent polysaccharide fraction giving lime-flower tea its soothing, film-forming quality.
- **Phenolic acids** — the flowers/bracts show high total phenolic and flavonoid content with notable antioxidant activity in comparative *Tilia* assays.

**Congener note:** where a specific constituent below is cited from *T. cordata*, *T. platyphyllos* or *T. rubra*, it is named as such. The flavonol (quercetin/kaempferol) and tiliroside profile is broadly shared across the genus; the quercetin/kaempferol glycosides have been quantified in *T. tomentosa* directly.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica, silver lime is filed as a **gentle nervine-diaphoretic** — a "cooling relaxant." Its Kenetica role is the domestic tilleul register: a warm flower infusion for the onset of a cold or fever (to encourage a light sweat), for evening restlessness, mild anxious tension and difficulty settling to sleep, and as a soothing demulcent drink for a dry, irritable throat. Unusually for a Signature Study plant, silver lime has **direct mechanistic support** for the nervine claim (GABA-A / benzodiazepine-receptor activity, below), which lifts it above pure tradition — though still short of human trial evidence.

The cultivator's use-case: an easy, long-lived amenity and pollinator tree whose summer flowers are harvested at first opening (with bract) and dried in shade for tisane. It also serves as the codex's teaching specimen for a **misattributed toxicity myth** (the "bee-killing tree," corrected below) — a mirror-image of the name-inheritance lesson: here the plant inherited a *bad* reputation it did not deserve.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Preparation | Method | Register / caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Lime-flower tisane (tilleul) | ~1–2 g dried flower-with-bract per cup, hot-water infusion, 5–10 min, covered | Traditional domestic use; the standard form |
| Bud extract (gemmotherapy-style) | Macerate of leaf/flower buds | Basis of the GABA-A/BDZ in-vitro work; not a validated dosed medicine |
| Encapsulated flower extract | Spray-dried, starch-wall microencapsulation | Research/nutraceutical formulation (stabilises quercetin/kaempferol glycosides) |

**No fabricated doses.** There is no established, trial-validated human therapeutic dose for *T. tomentosa*. Treat internal use at food/tisane level. Bud-extract and microencapsulation data are laboratory/formulation findings, not dosing guidance.

### Scientific Correlation

Claims graded:

- **Sedative / anxiolytic via GABAergic mechanism — MECHANISTIC, species-specific (preclinical).** *T. tomentosa* bud extracts activated a GABA-A chloride current in mouse hippocampal neurons, partly flumazenil-sensitive (i.e. acting at both GABA-A and benzodiazepine sites) and suppressed synchronous network firing. An earlier study isolated benzodiazepine-receptor ligands from *T. tomentosa* inflorescences; kaempferol bound weakly (Ki ≈ 93 µM) and was inactive in vivo, but a flavonoid-rich fraction produced a clear anxiolytic effect in mice (elevated plus-maze, holeboard) without impairing locomotion. *Graded: preclinical (in vitro + rodent), species-specific — a real mechanistic basis, but no human trials.*
- **Intestinal (smooth-muscle) relaxant — species-specific (preclinical).** *T. tomentosa* flower extract showed intestinal contractile/relaxant activity in a murine model, retained after microencapsulation, attributed to quercetin/kaempferol glycosides. *Graded: preclinical.*
- **Antioxidant / anti-inflammatory / anticancer — GENUS-LEVEL (preclinical).** Comparative work on *Tilia* flowers/bracts (*T. cordata*, *T. platyphyllos*, *T. rubra*, *T. tomentosa*) found antioxidant activity, inhibition of LPS-induced nitrite/IL-6/PGE₂, and cytotoxicity against pancreatic cancer cells — but the strongest effects were from *T. rubra*, not silver lime. *Graded: preclinical, genus-level; do not overstate for T. tomentosa specifically.*
- **Antidiabetic (enzyme-inhibition) — WEAK/genus-adjacent (preclinical).** *T. tomentosa* extract had high phenolic/flavonoid content and antioxidant activity in a 14-plant screen, but the notable α-glucosidase/lipase inhibition was from *Alhagi maurorum*, not silver lime. *Graded: preclinical; silver lime not a standout.*
- **Human sedative/diaphoretic efficacy — NULL / TRADITIONAL.** No randomised or controlled human trials of *T. tomentosa* for anxiety, insomnia, cold or fever were retrieved. The diaphoretic/cold-remedy use rests on tradition; the sedative use has mechanistic (not clinical) support. *Graded: traditional, with preclinical mechanism.*

**THE BEE-TOXICITY TWIST — corrected.** Silver lime carries a centuries-old claim that its nectar/flowers are **toxic to bees**, blamed on the sugar **mannose**, and pointed to by the sight of dead and dying bumblebees found under flowering silver limes in late summer. The modern review by Koch & Stevenson (2017, *Biology Letters*) reassessed this and found **no experimental evidence for toxicity in linden nectar**. The mass bee deaths are best explained by **starvation** — silver lime flowers late, when little other forage remains, and bees exhaust themselves on a nectar source that is drying up rather than being poisoned. There is some evidence the nectar contains **caffeine**, which may "deceive" foragers into continuing to visit an under-rewarding source, worsening the starvation risk. **The mannose-toxicity doctrine is refuted; the corrected view is late-season forage scarcity.** The practical cultivator's lesson is to plant complementary late-summer forage near silver limes, not to treat the tree as a poisoner. *(This contradicts the folk "toxic to bees" hook; per standing instruction it is refused and the correction is written.)*

### Traditional Energetic Classification

The Cool–Moist, relaxing and diaphoretic reading in the header is the standard humoral/Western-herbal characterisation of **lime flower (tilleul) as a genus**: cooling, moistening (mucilaginous), gently relaxing to the nervous system and opening the pores to promote sweat in fever. It is applied to *T. tomentosa* by genus membership and identical use rather than a distinct classical grading of silver lime specifically. Recorded as genus-level.

### Contraindications

- **Cardiac / frequent-use caution — [USER: verify]:** a long-repeated caution holds that very frequent, prolonged lime-flower use may be inadvisable for people with certain heart conditions. This claim is widely stated but poorly substantiated in the primary literature; no controlled evidence was retrieved. Flagged for verification; not asserted as established. Occasional tisane use is regarded as safe.
- **Allergy:** Malvaceae / lime-flower allergy is possible but rare; discontinue on any hypersensitivity reaction.
- **Sedative interaction (theoretical):** given the demonstrated GABA-A/benzodiazepine-receptor activity of *T. tomentosa* bud extract in vitro, concentrated extracts could in principle add to the effect of sedatives, benzodiazepines or alcohol. Ordinary tisane use is low-risk; avoid combining strong extracts with CNS depressants.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** insufficient species-specific safety data for medicinal/extract doses; keep to food/tisane level and seek advice for concentrated preparations.
- **Not a validated treatment:** absence of human trials means silver lime should not be presented as a treatment for anxiety, insomnia or fever; use stays at traditional tisane level.
- **Ecological note (not a human contraindication):** the "toxic to bees" belief is refuted; however, planting silver lime as a *sole* late-summer forage can still contribute to bee starvation — pair with other late-flowering forage.
