# Delphinium himalayi — Jakang, bya rkang (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary, by column position)

## *Delphinium himalayi, as printed "Delphinium himalayi Munz." in the scientific-name run of the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary's "J" block; the Tibetan name "Jakang" is set against it by column position only, and Clark 1995 prints no line for this binomial* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this binomial only by the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Clark 1995 prints no *Delphinium himalayi* in its text or in its index of Latin names (line 1578 lists *Delphinium brunonianum*, *Delphinium cashmirianum*, *Delphinium grandiflorum* and *Delphinium viscosum*). The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The entry rests on one glossary line for the binomial and on running-text lines for the Tibetan name that the glossary does not print beside it. The extraction note reads: "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 289). The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (lines 5335 to 5355).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "J" block (the letter at line 5335). The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. The phonetic names (lines 5337 to 5340) are "Jagoepoe", "Jakang", "Jeruk", "Ji tsher", "Jhapo Tsi tsi", "Jhew laphuk" and "Jitanga", seven in all. The transliterations (line 5342) are "bya rgod spos bya rkang byi rug byi tsher bya pho rtsi rtsi byi'u la phug byi tang ga", also seven. The scientific run begins on line 5344 with "Delphinium brunonianum Delphinium himalayi Munz. Mentha longifolia" and continues with "Xanthium strumarium" (5345), "Pisum sativum" (5346), "Torularia humilis" (5347) and "Embelia ribes" (5348). The English names are "Larkspur Larkspur" (line 5350), "Horsemint", "Cocklebur", "Garden pea", "Alpencress" and "Marsh nut". Counting in order, "Jakang" is the second phonetic name and "bya rkang" the second transliteration, and the second scientific name is *Delphinium himalayi*, against the second "Larkspur". The Codex therefore reads the glossary as pairing "Jakang" with *Delphinium himalayi*, by column position only and not as a printed row. The count can be checked on neighbours: "Ji tsher" stands fourth against *Xanthium strumarium* and "Cocklebur", and Clark's *byi.tsher* is a cocklebur entry; and "Jagoepoe" stands first against *Delphinium brunonianum*, which Clark prints for *bya.rgod.spos* (line 1011). *The same Tibetan name in the other texts.* The transliteration "bya rkang" is spelled as Clark spells *bya.rkang*. Clark's entry (lines 1125 to 1128) gives "Larkspur: Delphinium grandiflorum: Delphinium viscosum", a slightly blue leaf and a reddish-blue flower shaped like the head of a hoopoe bird, a "slightly bitter taste", and says that the drug "halts dysentery and is beneficial against external parasites, lice, etc.", and that "Plantago, Polygonum alopecuroides and larkspur halt diarrhea". The Men-Tsee-Khang running text prints the name as "Jhakang", not "Jakang". In chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), line 3493 ends: "Tharam, Naram and Jhakang stops diarrhea." In chapter 21, lines 3644 to 3646 read: "Kapae, Bilwa, Dhatig, Moen chhara, Mak, Tharam, Naram, Gya kyeg, antelope's blood and Jhakang are the group of medicines that cure diarrhea." The index (line 7403) lists "Jhakang 211,222". The text gives no Latin name in either passage. The Codex reads "Jhakang" as Clark's *bya.rkang* from the order (*tha.ram*, *na.ram*, larkspur in Clark; Tharam, Naram, Jhakang in Men-Tsee-Khang) and the action, and reads the glossary's "Jakang" as the same name from the spelling; the text says neither. A search of the Men-Tsee-Khang file for "Jakang" found only the glossary line 5337. [USER: verify]

*What this means for the identification.* The two Tibetan texts agree on the Tibetan name and on the action (diarrhoea), and differ on the species: Clark names *Delphinium grandiflorum* (and *D. viscosum*), and the glossary, by column position, *Delphinium himalayi*. The Codex does not choose between them. Everything this entry says about the drug's action is therefore carried by the name and not by the binomial.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Delphinium himalayi* and "Jakang" found no entry on this plant. I_974 (*Xanthium strumarium*) and I_1250 (*Celosia cristata*) quote the same "J" block of the glossary in their prose and are not entries on this drug. I_1265 (*Delphinium grandiflorum*), written in this batch, is the Clark row for the same Tibetan name, and I_1264 (*Delphinium brunonianum*) is a sibling row of the same genus. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Delphinium* is a genus of the buttercup family (Ranunculaceae) and that larkspurs are generally known to contain toxic diterpenoid alkaloids. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Men-Tsee-Khang text records that "Jhakang" "stops diarrhea" (line 3493) and lists it among the medicines that cure diarrhoea (lines 3644 to 3646). Clark's entry for the same Tibetan name, under another binomial, adds that it halts dysentery and is beneficial against external parasites. These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial by a column position and a spelling match, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Tibetan terms (diarrhoea, dysentery, external parasites) are named conditions and not measured outcomes in these texts, so no modern finding can be set beside them here. The identity of the drug rests on a column position, so a study of one *Delphinium* would not bear directly on it. It is a general, well-established fact that larkspurs are poisonous to grazing animals and to people who eat them.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** No taste, power or post-digestive taste is printed for "Jakang" or "Jhakang" in the Men-Tsee-Khang lines read, and the name does not appear in the taste-group lists of chapter 19. Clark's entry for *bya.rkang* gives a "slightly bitter taste" inside its brackets (lines 1125 to 1128), which belongs to the Clark name and is carried here only through the reading of the name as one.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Men-Tsee-Khang text classes the drug named Jhakang by what it does: it stops diarrhoea, and it stands in the group of medicines that cure diarrhoea. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the passages cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and the link from this binomial to that framing rests on a column position and a spelling match.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is an anti-diarrhoeal drug in the Men-Tsee-Khang text, the genus is toxic, the glossary pairs its name with this binomial only by column order, and there is no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. *Delphinium* is generally known to be poisonous, the identity rests on a column position, and no preparation or dose is given, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Dysentery and persistent diarrhoea need medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
