### Origin & Tradition

*Solanum tuberosum* is a herbaceous perennial of the nightshade family, grown as an annual, producing starchy underground stem tubers from stolons. Above ground it is unmistakably a Solanaceae: compound pinnate leaves, five-lobed white to purple rotate flowers with a prominent yellow anther cone, and — in some cultivars — small green berries that look like tiny tomatoes. Those berries are **not food**. The eaten organ is the tuber, a swollen stem, not a root.

**Origin is Andean and unambiguous.** The potato was domesticated in the high Andes of what is now southern Peru and adjacent Bolivia, from wild *Solanum* relatives, at altitudes where frost, thin air and short seasons ruled out most cereals. Andean farmers developed thousands of landraces adapted to microclimate and altitude, along with the freeze-dry processing method (*chuño*) that made high-glycoalkaloid, frost-exposed tubers storable and safer. The Andean origin is corroborated even from unexpected directions: according to PubMed, Fuentes and colleagues (2022, *Plant Disease*) sequenced 50 new isolates of Potato virus V and found the population's basal phylogroups are all Andean (Peru, Colombia), with a single lineage spreading worldwide — the virus, like its host, "originated in the Andean region" and was carried to Europe in the Columbian Exchange. The pathogens track the crop's history.

**On the tradition tags — read this before citing either.**

**TCM.** The tag reads **"Chinese medicine," and in this Codex it denotes POST-COLUMBIAN adoption of the potato into Chinese materia medica.** It does **not** denote the *Shennong Bencao Jing* (c. 200 CE) or any pre-Columbian Old-World source. This is a formal relabelling already ruled by the project owner and it must be reproduced as written. The potato reached China only after transoceanic contact — most plausibly via Spanish Manila trade and coastal ports from the late sixteenth or seventeenth century onward — and was absorbed first as a famine and staple crop, and only secondarily given a materia medica reading in the existing sweet/neutral, spleen-and-stomach-tonifying idiom. Any text presenting potato as an ancient Chinese herb is anachronistic. Do not assert a pre-Columbian Old-World attestation under any circumstances.

**BM (Badianus).** The *Libellus de Medicinalibus Indorum Herbis* (the Badianus Manuscript, 1552) is an Aztec/Nahua herbal from central Mexico. The potato is Andean, not Mesoamerican, and was not a staple of the Valley of Mexico at that date. A genuine *S. tuberosum* entry in the Badianus is therefore **improbable on geographic grounds**, and I could not substantiate one. The tag most likely reflects a "New World Solanaceae" grouping rather than a specific attestation. **[USER: verify against the Badianus facsimile/Emmart or Gates edition before this tag is published; if unverified, the tag should be demoted or removed.]**

**Folk medicinal use** — genuine, and separable from the two contested tags. In European and North American domestic practice, and continuing today, scraped **raw potato or expressed potato juice** was applied as a cooling poultice to burns, scalds, bruises, and inflamed or swollen skin, and to the eyes for puffiness. Purified **potato starch** served as a dusting powder and as a demulcent bulking agent. These are attested folk practices; their efficacy is a separate question, handled below.

### Active Components

**The eaten tuber (flesh, mature, stored dark):** predominantly **starch** — amylose and amylopectin — with modest protein of unusually good amino acid balance, dietary fibre concentrated in the skin, **vitamin C**, **potassium**, vitamin B6, and small amounts of phenolics (chlorogenic acid) and, in coloured cultivars, anthocyanins.

**The toxic fraction — steroidal glycoalkaloids (GAs):** principally **α-solanine** and **α-chaconine**, biosynthesised by the plant as a chemical defence. Their distribution is the entire safety story:

- Concentration is highest in **leaves and stems (tops), sprouts, flowers, berries, and the skin and immediately subsurface layer of the tuber** — lowest in the peeled interior flesh.
- **Light exposure and greening raise glycoalkaloid content.** Chlorophyll (the green) is not itself toxic — it is a *marker* that the tuber has been light-exposed, and glycoalkaloid synthesis rises under the same conditions. Green is a warning flag, not the poison.
- Mechanical damage, sprouting, and prolonged or warm storage also raise levels.

The magnitude of the gradient is documented. According to PubMed, Phillips et al. (1996, *Food and Chemical Toxicology*) measured across seven varieties: **tubers protected from light** contained 0.05–0.65 mg/100 g α-solanine and 0.3–0.63 mg/100 g α-chaconine, while **leaf samples** contained 0.64–22.6 mg/100 g α-solanine and 0.06–55.7 mg/100 g α-chaconine — up to roughly two orders of magnitude higher in the tops.

**Mechanism of toxicity is dual.** According to PubMed (Mensinga et al., 2004, *Regulatory Toxicology and Pharmacology*), glycoalkaloids "may induce gastro-intestinal and systemic effects, by cell membrane disruption and acetylcholinesterase inhibition, respectively." The membrane-lytic action produces the gut symptoms; the cholinesterase inhibition produces the neurological ones.

**Glycoalkaloids are heat-stable at normal cooking temperatures.** Boiling, baking and frying do not reliably destroy them. Peeling removes a large share, because the toxin is concentrated peripherally. Cooking is not a decontamination step; **selection and peeling are.**

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

The potato enters Kenetica as the Codex's clearest instance of a **dose-and-part-partitioned substance**: the single most-eaten tuber on earth, and a true Solanaceae carrying a real toxic fraction in the same organism. Its instructional value is that both facts are simultaneously true, and that safety lies entirely in *which part, in what condition*.

Working uses:

1. **Staple / nutritive base.** As food, not medicine. Whole, sound, peeled or well-scrubbed tubers, cooked.
2. **Cooling topical poultice.** Grated raw tuber or expressed juice on minor burns, scalds and inflamed skin — a **traditional-grade** use retained for its cooling and moisture-holding effect, not for any demonstrated pharmacology.
3. **Demulcent / excipient starch.** Potato starch as a bland dusting powder and as a carrier or bulking base in dry preparations.
4. **Resistant-starch preparation.** The cook-and-chill method (below), which has genuine human trial support for postprandial glycaemic effect.
5. **Signature study — the green flag.** Training the reflex: green skin, sprouts, bitterness, stinging tongue → discard, do not "cut around it" casually.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Preparation | Method | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Culinary tuber | Sound, unblemished, ungreened, unsprouted; store cool and **dark**; peel; cook | Food-grade |
| Cook-and-chill (resistant starch) | Cook, then refrigerate before eating (retrogradation raises resistant starch) | Human trial support — see below |
| Raw poultice | Fresh grated tuber or expressed juice, applied to intact skin, brief application, discard after use | Traditional only |
| Potato starch | Purified starch as dusting powder / dry base | Traditional excipient |
| Green / sprouted tubers | — | **Discard. No preparation renders these reliably safe.** |
| Leaves, stems, sprouts, berries, flowers | — | **Not for internal use in Kenetica.** |

**On the cook-and-chill figures**, the one place a real number is available: according to PubMed, Patterson et al. (2019, *Nutrients*) gave 30 women with elevated fasting glucose 250 g of boiled (low resistant starch) versus baked-then-chilled (high resistant starch) russet potato in a randomised crossover; the chilled potato reduced glucose by 4.8% and 9.2% at 15 and 30 minutes, insulin by 25.8% and 22.6%, and GIP by 41.1% and 37.6%, with lower AUC for insulin and GIP — **but no difference in glucose AUC, GLP-1, peptide YY, or subjective satiety.** State both halves.

**No medicinal dose of potato is given in this Codex.** The only human dosing data that exists is toxicological, not therapeutic.

**Safety threshold — the one sourceable figure.** According to PubMed, Mensinga et al. (2004) describe a total glycoalkaloid concentration of **nearly 200 mg/kg fresh weight** as "the presently recognised upper limit of safety" for potatoes at that time. Treat this as the reference point it is — a widely used advisory ceiling reported in a peer-reviewed toxicology study, **not** as a currently binding statutory limit in any named jurisdiction. **[USER: verify the current regulatory position for your jurisdiction before publishing this figure as law.]**

### Scientific Correlation

Each claim graded.

- **Andean domestication origin — ESTABLISHED.** Supported archaeologically, agronomically, and independently by host-pathogen phylogeography.
- **Tubers contain α-solanine and α-chaconine, concentrated in tops, skin, sprouts and greened tissue — ESTABLISHED, species-specific, quantified.**
- **Glycoalkaloid toxicity mechanism: membrane disruption (GI) plus acetylcholinesterase inhibition (systemic) — ESTABLISHED.**
- **Human poisoning outbreaks and livestock deaths attributable to potato glycoalkaloids — ESTABLISHED as documented (described as "sporadic outbreaks" in the peer-reviewed literature).**
- **Glycoalkaloid clearance exceeds 24 h, raising an accumulation concern with daily consumption — ESTABLISHED in a controlled human study, small sample (2–3 subjects per treatment arm). Treat as a real but preliminary signal.**
- **Cook-and-chill potato lowers early postprandial insulin and GIP — SUPPORTED by human RCT in a specific population (overweight women with elevated fasting glucose, n=30). Do not generalise to all people, and note the null results in the same trial.**
- **"Potato is low glycaemic index" — NOT SUPPORTED as a blanket claim.** Response is highly individual and preparation-dependent; a precision-nutrition model explained only about 32% of the variance in postprandial response. Overclaiming here is unwarranted in both directions.
- **Raw potato poultice for burns and inflammation — TRADITIONAL. Clinical evidence: NULL.** No human trials retrieved. Any perceived benefit is plausibly the cooling/moist-occlusion effect of any cold wet compress, which is not specific to potato.
- **Potato starch as demulcent — TRADITIONAL / excipient. Clinical evidence: NULL.**
- **Glycoalkaloid teratogenicity / pregnancy risk — UNRESOLVED.** Historic hypotheses linking blighted-potato consumption to neural tube defects circulate widely; I retrieved **no** paper this run supporting them, and a review indexed under "Abnormalities, Drug-Induced" indicates the question has been raised without being settled. **[USER: verify] — do not assert either a risk or an all-clear.**
- **BM (Badianus) attestation — UNSUBSTANTIATED, geographically improbable. [USER: verify]**
- **Pre-Columbian Chinese attestation — FALSE by chronology. Do not write it.**

### Traditional Energetic Classification

In post-Columbian Chinese materia medica the tuber is generally read as **sweet in flavour and neutral in thermal nature**, acting on the spleen and stomach, supplementing qi and harmonising the middle burner — a classification applied to a New World crop *after* its arrival, by fitting it into the existing framework. It is a genuine record of Chinese medical thought; it is not evidence of antiquity. **[USER: verify the specific source text and date.]**

The European folk reading is simpler and consistent with the topical use: the **raw** tuber is cool and moist, hence the burn poultice; the **cooked** tuber is warm, heavy, filling and grounding.

Kenetica's own classification: **Nutritive Base — Partitioned.** The defining trait is that nourishment and toxicity occupy different tissues of one organism, and the practitioner's skill is the partition, not the plant.

### Contraindications

- **Never eat green, greening, sprouted, soft, shrivelled or bitter potatoes.** Bitterness and a burning or stinging sensation on the tongue are the practical warning signs of high glycoalkaloid content. Discard rather than trim. Cooking does not reliably destroy glycoalkaloids.
- **Do not eat leaves, stems, sprouts, flowers or berries.** Glycoalkaloid concentrations in the tops run up to roughly a hundredfold above light-protected tuber flesh, and aqueous leaf extracts are cytotoxic and haemolytic in vitro.
- **Poisoning presentation:** nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain and diarrhoea (membrane-disruptive gut effects), and in severe cases headache, confusion, drowsiness, fever, and neurological signs consistent with cholinesterase inhibition. **Suspected significant ingestion is a medical emergency — contact a poisons centre or emergency services. Do not manage at home.**
- **Cumulative exposure.** Glycoalkaloid clearance exceeding 24 hours means daily consumption of high-glycoalkaloid potatoes can accumulate. Store cool, dark, dry; rotate stock; do not use tubers stored long in light.
- **Children and small body mass** are at proportionally greater risk from the same absolute ingested amount.
- **Pregnancy:** questions about glycoalkaloid developmental toxicity have been raised in the literature and are not settled. Kenetica gives no pregnancy clearance and makes no pregnancy claim. Ordinary sound culinary potato is not being flagged here; green and sprouted material should be avoided by everyone and by pregnant women without exception. **[USER: verify current authoritative guidance.]**
- **Nightshade allergy / Solanaceae sensitivity:** avoid in known allergy. Contact dermatitis from handling foliage is reported in agricultural settings.
- **Cholinesterase-inhibiting medication** (including some myasthenia gravis and Alzheimer's therapies) and organophosphate exposure: the shared mechanism makes high-glycoalkaloid exposure a theoretical additive risk. No interaction study retrieved — flagged as precaution, **not** as demonstrated interaction.
- **Livestock:** potato tops, greened cull tubers and sprouts have caused documented animal deaths. Do not feed cull potatoes without inspection.
- **Topical poultice:** apply only to intact skin. **Not** for deep, extensive, blistered or full-thickness burns — those require medical care, and folk remedies delay it. Raw plant material on broken skin is an infection risk.
