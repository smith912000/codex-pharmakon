# Skink — SKINKOS (Dioscorides 2-71), "Schinchi"
## *Scincus officinalis — Skink, in the edition's own gloss; the text calls it "an earth crocodile of its own kind"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides treats the skink in Book Two of *De Materia Medica*, the book that begins with animal products, in the Osbaldeston and Wood 2000 English edition (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). The chapter is **2-71. SKINKOS**, with the heading at line 5220 (the OCR runs it on from the end of the Seps chapter), the edition's gloss "Scincus officinalis — Skink" at line 5221, and the text at line 5222. The index of the edition lists "Skink 206" (line 17584; the Greek-name index has "SKINKOS 206", line 12570). The chapter reads in full:

"Schinchi are found in Egypt, India and some at the Red Sea. Some are found in Libya, others near a river of Mauritania. It is an earth crocodile of its own kind that is preserved in salt with nasturtium [2-185]. They say that a teaspoonful of the part of it that lies around the kidneys (taken in a drink of wine) is a great provocative to lust [aphrodisiac], and yet for all that, a decoction of lentils taken as a drink with honey (or the seed of lettuce taken in a drink with water) represses the intensity of this inclination. It is also mixed with antidotes."

Three things are worth stating about it. First, the sentence on the aphrodisiac is hedged by Dioscorides himself with "They say", and the sentences on where the animal is found and how it is preserved are put as fact. Second, the text answers its own claim: the same chapter gives two things that "repress" the effect, a drink of lentil decoction with honey and a drink of lettuce seed in water. Third, the last sentence says the skink "is also mixed with antidotes", with no antidote named. The chapter before it (2-70, *Seps*) is a different lizard and is poisonous ("Lizard of scincoid genus Seps — POISONOUS", line 5219).

The project's source list (`_sources/dioscorides.csv`, line 252) enters "Skinkos" as "plant", which is wrong for this text: the chapter is in the animal book and describes an animal. The names file (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, locus "2-71 (line 5220)") has the class as animal. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Skinks are lizards, and what the text gives is the animal preserved in salt, with the part "around the kidneys" taken as a drink; that is to say fat and tissue like those of any small reptile, with protein and lipid, and with the salt added by the curing. No aphrodisiac principle is known in lizard tissue, and the text does not claim one. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced use is an aphrodisiac drink of the preserved animal's kidney-region part in wine, and the Codex gives no protocol for it. Dioscorides himself reports the effect at second hand ("They say"), and he gives a counter-drink in the same chapter. The sources retrieved say nothing of safety, and the animal is a wild reptile carrying the hygiene risks set out below.

### Preparation & Dosing

Two preparations are stated. The animal is "preserved in salt with nasturtium [2-185]" (line 5222); the edition's bracket points to the cress of chapter 2-185. For use, "a teaspoonful of the part of it that lies around the kidneys (taken in a drink of wine)" is the stated amount, and it is the only quantity in the chapter. That is the edition's wording for what the Greek measures, and the Greek is not in the file read, so the measure is quoted as printed and not converted. The antagonist drinks carry no quantity: "a decoction of lentils taken as a drink with honey" or "the seed of lettuce taken in a drink with water". [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. No lizard tissue has been shown to be a safe or effective aphrodisiac, and there is no recognised pharmacology of kidney-region reptile fat. Reptiles commonly carry *Salmonella* in the gut, and handling or eating them or their preserved remains can transmit it; salt curing does not remove that risk. Alcohol (the wine of the prescription) itself affects sexual performance, depending on dose. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** None is given. Chapter 2-71 prints no quality (warming, cooling, astringent or the like) for the skink. The only action stated is the effect it is "said" to have, that of a "provocative to lust", together with its reversal by lentils or lettuce seed. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied here. What he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention, and this chapter shows a little of it: it names the places where the animal is found (Egypt, India, the Red Sea, Libya, a river of Mauritania), says how it is cured (in salt with nasturtium) and which part is used, gives a measure, and reports the claim as hearsay ("They say"). He then sets down what puts the effect right again, so that the person taking the drink has a way back. None of that is a cultivation status: the chapter is about a stimulant, not about the person's constitution, and the lentil and lettuce passage is a counter-measure and not a practice. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is an aphrodisiac at second hand from a wild reptile, and the chapter's own advice is to hold a counter-drink ready. If anything is taken from it, it is the structure: a claimed effect, a stated measure, and a way to reverse it, all in one chapter.

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** reptiles are a recognised source of *Salmonella*, and preserved reptile remains of unknown handling should not be eaten or drunk.
- **Alcohol:** the prescription is taken in wine; avoid alcohol with medicines, in pregnancy, and where driving or machinery is involved.
- **Legal and conservation status:** skinks are wild reptiles, and several species are protected or their trade is controlled in some countries; the edition's species name is only its gloss. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** killing a wild animal for a substance with no supported benefit is hard to justify.
