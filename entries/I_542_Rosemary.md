## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Salvia rosmarinus* Spenn. [accepted name since 2017]
**Former Binomial:** *Rosmarinus officinalis* L. [still widespread in literature and commerce]
**Family:** Lamiaceae
**Tribe:** Mentheae

**Synonyms & Historical Names:**
- *Rosmarinus officinalis* L. (historical; most used in phytochemical/medical literature)
- *Salvia rosmarinus* Spenn. (current accepted APG IV taxonomic placement)
- *Rosmarinus laxiflorus* Noë
- *Rosmarinus eriocalyx* Jordan & Fourr.

**Taxonomic Update Note:**
In 2017, phylogenetic analysis by Upson & Andrews established that *Rosmarinus* is nested within *Salvia* (Lamiaceae), making *Rosmarinus* paraphyletic. The genus was formally subsumed into *Salvia* as *S. rosmarinus*. The Codex uses the current accepted name but notes that *R. officinalis* is used in the entire pre-2017 literature including all clinical evidence cited below.

**"Ros Marinus" — Sea Dew:**
The Latin name captures rosemary's primary habitat: rocky coastal cliffs and hillsides bordering the Mediterranean Sea, where morning sea mist ("dew") condenses on the aromatic foliage. This maritime habitat association connected rosemary to the sea and, by extension, to Venus (born from sea foam in Roman mythology) — making rosemary a plant of love, fertility, and memory in the Roman tradition.

**Botanical Identification:**
Woody, perennial evergreen shrub, 50–200 cm; leaves needle-like (1–4 cm), dark green above, white-woolly below, revolute margins; light blue to violet flowers (occasionally white or pink) in axillary clusters; intensely aromatic throughout. Native to the Mediterranean basin; widely cultivated globally as culinary, aromatic, and medicinal herb.

**CM — Ibn Sīnā:**
One of the most significant Ibn Sīnā rosemary records in the Canon: rosemary recommended for strengthening memory, treating weakness of the brain, and improving cognitive function. This is pharmacologically remarkable — a pre-modern clinical observation of cognitive enhancement that maps precisely onto the modern ACh esterase inhibition mechanism of rosmarinic acid and 1,8-cineole. Ibn Sīnā also recommended rosemary wine for cardiac complaints, hepatic weakness, and as a general tonic for the elderly — the "warming and drying in the third degree" Galenic classification.

**EP — Ebers Papyrus:**
Rosemary appears in Egyptian aromatic compound preparations — incense mixtures burned in temples and healing spaces. Whether these were therapeutic aromatherapy (modern concept) or ritual fumigation, the pharmacological effect of aerosolized rosemary compounds on respiratory and cognitive function is consistent.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Degree of Cross-Traditional Agreement:** High (6/9) — strong Old World consensus.

**Core Cross-Traditional Functions:**
1. **Memory / cognitive support** — explicitly documented in CM (Ibn Sīnā), and European herbalism (Culpeper, Banckes's Herball); pharmacologically supported by modern ACh esterase inhibition data
2. **Circulatory stimulant / warming** — universal in confirming traditions; increases peripheral circulation
3. **Hepatic tonic** — liver-strengthening in DMM and CM; choleretic activity in modern phytotherapy
4. **Antimicrobial / fumigation** — EP, AT aromatic compound use; carnosic acid/thymol antimicrobial activity
5. **Hair / scalp stimulant** — documented in European tradition from medieval period; now among the best-supported modern clinical applications (capillary circulation stimulation)

**The Memory Tradition:**
"Rosemary is for remembrance" is among the oldest botanical-cognitive associations in the Western pharmacological tradition. Shakespeare encoded it in *Hamlet* (Ophelia: "There's rosemary, that's for remembrance") and students in ancient Greece reportedly wore rosemary garlands during examinations. The modern discovery that rosemary aroma inhibits acetylcholinesterase (the same enzyme targeted by Alzheimer's drugs donepezil and rivastigmine) is not a new finding — it is the molecular explanation of a 2,000-year-old clinical observation.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Primary Cognitive Mechanism — Cholinesterase Inhibition:**
1,8-Cineole, α-pinene, and rosmarinic acid all demonstrate acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibition in vitro:
- 1,8-Cineole IC₅₀: 0.2-0.5 mM against human AChE (moderate affinity; reversible competitive)
- α-Pinene IC₅₀: 0.3-0.6 mM against human AChE
- Rosmarinic acid: dual AChE and BuChE inhibition; IC₅₀ 0.1-0.3 mM

These are weaker AChE inhibitors than pharmaceutical drugs (donepezil IC₅₀ ~0.006 μM, 10,000× more potent), but at the concentrations achieved by rosemary aromatherapy or oral tea consumption, significant AChE inhibition in brain tissue has been demonstrated in rodent studies. The inhalation route delivers lipophilic compounds (1,8-cineole) directly to olfactory cortex via olfactory nerve, bypassing blood-brain barrier — potentially achieving higher CNS concentrations from aromatherapy than from equivalent oral doses.

**Antioxidant / Neuroprotective (Carnosic Acid):**
Carnosic acid → electrophilic activation by oxidative stress → activated form reacts with reactive oxygen species (ROS) → "electrophilic stress sensing antioxidant." This is an unusual mechanism: carnosic acid is relatively inactive until the brain is under oxidative stress, at which point it is converted to the active radical-scavenger form. This makes it a conditionally-activated neuroprotector — more active when needed. Nrf2 pathway activation → HO-1 and GPx upregulation → sustained antioxidant defense.

**Circulatory Mechanism:**
Camphor + 1,8-cineole → sympathomimetic peripheral vasodilation → increased cutaneous and scalp microcirculation. This is the mechanism for both the traditional circulatory tonic use and the hair-growth application. External application to scalp increases dermal papilla blood supply → increased oxygen and nutrient delivery to follicle bulb.

**Anti-inflammatory:**
- Carnosol → NF-κB p65 inhibition (via IKKβ phosphorylation inhibition)
- Rosmarinic acid → 5-LOX and COX-2 dual inhibition → reduced eicosanoid production
- Ursolic acid → AP-1 transcription factor inhibition → reduced IL-6, TNF-α

**Antitumor (In Vitro):**
Carnosol and carnosic acid demonstrate selective cytotoxicity against cancer cell lines (breast MCF-7, prostate LNCaP, colon HT-29) with limited normal-cell toxicity. Mechanism: Nrf2 activation in normal cells (protective) vs. Nrf2-suppression + ROS induction in cancer cells (cytotoxic). This differential response is a pharmacologically interesting pattern suggesting selective targeting, but has not been translated to clinical trials.

**Antimicrobial:**
Carnosic acid + 1,8-cineole: synergistic activity against *S. aureus*, MRSA, *E. coli*; carnosic acid particularly active against gram-positive organisms (membrane disruption similar to thymol mechanism). MIC values: *S. aureus* 0.1–0.5 mg/mL carnosic acid; similar range for whole EO.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Overall Evidence Level: B/A** — strong mechanistic and observational; emerging RCT data.

**Level A — Hair Growth RCT:**
- Panahi Y et al. (2015, *Skinmed*): Rosemary oil (2% in jojoba carrier) vs. 2% minoxidil (current standard treatment) in androgenetic alopecia; n=100; 6 months; primary endpoint: hair count in defined scalp area. Rosemary oil = minoxidil (non-inferior): both groups +87-89 hairs in target area; scalp itch significantly lower in rosemary group. Level A — head-to-head vs. active standard; RCT; sufficient power. This is one of the most pharmacologically significant modern rosemary RCTs.

**Level A — Cognitive Function:**
- Moss M et al. (2012, *Therapeutic Advances in Psychopharmacology*): 1,8-cineole serum levels correlated with cognitive performance (speed and accuracy) in 20 volunteers performing cognitive tasks in rooms diffused with rosemary aromatherapy vs. lavender control; significant correlation r=0.58 (p<0.01). Not a pure placebo-controlled RCT but mechanistically confirming (serum cineole as objective pharmacokinetic marker).
- Ballard CG et al. (2002, *Journal of Clinical Psychiatry*): Aromatherapy using rosemary in dementia patients; neuropsychiatric symptoms measured; mixed results but cognitive subscales showed improvement with rosemary and lavender combined.

**Level B — Cognitive:**
- Multiple crossover studies of rosemary aromatherapy on cognitive tasks (mood, memory speed, accuracy): consistently positive effects vs. no-aroma or lavender control; 1,8-cineole as pharmacokinetic marker correlates with cognitive measures in several studies

**Level B — Antioxidant (Food Safety application):**
- Extensive food science literature demonstrates rosemary extract (standardized for carnosic acid/rosmarinic acid) effectively prevents oxidative rancidity in meat products, oils, and fats — equivalent to or superior to BHA/BHT synthetic antioxidants. This has driven EU approval of rosemary extracts as food antioxidant (E392).

**Level B — Anti-inflammatory:**
- Khanna D et al. (2007): Rosemary extract reduced inflammatory markers in TNF-α-induced human endothelial cell model; carnosol identified as primary active component; relevant to cardiovascular anti-inflammatory applications.

**Regulatory Status:**
- **Commission E:** APPROVED for internal use: dyspepsia; topical: supportive therapy in rheumatic conditions and circulatory problems (1990 monograph)
- **ESCOP:** Monograph positive for dyspepsia (internal) and circulatory/rheumatic (topical)
- **EU:** Rosemary extract (E392) approved as food antioxidant
- **EMA/HMPC:** Community herbal monograph for rosemary leaf established

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**"Rosemary Is for Remembrance":**
The cognitive tradition around rosemary spans Greek student examinations (rosemary garlands worn during study and testing), Roman wedding ceremonies (rosemary in the bride's crown as a symbol of love and fidelity — and memory of the marriage vow), medieval funeral rites (rosemary at gravesides as a symbol of remembrance of the deceased), and Elizabethan theatrical encoding in Shakespeare's *Hamlet* (Act IV, Scene 5: Ophelia distributes herbs symbolically; "There's rosemary, that's for remembrance; pray, love, remember"). Each use encodes the same pharmacological claim: rosemary strengthens memory. The 2015 Panahi RCT on hair growth and the 2012 Moss study on cognitive function from rosemary aromatherapy both provide mechanistic validation for a tradition articulated continuously for over 2,000 years.

**The Sea-Dew Name:**
*Ros marinus* (dew of the sea) connects rosemary pharmacologically to its habitat. Rocky Mediterranean coastal cliffs, sea spray, intense sunshine, and thin limestone soils produce rosemary with exceptionally high carnosic acid and EO content — the plant's antioxidant and aromatic chemical defenses are maximal under these stressful conditions (high UV, drought, heat). Mediterranean wild rosemary is pharmacologically more potent than cultivated commercial rosemary from temperate gardens. This habitat-potency relationship is a general principle in aromatic Mediterranean herbs (also thyme, sage, lavender) — stress drives secondary metabolite production.

**Remembrance at Funerals:**
The dual use of rosemary for weddings (remember the vow) and funerals (remember the dead) created an enduring botanical paradox: the same plant for love and grief. This was not contradiction but pharmacological sophistication — a plant that objectively strengthens memory function is appropriate at any ceremony whose central act is encoding durable memory. The Victorians carried sprigs of rosemary at both weddings and funerals, a custom documented as "old custom" in 19th-century ethnographic records, suggesting medieval or earlier origin.

**Rosemary in Perfumery — Hungary Water:**
The first documented perfume in European history was "Hungary Water" (Eau de la Reine de Hongrie), recorded from 14th-century Hungary — reportedly given to Queen Elizabeth of Hungary circa 1370 CE. The formula: rosemary tops distilled in aqueous alcohol. This represents the first distillation-based fragrance product in Western history, predating Cologne water by 300 years. Whether the Queen's alleged rejuvenation from using it (she reportedly attracted marriage proposals from European royalty in her 70s) reflects pharmacological anti-aging effects of carnosic acid or is hagiographic embellishment is not determinable, but Hungary Water's historical position as the first perfume is secured.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Indications (Commission E / ESCOP):**
- **Internal:** Dyspepsia, biliary dysfunction, supportive in hypotension (as cardiac/circulatory stimulant)
- **Topical (massage):** Rheumatic conditions; peripheral circulatory disorders; muscle tension
- **Topical (scalp):** Androgenetic alopecia — Level A evidence; non-inferior to minoxidil 2%
- **Aromatherapy:** Cognitive support, memory; Level B evidence; mechanistically supported

**Traditional Additional Uses (Level C):**
- Hepatic tonic
- Menstrual irregularity (emmenagogue — use with caution; limited to non-pregnant adults)
- Headache (topical application to temples; circulatory mechanism)
- Oral hygiene

**Preparation Methods:**
| Form | Preparation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Infusion | 2 g dried leaf / 150 mL boiling water; steep 10 min (covered) | Dyspepsia; systemic use |
| Tincture | 1:5 in 45% ethanol; 2-4 mL three times daily | — |
| Essential oil (topical) | 2-4% in carrier oil (jojoba for scalp; arnica oil for muscle) | Scalp: Panahi 2015 protocol = 2% in jojoba |
| Aromatherapy | 2-4 drops in diffuser; 1 drop on tissue to inhale | Cognitive use; Moss 2012 protocol |
| Bath additive | 50 g herb in muslin bag; add to bath; or 5-10 drops EO | Circulatory/rheumatic |

**Dosing:**
- Internal: 4-6 g dried herb per day (infusion); 2-4 mL tincture (1:5) three times daily
- EO topical: 2-4% dilution in carrier; apply to scalp daily for hair growth
- Aromatherapy: 2-6 drops in 100 mL water in diffuser; 30-60 min sessions

**Contraindications:**
- **Pregnancy: CONTRAINDICATED** at therapeutic doses — emmenagogue; historical abortifacient use; culinary use safe; therapeutic extracts avoid
- Epilepsy: camphor content; avoid high-dose EO internally; camphor lowers seizure threshold
- Hypertension: circulatory stimulant may transiently increase blood pressure; monitor in hypertensive patients at high doses
- Allergy to Lamiaceae

**Drug Interactions:**
- Anticoagulants: rosmarinic acid → mild antiplatelet; monitor with warfarin/novel anticoagulants
- Antihypertensives: circulatory stimulant effect may partially antagonize; caution
- ACh esterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine): additive AChE inhibition — potentially beneficial but monitor for cholinergic excess
- Iron supplements: rosmarinic acid chelates iron; separate by 2+ hours

**Quality Assurance:**
- PhEur EO specification: 1,8-cineole ≥15%, camphor ≤30%, α-pinene ≥7%
- Carnosic acid: should be ≥1.0% in dried leaf for antioxidant/neuroprotective applications
- Adulteration risk: *Salvia* spp. and other aromatic Lamiaceae may be substituted; HPLC-DAD or GC-MS EO profiling for authentication
- Geographic variation: Mediterranean wild-harvested > temperate commercial; specify origin for high-value applications
