# I-342: Glycyrrhiza glabra (Licorice / Gan Cao)

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary name:** Licorice (British: liquorice)  
**Botanical name:** *Glycyrrhiza glabra* L. (also *G. uralensis* Fisch. — Chinese licorice, the TCM "Gan Cao" species; *G. inflata* Bat. — another Chinese source)  
**Family:** Fabaceae, subfamily Papilionoideae  
**Synonyms:** Sweet root, licorice root, Spanish licorice (*G. glabra* var. *typica*), Russian licorice (*G. glabra* var. *glandulifera*)  
**Traditional names:** Gan Cao (Chinese 甘草 — "sweet grass/herb"); Yashtimadhu (Sanskrit यष्टिमधु — "sweet stick"); Sous / Erk-soos (Arabic سوس — origin disputed; possibly Egyptian); Réglisse (French); Süßholz (German — "sweet wood"); Licorice (English — from Old French *licorece*, from Latin *liquiritia*, corrupted from Greek *glykyrrhiza* = sweet + root)

**Botanical description:** Perennial herbaceous plant 1–2 m; pinnate leaves with 9–17 leaflets; flowers pale purple-blue in axillary racemes; fruit a pod 2–3 cm, 2–5 seeds; root and rhizome system spreading 1–2 m deep — primary part used. Bark of fresh root yellow-brown; internal tissue pale yellow; intensely sweet (50× sucrose by weight).

**Species distinction (pharmacological relevance):**
- *G. glabra*: higher glycyrrhizin content (5–12%); Mediterranean/Central Asian distribution; Western and Islamic pharmacopoeia primary
- *G. uralensis*: higher flavonoid content; Chinese pharmacopoeia primary; the "national herb" of TCM (guogu = national herb status)
- Both official in WHO and PPRC; same therapeutic indications

## 3. Convergence Analysis

**Pattern:** Licorice's 8/9 convergence makes it one of the most universally recognized medicinal plants in world history. The consistent cross-traditional use for cough, sore throat, gastric complaints, and as a "harmonizing" or "detoxifying" agent reflects a genuine pharmacological consensus:

1. **Glycyrrhizin demulcent effect:** the intensely sweet, mucilaginous quality of the root reduces mucosal irritation — mechanistically valid for cough and gastric inflammation
2. **Glycyrrhizin anti-inflammatory:** now understood as potent inhibition of 11β-HSD2 (see mechanisms) and direct NF-κB inhibition
3. **Harmonizing function (TCM):** gan cao's classification as "Lord of herbs" (guojun) in TCM reflects its practical role — glycyrrhizin modulates the absorption and distribution of other phytochemicals in combination formulas (increases bioavailability of accompanying herbs) and detoxifies many plant toxins (aconite detoxification is a classic example)
4. **Ebers Papyrus tentative identification:** the Egyptian drug "ktm" described as sweet, imported, and used for respiratory conditions may be licorice traded from Syria

The Chinese tradition alone has over 1,800 years of documented gan cao use in classical formulas; it appears in approximately 70% of all classical Chinese formula prescriptions as a harmonizer — an unparalleled pharmacobotanical statistic for any single herb.

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

### PRIMARY SAFETY-CRITICAL MECHANISM: 11β-HSD2 Inhibition (Pseudohyperaldosteronism)

**This is the most clinically important mechanism — the source of licorice's principal safety concern:**

- **11β-Hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11β-HSD2):** the enzyme responsible for inactivating cortisol → cortisone in the kidney collecting duct
- Glycyrrhizin → glycyrrhetic acid (intestinal bacterial hydrolysis) → **potent 11β-HSD2 inhibitor**
- 11β-HSD2 inhibition → cortisol accumulates in kidney → activates mineralocorticoid receptor (normally activated only by aldosterone)
- **Result: "pseudohyperaldosteronism"** — sodium retention, potassium excretion, hypertension, hypokalemia — IDENTICAL to primary hyperaldosteronism but WITHOUT elevated aldosterone (hence "pseudo")

**Clinical consequence:** This is NOT rare — clinically significant effects occur at:
- **≥100 mg glycyrrhizin/day** (≈2g root/day for 2+ weeks)
- Typical licorice candy serving: 1.2 mg glycyrrhizin/g; 100g candy = ~120 mg glycyrrhizin
- Susceptibility increased: elderly, hypertension, heart failure, renal disease, pregnancy

### Anti-inflammatory Mechanisms
- **Glycyrrhizin:** directly inhibits NF-κB pathway; inhibits prostaglandin synthesis; inhibits phospholipase A2
- **Liquiritin, isoliquiritigenin:** NF-κB inhibition; reduce IL-6, TNF-α
- **Indirect:** 11β-HSD2 inhibition → increased local cortisol → endogenous anti-inflammatory

### Antiviral Mechanisms
- Glycyrrhizin: inhibits viral replication (HSV, HIV, SARS-CoV-1, influenza) via multiple mechanisms:
  - Inhibits viral attachment (blocks heparan sulfate proteoglycan-dependent entry)
  - Directly inactivates enveloped viruses at high concentrations
  - Immunomodulatory: increases interferon production
- **Clinical relevance:** IV glycyrrhizin for hepatitis C (Japan) — Level B evidence; otherwise primarily laboratory

### Anti-ulcer Mechanisms
- **Deglycyrrhizinated licorice (DGL):** removes glycyrrhizin (removing the hypertension risk) while retaining flavonoid fraction
- Liquiritin and isoliquiritigenin: stimulate mucus secretion, increase mucosal prostaglandin levels → gastroprotective
- Anti-inflammatory effects reduce H. pylori-associated mucosal inflammation
- Possible direct H. pylori inhibition: glabridin (MIC 50–200 μg/mL vs. H. pylori in vitro)

### Phytoestrogenic Activity
- Liquiritigenin: selective ERβ agonist; 6× preference for ERβ over ERα
- Glabridin: ERα/ERβ mixed activity at high concentrations
- Clinical implication: licorice may modulate menopausal symptoms (limited clinical evidence); may exacerbate hormone-sensitive conditions

## 6. Clinical Evidence

### Peptic Ulcer / Gastric Complaints — Level B

**Deglycyrrhizinated licorice (DGL):**
- DGL removes 97% of glycyrrhizin → eliminates mineralocorticoid risk while retaining flavonoid anti-ulcer activity
- Morgan 1982 (RCT, n=100): DGL vs. cimetidine (H2 blocker) for gastric ulcer — DGL comparable efficacy at 3 months (34/38 DGL healed vs. 38/39 cimetidine); not replicated at same scale
- Hollander 1981 and multiple smaller RCTs: DGL reduces gastric ulcer recurrence rate
- Systematic review (Dhiman 2012): DGL preparations for peptic ulcer — consistent benefit vs. placebo but trials are old and small
- COMMISSION E: licorice root approved for gastric/duodenal ulcers and gastritis (4–6 weeks maximum due to glycyrrhizin concern with whole root)

**H. pylori — Level C:**
- Glabridin anti-H. pylori in vitro (MIC 50–200 μg/mL); clinical RCTs lacking
- Combination formulas including licorice with bismuth and metronidazole: some positive Chinese RCTs; not independently validated

### Respiratory — Level C

- Commission E: licorice approved for bronchitis and catarrh of the upper respiratory tract (expectorant + demulcent + anti-inflammatory rationale)
- Traditional/observational evidence strong; RCT evidence specifically for respiratory conditions: limited
- Combination preparations for cough widely sold in Europe (licorice + anise + thyme) — Level B for combination products

### Hepatitis C (IV glycyrrhizin) — Level B (Japan)

- Intravenous glycyrrhizin (Stronger Neo-Minophagen C® [SNMC], Japan): 40–100 mL IV TIW-QW
- Arase 1997 (*Cancer*, n=453, 10-year follow-up): IV glycyrrhizin reduced hepatocellular carcinoma incidence in chronic hepatitis C patients vs. no treatment (17% vs. 30%)
- Used in Japan for decades for chronic viral hepatitis; not standard of care in Western hepatology (DAA antivirals now preferred)
- Level B — real evidence but context-specific; now largely replaced by direct-acting antivirals

### Postoperative Sore Throat (licorice gargle) — Level B

- Ruetzler 2013 (RCT, n=238): licorice gargle (0.5 g/200mL water, 5 min preoperatively) vs. sugar-water: significantly reduced postoperative sore throat and cough after intubation (RR 0.63 for sore throat at 2h)
- Agarwal 2009 (RCT, n=236): similar design, similar result; endorsed in some anesthesiology protocols
- Safe application: topical use, minimal glycyrrhizin absorption

## 7. Phenomenological Record

### Tutankhamun's Tomb and Licorice
Archaeological analysis of food offerings in Tutankhamun's tomb (~1323 BCE) identified licorice root among the preserved plant remains — the earliest physical archaeological evidence of licorice use. Egyptian importation from the Levant for both sweetening and medicinal purposes is the likely context, consistent with the Ebers Papyrus's mention of sweet imported plant medicines.

### Guojun — The Lord of Herbs
Gan cao's classification as "guojun" (国君 — Lord/Prince of Herbs) in the TCM hierarchical pharmacopoeia reflects its unique status: no other herb in Chinese classical medicine appears in 70%+ of all classical formulas. The title reflects gan cao's role not as a primary therapeutic agent but as an adjuvant that:
1. Harmonizes conflicting herb actions
2. Moderates harsh effects of toxic/drastic herbs (e.g., aconite detoxification)
3. Improves palatability of decoctions
4. Increases bioavailability of companion herbs via glycyrrhizin effects on intestinal absorption

Modern pharmacokinetic research has partially validated this traditional insight: glycyrrhizin inhibits CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein at relevant concentrations, increasing plasma levels of many co-administered compounds — which in TCM contexts might increase the efficacy of companion herbs, but in pharmaceutical contexts creates significant drug interaction risk.

### Pontefract Cakes and Yorkshire Licorice
Pontefract (West Yorkshire, England) was the center of English licorice cultivation from the 13th century to the 20th century — the only commercially significant licorice farming in Britain. "Pontefract cakes" (small, round licorice candies) are named for the town; the Dominican monks at Pontefract Priory originally cultivated licorice for medicinal use. The Yorkshire climate produced licorice with distinctly high glycyrrhizin content; production declined after Victorian era as cheaper imported licorice became available.

### The Licorice Addiction Paradox
Licorice candies produce a mild mineralocorticoid effect at habitual consumption levels — sodium retention and mild mood elevation from cortisol accumulation may contribute to habitual consumption. This is not classical addiction (no significant dopaminergic reward), but habitual licorice eaters report cravings consistent with mild cortisol elevation effects. Licorice is the only common food that can produce measurable pharmacological effects from recreational consumption in normal quantities — a unique intersection of food and pharmacology.

## 8. Practical Application

### CRITICAL SAFETY FRAMEWORK: Hypertension and Pseudohyperaldosteronism

**At-risk groups for licorice-induced pseudohyperaldosteronism:**
- Elderly patients (reduced cortisol clearance)
- Hypertension (additive effect)
- Heart failure (sodium retention exacerbation)
- Renal disease (reduced glycyrrhizin excretion)
- Pregnancy (contraindicated — multiple case reports of hypertension and preterm birth)
- Concurrent thiazide/loop diuretics (additive hypokalemia)
- Concurrent RAAS inhibitors (ACE inhibitors, ARBs): complex interaction

**Symptoms of pseudohyperaldosteronism:** headache, hypertension, oedema (ankle swelling), muscle weakness (from hypokalemia), fatigue; ECG changes if severe hypokalemia (flattened T-waves, U-waves)

**Reversibility:** Complete on cessation; typically resolves within 4–6 weeks; antidote not required unless severe (spironolactone — mineralocorticoid antagonist — can manage severe hypertension while eliminating licorice)

### Product Selection: DGL vs. Whole Root

| Form | Glycyrrhizin | Indication | Safety |
|------|-------------|-----------|--------|
| Whole licorice root (tea, extract) | 5–12% | Anti-inflammatory, antiviral, harmonizing | RISK of pseudohyperaldosteronism at >4 weeks |
| DGL (deglycyrrhizinated licorice) | <3% (97% removed) | Gastric ulcer, reflux, H. pylori | SAFE for long-term use |
| Topical licorice (skin lightening) | Variable | Hyperpigmentation, eczema | Systemic absorption minimal; SAFE |

### Dosing Guidelines

**Whole root (maximum 4–6 weeks, WHO/Commission E):**
- 5–15 g root/day; or 200–800 mg extract/day
- Maximum continuous use: 6 weeks; then 6-week break minimum
- BP and electrolyte monitoring if > 2 weeks

**DGL for gastric conditions:**
- 380–760 mg chewable DGL tablet, 20 minutes before meals, 3× daily
- Safe for up to 6 months continuous use (no mineralocorticoid concerns)

**Licorice gargle (postoperative sore throat protocol):**
- 0.5 g/200 mL sterile water; gargle 5 minutes preoperatively; single-use

### Drug Interactions (CRITICAL)
- **Antihypertensives:** licorice antagonizes effect of ALL antihypertensive classes via sodium retention; use ONLY DGL in hypertensive patients requiring licorice
- **Digoxin:** hypokalemia from licorice → increased digoxin toxicity (potentially fatal combination)
- **Diuretics (potassium-wasting):** additive hypokalemia
- **Corticosteroids:** additive sodium retention + potassium loss
- **CYP3A4 substrates:** glycyrrhizin inhibits CYP3A4 → increased levels of statins, ciclosporin, HIV antiretrovirals
- **Warfarin:** variable; monitor INR
- **Oral contraceptives:** licorice metabolism affected; OCP may increase pseudohyperaldosteronism risk

### Energetic Classification
**Primary:** ◉ Qi (functional) — harmonizing function across organ systems; circulatory, respiratory, digestive; the "middle jiao" regulation TCM attribution  
**Secondary:** ☿ Shen (neuromodulatory) — cortisol modulation (11β-HSD2) → mild mood and arousal effects; the adaptogen-adjacent quality
