# Seawater — Udor Thalassion (as printed in the 2000 English edition of Dioscorides)
## *Water from the sea; the translator's gloss under the heading 5-19 UDOR THALASSION is the single word "Seawater"* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The loci given for this entry are `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` ("5-19 (line 9863)", gloss "Seawater", class "other") and `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 748 ("Udor Thalassion", class mineral, DMM V, identification marked yes). The chapter is *De Materia Medica* 5-19 *Udor Thalassion* in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading at line 9863, gloss at line 9864, text at line 9865. Book 5 holds both the wines and the stones, and seawater sits among the wines: it follows 5-17 *Melikraton* (honey water, line 9855) and 5-18 *Udor* (fresh water in general, line 9860), and precedes 5-20 *Thalassomeli* (seawater with honey, line 9870) and 5-21 *Oxus* (vinegar). It is therefore not a stone chapter, and the spine's "mineral" class is a classification made after the fact.

The chapter reads (line 9865): "Water from the sea is hot, sharp, and bad for the stomach. It troubles the bowels, expelling phlegm. Applied with hot cloths when it is warm it extracts and dissolves. It is good for disorders of the nerves, and chilblains before ulceration. It is conveniently mixed with poultices of barley meal, and it is suitable for plasters and solutions for warm compresses. It is given lukewarm as a suppository for evacuations, and warm for griping. It is a wash for parasitic skin diseases, itches, lichen [skin disease with red pustules], nits, and breasts swollen with milk. Applied with hot cloths it dissolves all bruises, and is good for bites from poisonous creatures that cause trembling and quaking chills — especially from scorpions, harvest spiders and snakes. Moreover, it is good if anyone goes into a hot bath of it; such a bath is helpful for long-lasting disorders of the body and the strength. Inhaled as steam it eases dropsy, headaches, and hardness of hearing. Seawater put in jars alone and not mixed with drinkable water becomes unpoisonous, but some boil it beforehand and then put it in jars. It is given as a purge by itself (or with posca [hot drinks], wine or honey), and after the purging they give the broth of a hen or fish to alleviate the sharpness of the pangs of hunger." The words in square brackets are the translator's.

So the text does five things with seawater. It states qualities (hot, sharp, bad for the stomach, troubling the bowels, expelling phlegm). It gives external uses: hot cloths and plasters, poultices with barley meal, warm compresses, a wash for skin diseases, nits and swollen breasts, a hot bath "for long-lasting disorders of the body and the strength", and steam. It gives internal uses: a suppository, and a purge taken alone or with posca, wine or honey, followed by hen or fish broth "to alleviate the sharpness of the pangs of hunger". It gives a storage rule: seawater kept in jars, not mixed with drinkable water, "becomes unpoisonous", and some boil it first. And it names bites and stings as an indication, with scorpions, harvest spiders and snakes. The chapter gives no place of collection, no season, no depth, no test of the water, and no quantity for the purge.

Related chapters that use seawater are in the same text. 5-20 *Thalassomeli* (line 9870) is made "from the same amount each of honey, rainwater and seawater, strained, and placed in the sun in a pitch-glazed jar in the burning heat of the hottest days", or from two parts of boiled seawater and one of honey, and "is thought to purge very well"; it is "more moderate and milder than seawater alone" in the second form (lines 9871-9872). 5-27 *Oinos Thalassikos* (line 9910) is wine "made with seawater in various ways": seawater added after the grapes are gathered, or to trodden grapes after sunning, or to raisins steeped in casks; the harder kind is "good against the signs of approaching acute fevers (if there is nothing better at hand)" but "bad for the stomach and cause[s] wind" (lines 9911-9912). In the chapters on fats and resins, seawater is a working fluid: ox or cow suet "must be washed in sea water taken out of the ocean" and boiled in it (2-89, line 5311), and the resin scraped off ships is called *apochyma* "as it is soluble because it was steeped in sea water" (1-98, line 4277). These are uses of the water, not further doctrine about it.

Other traditions are not tagged for this entry. Two further loci turned up on search and are noted, not applied: the Explanatory Tantra lists "sea water" in its sequence of types of water in which the earlier are superior and the later inferior (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3071; Clark 1995, line 715, "sea and forest water"), and both texts count salted water, or water "contaminated by salt", among the waters that increase the humours (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3072, Clark line 715). The Ebers (Bryan), Assyrian (Thompson) and Shennong (Yang) texts returned nothing for a search on "sea water" and "water of the sea"; Sushruta was not searched for it.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, seawater is water holding dissolved salts, mainly sodium chloride, with magnesium, sulphate, calcium and potassium salts, and trace elements; it also carries living microorganisms and, in some places, pollutants. The Greek author did not know this composition and the text does not state it. The "unpoisonous" of the storage rule cannot be tied to any chemical change that the sources describe. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say that Dioscorides used seawater warm on the skin, as a bath, as a suppository and as a purge, and kept it in jars. The Codex does not recommend drinking seawater: it is a dehydrating purge in a body that already needs fluid, and a seawater suppository or untreated collected water is a hygiene risk. A warm bath or compress of salt water is the one use the sources would most readily support, but the text is not a protocol and gives no strength, time or temperature, and no dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations in the text are: the warm water on hot cloths or in plasters with barley meal (line 9865); the bath, the steam and the wash (same line); the suppository, lukewarm "for evacuations" and warm "for griping"; the purge, alone or with posca, wine or honey, followed by hen or fish broth; and storage in jars, alone, unmixed with drinkable water, sometimes after boiling. The related preparations are *thalassomeli* (5-20) and wine with seawater (5-27). The lines give no quantity of seawater for the purge, no volume of the suppository, no length of the bath and no number of days. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Seawater is about 3.5 per cent dissolved salt by weight; drinking it draws water out of the body and worsens dehydration, so it is not a safe drink, and in quantity it acts as a purge by osmosis in the gut. Salt-water washes and baths are a familiar folk practice for skin and for the body, and an irritant for open wounds in some people. Seawater can carry bacteria such as *Vibrio* species and runoff pollutants, which make a collected sample unsafe for the gut and for broken skin. There is no evidence that it neutralises venom. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** the chapter prints qualities in its first sentence (line 9865): seawater "is hot, sharp, and bad for the stomach", and it "troubles the bowels, expelling phlegm". It prints no degree and no wet/dry rating. Of the related preparations, *thalassomeli* is said to be "more moderate and milder than seawater alone" in the boiled form (line 9872). No other tradition is tagged, so no other classification is offered.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for seawater. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, season, assay, adulteration), and this chapter gives only a storage rule and a recipe for what to eat after the purge. It records one use that is not strictly medicine: the hot bath "helpful for long-lasting disorders of the body and the strength". That is a therapeutic statement about weakness, not a cultivation status, and the text does not call the bath or the water a source of vitality. It records no amuletic use.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator beyond what any bather already knows: a warm sea or salt-water bath, if the water is clean and the skin intact, is the use closest to what the chapter describes. The chapter's purge and suppository are not a regimen to repeat, and the sources retrieved give no dose or duration for them. The useful habit in the text is its care about how the water is kept and what follows a purge (broth for the hunger it leaves). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Drinking seawater:** dehydrates; contraindicated in kidney disease, heart disease, high blood pressure, pregnancy, in children and in anyone already short of fluid.
- **Purging:** a forced purge can cause dehydration and salt imbalance; do not repeat the text's purge.
- **Suppository or enema of collected water:** unsafe; collected seawater is not sterile.
- **Broken or ulcerated skin:** salt water stings and can infect; the text itself limits its chilblain use to "before ulceration" (line 9865).
- **Bites and stings:** the text names scorpions, spiders and snakes; a bite from a venomous animal needs emergency medical care and is not treated by a compress.
- **Collected water:** pollution and microbes vary by shore; this entry does not cover how to collect it. [USER: verify]
