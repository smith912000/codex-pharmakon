# Henbane — بنج (Banj) / Ὑοσκύαμος (Hyoscyamos) / धत्तूर सहोदर (Dhattura Sahodara)
## *Hyoscyamus niger L. — Black Henbane*
**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM, EP, SA, SBJ, SS, FMT
**Energetic Classification (TCM):** ☿ Shen (extreme sedative tier)
**Training Phase:** `[SPECIALIST ONLY]`

---

> ⚠️ **CRITICAL SAFETY NOTICE:** Henbane (*Hyoscyamus niger*) contains tropane alkaloids (hyoscine/scopolamine, hyoscyamine, atropine) that are toxic at doses only modestly above therapeutic levels. The therapeutic window is narrow and individual variation in sensitivity is high. **This entry is for pharmacological and historical reference only.** Internal use requires qualified medical supervision. Overdose produces anticholinergic toxidrome and can be fatal. Do not self-administer.

---

### Origin & Tradition

Henbane holds a singular position in the history of medicine: it was one of the primary agents of surgical anesthesia for over two thousand years before the discovery of ether (1846). Across six major medical traditions — Assyrian, Egyptian, Greek, Islamic, Ayurvedic, and Mesoamerican — independent encounter with the Solanaceae family produced recognition of the same fundamental pharmacological properties: narcosis, pain relief, and the capacity to render patients insensible to surgical intervention.

**AT — Assyrian (Šimmatu / Anzahal identification debated):** Henbane or a closely related *Hyoscyamus* species appears in Thompson's 1924 *Assyrian Herbal* among the narcotic plant preparations used for pain management and sedation. Assyrian physicians combined narcotic plants with ritual incantation for procedures requiring patient immobilization. The compound narcotic-ritual preparation was designed to operate simultaneously on the physical (Asu) and the spirit-level (Ashipu) dimensions of pain.

**EP — Egyptian (referenced among sedative preparations):** The Ebers Papyrus references aromatic soporific preparations containing Solanaceae-type plants. Egyptian medical practice developed sophisticated sedative protocols for surgical intervention and for managing severe pain states. Henbane's presence in Egyptian materia medica is documented in modern Egyptological pharmacobotany, though specific hieroglyphic identification remains debated.

**DMM — De Materia Medica (Ὑοσκύαμος — Hyoscyamos):** Dioscorides provides one of the most complete and clinically accurate ancient accounts of henbane in Book IV, Chapter 69. He distinguishes three varieties by flower color (white, yellow, black) and notes that black henbane is the most powerful. His description is precise: "The juice of the leaves, taken as a drink — the amount of a drachm — causes madness and deep sleep. Its seed also, if eaten, confuses the mind. Its leaves, applied as a poultice, alleviate pain in the hypochondrium and reduce swellings." Dioscorides explicitly acknowledges the antidote: "the antidote for those who have taken it is raw cabbage, with oil and vinegar, or warm wine."

**CM — Canon of Medicine (بنج — Banj):** Ibn Sina devotes extensive attention to henbane in Book II and discusses it as a surgical adjunct in Book V (on compound preparations). He classifies it as cold in the fourth degree — the maximum in his graduated system — acknowledging its capacity to produce complete narcosis. His observations on the mechanism are remarkably accurate: "It extinguishes the vital heat from the affected part and prevents the spirit from arriving there, which causes numbness and loss of sensation." Ibn Sina describes the soporific sponge preparation (*al-isfanj al-murakkhī*): a sponge soaked in a decoction of henbane, opium, mandrake, hemlock, and black nightshade, brought to the nose of the surgical patient until insensibility is achieved. The antidote procedure — warming, vinegar, stimulant aromatics — maps directly to the modern management of anticholinergic poisoning.

**SS — Ayurveda (Parasika Yavani — पारसीक यवानी):** The Sanskrit name "Parasika Yavani" translates as "the barley of Persia/Parsa" — explicitly acknowledging its western origin; Ayurvedic texts recognized henbane as an imported drug, not a native Indian plant. It is classified as Vishaghnā (antivenomous), Vedanasthāpana (analgesic), and used cautiously in compound formulas for extreme pain states. The Chakradatta and Sharngadhara Samhita describe its use in preparations for severe neurological pain.

**SBJ — Bencao Gangmu (天仙子 — Tiānxiānzǐ, "heavenly immortal seeds"):** Li Shizhen documents henbane seeds as a specialist analgesic-sedative — bitter, acrid, warm, **very toxic (大毒, *dà dú*)**. Applications: stops pain; seed powder packed into dental caries (the dental-analgesic application paralleling Ibn Sina's *banj* fumigation); antispasmodic; sedation. The explicit 大毒 classification marks it as a measured-dispensing specialist herb.

**BM — Badianus Manuscript (Solanaceae relatives):** The Aztec herbal documents several New World Solanaceae species (*Datura stramonium*, *D. meteloides*, and related taxa) with essentially identical pharmacological applications to Old World henbane. The convergent pharmaceutical use of Solanaceae tropane alkaloids across unrelated cultures for pain management, narcosis, and ritual altered states represents one of the most striking examples of independent pharmacological convergence in medical history.

### Active Components

All pharmacological activity derives from the **tropane alkaloid** family — among the most precisely characterized plant alkaloids in pharmacology:

- **Hyoscine (Scopolamine) — (1R,2R,4S,5S,7s)-tropan-3α-yl (2S)-3-hydroxy-2-phenylpropanoate:**
  The primary alkaloid in *H. niger* leaves and seeds. Competitive antagonist at all muscarinic acetylcholine receptor subtypes (M1–M5). CNS penetrant; produces sedation, amnesia, motion sickness control, and at high doses, delirium and hallucination. Modern pharmaceutical use: transdermal patch for motion sickness (Transderm Scōp), pre-anaesthetic medication, palliative care for secretion control.

- **Hyoscyamine (L-Atropine) — (1R,3r,5S)-8-methyl-8-azabicyclo[3.2.1]octan-3-yl (2S)-3-hydroxy-2-phenylpropanoate:**
  The natural levorotatory form of atropine. More potent CNS activity than the racemic mixture. Antisecretory, antispasmodic, mydriatic. Found throughout the plant; higher concentration in roots and seeds.

- **Atropine — (±)-hyoscyamine (racemic):**
  The racemic mixture formed during extraction/processing. Primary modern pharmaceutical agent derived from *Atropa belladonna* (closely related Solanaceae). Emergency cardiac use (bradycardia), nerve agent antidote, mydriasis. *Atropine auto-injectors* (EpiPen-style for nerve agent exposure) contain atropine derived from or modelled on this chemistry.

- **Additional alkaloids:** Scopolamine-N-oxide, norhyoscyamine, aposcopolamine; total alkaloid content typically 0.04–0.15% dry weight of leaf

- **Ratio note:** *H. niger* (black henbane) contains primarily scopolamine ≈ 60–65% of total alkaloid with hyoscyamine ≈ 35–40%. This ratio contrasts with *Atropa belladonna* (primarily hyoscyamine/atropine) and *Datura* species (primarily scopolamine + hyoscyamine in various ratios).

### Mechanism of Action

**Muscarinic receptor blockade** — all therapeutic and toxic effects follow from this single mechanism:

| Receptor site | Effect of blockade | Clinical manifestation |
|---------------|-------------------|----------------------|
| Heart (M2) | Removes vagal tone | Tachycardia |
| Pupil (M3) | Blocks sphincter pupillae | Mydriasis (dilation) |
| Exocrine glands (M3) | Reduces secretion | Dry mouth, dry eyes, reduced sweating |
| Smooth muscle (M3) | Reduces contraction | Bronchodilation; reduced gut motility |
| CNS (M1, M4) | Disrupts cholinergic transmission | Sedation → amnesia → delirium → coma |
| Bladder (M3) | Reduces detrusor contraction | Urinary retention |

The **anticholinergic toxidrome** (overdose presentation) is classically memorized:
> *"Dry as a bone, hot as a hare, blind as a bat, red as a beet, mad as a hatter"*

Corresponding to: anhidrosis, hyperthermia, mydriasis, flushing, and delirium.

### Historical Significance in Anaesthesiology

The **soporific sponge** (*spongia somnifera*) described by Ibn Sina, Hugh of Lucca (~1200 CE), Theodoric of Cervia, and others represents a continuous tradition of compound narcotic anaesthesia from at least 100 CE through approximately 1500 CE — a 1,400-year window of surgical anesthesia largely forgotten by the Victorian period:

**Composition (various historical formulas):**
- Opium (*Papaver somniferum*) — μ-opioid analgesia
- Henbane (*Hyoscyamus*) — scopolamine, amnesia and sedation
- Mandrake (*Mandragora*) — hyoscyamine, atropine, narcosis
- Hemlock (*Conium maculatum*) — coniine, flaccid paralysis
- White mulberry juice, black nightshade (variable by formula)
- Wine as vehicle and preservative

The sponge was soaked in the decoction, dried, and stored. For use: re-moistened with water and held beneath the patient's nose. When the desired sedation was achieved, the surgeon operated. Reversal was accomplished with a sponge soaked in vinegar (acidic medium reverses alkaloid absorption from the nasal mucosa).

Modern anaesthesiologists have noted that the pharmacological logic of this formula is sound — the combination of opioid analgesia, anticholinergic sedation/amnesia, and partial paralysis effectively mirrors modern balanced anaesthesia.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Training applications: NONE.** Henbane has no appropriate place in training-phase pharmacology for the Kenetica practitioner.

**Historical pharmacological significance:** The henbane family provided the primary surgical anaesthetic agents for pre-modern surgery across six traditions. The insight that pain management and consciousness modulation were pharmacologically achievable — derived from empirical encounter with the Solanaceae — is one of the most consequential discoveries in medical history.

**Modern pharmaceutical descendants:** Scopolamine (transdermal motion sickness patch), hyoscine butylbromide (Buscopan — antispasmodic), atropine (emergency medicine, ophthalmology), ipratropium (inhaled bronchodilator for COPD) — all trace their pharmacological lineage directly to *H. niger* and related Solanaceae.

### Preparation & Dosing

**For reference/historical purposes only:**

Traditional preparations included:
- **Seed decoction** — most toxic form; seeds contain highest alkaloid concentration
- **Leaf poultice** — topical analgesic for joint pain, tumors (used by Dioscorides)
- **Smoke inhalation** — seeds burned on charcoal for toothache (still used in folk medicine in parts of South Asia and North Africa)
- **Wine maceration** — narcotic wine; soporific compound preparations
- **Soporific sponge** — the surgical anaesthetic preparation described above

**Modern pharmaceutical doses (for reference):**
- Scopolamine transdermal patch: 1mg/72h (motion sickness — very low systemic dose)
- Hyoscine butylbromide (Buscopan): 10–20mg oral (peripheral antispasmodic; does not cross BBB at this dose)
- Atropine IV: 0.5–1mg (cardiac bradycardia — hospital emergency use only)

### Scientific Correlation

- **Muscarinic pharmacology:** Classic pharmacology textbook reference; the muscarinic receptor was defined and characterized largely through experiments using atropine from Solanaceae
- **Motion sickness:** Multiple RCTs confirm scopolamine transdermal superior to antihistamines for motion sickness prevention
- **Palliative care:** Hyoscine hydrobromide SC injection is first-line for death rattle (terminal secretion management) in palliative medicine
- **Anaesthesia history:** Nunn JF (1996), *Ancient Egyptian Medicine*; Kee WD (1999) — historical anaesthesia review; Watt JM (1967) on soporific sponge history
- **Nerve agent antidote connection:** Atropine + pralidoxime standard treatment for organophosphate nerve agent poisoning; directly exploits the muscarinic receptor mechanism understood from Solanaceae pharmacology for 2,000+ years
- **Antispasmodic (pharmaceutical validation):** Tytgat GN (2008) *Aliment Pharmacol Ther* 27 Suppl 2:26–33 — hyoscine butylbromide (Buscopan) significantly reduced IBS abdominal cramping vs placebo, directly validating Ibn Sina's colic indication; El Bazaoui A et al. (2011) *Ind Crops Prod* 34:3–8 — HPLC of *Hyoscyamus* tropane-alkaloid profiles, confirming the inter-specimen variability behind traditional dose unpredictability

### Cultivation Register

*Henbane is a deadly tropane plant. This section records historical religious and ritual associations **as history**. It is not a recommendation, it contains no preparation or dose guidance, and nothing in it should be read as an invitation to use. Where a tradition's association is unverified, it is marked unverified rather than repeated.*

**Assyrian (AT).** Absent. Henbane is frequently equated with the Akkadian *šakirû* in popular literature, but **no scholarly confirmation of that identification was found**, and the Codex's blocking warning on Assyrian substance identifications, deity and demon attributions and tablet references remains in force. Illness in the cuneiform material is classified partly by causal agency — divine hand, ghost, oath — so a substance can be partly defined by what agency it opposes; but assigning henbane such a role without a secure identification would be exactly the fabrication this register exists to prevent. No cultivation status is claimed.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** Henbane is attested in the Unani materia medica as *banj* / *bazr al-banj*, in the class of stupefying drugs (*mukhaddir*). **No substance-specific cultivation status is attested** in academic sources retrieved, and no *Canon* chapter reference is given here on principle. The tradition's cultivation frame stands on its own: **innate heat and radical moisture are a finite endowment**, ageing *is* their depletion, and longevity practice is therefore **conservation, not acquisition**. Where henbane falls against that measure is not stated by the sources, and is not inferred here.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured; a cultivation status is not available in principle. He does distinguish kinds of *hyoskyamos* and treats the plant as dangerous — and it is worth recording that he elsewhere **notes amuletic and ritual uses of plants while explicitly distancing himself from them**. Recording is not endorsing, and that distinction is itself part of what the tradition teaches. What it offers a cultivator is a **discipline of attention**: identification, provenance, season, and the sober recognition of a plant one is not going to handle.

**Egyptian (EP).** Absent. Henbane is often asserted to appear in the Ebers material, but the corpus is **indication-organised** — it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to a person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib* — and the identification of its plant names is a **modern hypothesis** that no source meeting this project's standard settles for henbane. *Heka* is real where attested; it is not attested for this plant, and no deity attribution is offered.

**South American (SA).** Absent, and importantly so. *Hyoscyamus niger* is an Old World plant with **no South American attestation retrieved**. The *dieta*, the *plantas maestras* relation in which the plant is an agent rather than a tool, and the structural role of the *vegetalista* are genuine features of South American practice — **and none of them is extended to henbane here.** The temptation to transfer the framing attached to *Brugmansia*, *Datura* or ayahuasca onto a botanically or pharmacologically adjacent plant is precisely the error this register forbids. There is also **no pan-South-American subtle-anatomy model** to invoke, and none is invented.

**Chinese / Shennong (TCM).** Henbane is attested in the Chinese materia medica as *langdang* 莨菪, its seeds as *langdangzi* 莨菪子, and it is also recorded under the name **天仙子, "heavenly transcendent seeds."** That name is a **lexical fact and nothing more** — a name is not a status assignment, and no *san pin* 三品 grade or cultivation status is built on it here. Secondary literature associates prolonged-use and spirit-communication language from the *Shennong bencao jing* with this plant, but **the primary wording could not be verified in scholarship read directly for this entry** (the trail runs through encyclopaedic summaries to Li Hui-Lin, *Botanical Museum Leaflets, Harvard University* 25(6):161–181, 1977, which was not accessible here). Under this project's sourcing rule that claim therefore **stays unmade**: henbane carries **no attested cultivation status in this register**, only the recognition that the tradition placed it among the potent and dangerous drugs. When the underlying scholarship is read, this paragraph can be revisited — upward only on evidence.

**Ayurveda (SS).** Henbane is attested in the Ayurvedic and nighantu literature as ***Pārasīka yavānī***, and the recorded name is itself a foreign-origin designation. The classification given above — *Vishayukta*, *Tikshna*, and use only in *Shodhita* form if at all — places it firmly in the toxic class. **No *rasayana* or *vajikarana* membership is attested**, and no *ojas* claim is made; the tradition's own handling of this plant is one of restriction, not cultivation. Worth noting that *shodhana* here is not symbolic: purification procedures in this tradition are **measurably** effective on other toxic drugs, demonstrably reducing strychnine and brucine in *nux-vomica* and abrin in *Abrus*.

**Tibetan (FMT).** No cultivation status is attested, and **no formal category of "substances for subtle-body practice" exists** in this tradition to place it in; none is asserted. The framing the system does offer: the three *nyes pa* derive from the three poisons — attachment, aversion, delusion — so pathology is at root a spiritual condition, and medicine is the **third of four treatment branches**, supporting practice rather than reaching its root. A stupefying drug is conspicuously not that root.

**On the witchcraft and oracular reputation.** Henbane's association with divination, flight and sorcery is genuine and well known — but it belongs overwhelmingly to **early-modern European** magical literature and to the Greek world, **neither of which is among the eight traditions this entry is tagged with**. It is recorded here as a historical fact about the plant's reception, deliberately **not** written up as a cultivation paragraph, because doing so would mean fabricating a tradition-slot for it. Historically, that reputation is inseparable from the plant's toxicology: the delirium is the mechanism, and the people it killed are part of the record too.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with henbane. Not "nothing yet," not "nothing without a teacher" — nothing. This is a plant that kills by the same action that made it famous, at doses that are not reliably distinguishable from the ones that intoxicate, and no tradition tagged in this entry assigns it a cultivation status that would justify the risk. Its correct place in a cultivator's education is as **knowledge**: learn to identify it so as to recognise it in a hedgerow, in a field margin, and in someone else's poorly labelled preparation. Abstention is a legitimate practice and here it is the only one. Where the historical record shows this plant used ritually, that record is a subject of study, not a set of instructions.

### Contraindications

- **Glaucoma** — raises intraocular pressure by blocking ciliary muscle; acute angle-closure glaucoma emergency if administered
- **Urinary retention / benign prostatic hyperplasia** — blocks detrusor, worsens retention acutely
- **Tachycardia / cardiac arrhythmia** — removes vagal tone; dangerous in tachyarrhythmias
- **Myasthenia gravis** — anticholinergic agents are absolutely contraindicated
- **Children and elderly** — extreme sensitivity; delirium at doses adults tolerate
- **Pregnancy** — Category C risk; crosses placenta; produces fetal tachycardia
- **Heat stress environments** — blocks sweating; dangerous hyperthermia in hot conditions
- **Concurrent anticholinergic medications** — additive toxicity (antihistamines, TCAs, antipsychotics all have anticholinergic activity)
- **LETHAL DOSE RISK:** The margin between effective dose and lethal dose for naive users is narrow; consumption of seeds is the most common cause of anticholinergic poisoning from accidental plant ingestion in Europe and the Middle East
