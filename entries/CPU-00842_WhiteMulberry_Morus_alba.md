# White Mulberry — Sang (桑, Chinese); leaf *Sang Ye* (桑叶), fruit *Sang Shen* (桑椹); *Tūt* (Persian/Arabic); *Dar-mo* (Tibetan materia medica)

## *Morus alba* L. | Family: Moraceae

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ, SS, FMT
**Energetic Classification:** Leaf — cool, sweet-bitter, light-dispersing; Fruit — cool-to-neutral, sweet, moistening and nourishing
**Training Phase:** Metabolic Regulation / Clearing — the phase concerned with steadying the sugar-and-fluid economy and cooling low-grade heat

---

### Origin & Tradition

White mulberry is one of the most thoroughly "dual-use" plants in the cross-tradition canon: the same tree yields a **cooling, dispersing leaf** and a **nourishing, moistening fruit**, and the traditions keep the two firmly distinct.

**Chinese / Shennong layer (SBJ).** In the Chinese materia medica descending from the *Shennong Bencao Jing*, the mulberry is a whole-plant pharmacy. *Sang Ye* (mulberry leaf) is a cool, sweet-bitter herb of the Lung and Liver channels used to "disperse wind-heat," clear the eyes, and cool the upper body; *Sang Shen* (the fruit) is a sweet, cooling *yin*- and blood-nourishing tonic for dryness, thirst and depletion; the root bark (*Sang Bai Pi*) and twig (*Sang Zhi*) have their own indications. The leaf's use for "wasting-thirst" (*xiao ke*) — the classical syndrome of great thirst, hunger and copious urine that maps closely onto what we now call diabetes — is the traditional root of its modern glycaemic reputation.

**Ayurvedic / Sushruta layer (SS).** Mulberry (*tūt*) appears in the Indian materia medica as a sweet, cooling, moistening fruit — *madhura* rasa, *sheeta virya* — used to relieve thirst, cool the blood, and soothe dryness of the throat and gut. Its placement as a cooling, fluid-restoring food aligns it with the same "quench heat and thirst" logic seen in the Chinese fruit indication.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi layer (FMT).** In the Tibetan medical tradition organised around the *rGyud-bzhi* (Four Tantras), mulberry material enters compound formulae as a cooling, fluid-balancing ingredient; as with many shared Asian materia medica plants it is used in multi-herb prescriptions rather than as a solo drug. **Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The mulberry's doctrine is *the tree that feeds the transformer* — its leaf is the sole food of the silkworm, and the tradition reads it as a plant that governs transformation and orderly conversion. Under Kenetica that symbolism is a near-literal readout of its pharmacology: the leaf's signature alkaloid, **1-deoxynojirimycin (DNJ)**, is a sugar-shaped molecule that sits in the gut's carbohydrate-digesting enzymes and slows the conversion of dietary starch and sucrose into absorbable glucose. A plant emblematic of measured transformation contains, in fact, a molecule that meters the transformation of sugar. The cooling of "wasting-thirst" and the slowing of the post-meal glucose rise are the same action seen from two epochs.

### Active Components

- **1-Deoxynojirimycin (DNJ)** — an iminosugar α-glucosidase inhibitor; the principal antihyperglycaemic constituent of the leaf, concentrated in young top leaves harvested in summer.
- **Polyhydroxylated alkaloids** related to DNJ (fagomine and others).
- **Flavonoids** — rutin, quercetin and kaempferol glycosides (leaf); **anthocyanins** in the fruit (in *Morus alba* generally paler/lower than in black mulberry).
- **Chlorogenic and other phenolic acids**; **1,5-anhydroglucitol** and polysaccharides.
- Fruit fraction: sugars, organic acids, vitamin C, carotenoids — the nourishing/moistening principle.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Mulberry leaf is the phase's **glucose-metering** agent: taken with or just before a carbohydrate meal, its DNJ blunts the postprandial glucose spike by inhibiting intestinal α-glucosidase — the same target class as the drug acarbose. Its Kenetica use case is the Metabolic-Regulation indication of an over-sharp post-meal rise and the low-grade heat/thirst pattern that accompanies it. The **fruit** is the complementary moistening tonic for the dry, depleted, thirsty presentation. Leaf = disperse and meter; fruit = nourish and moisten.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Preparation | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mulberry leaf tea / powder (with meals) | powder standardised to ~**12 mg DNJ** taken up to 3× daily with meals | Trial-supported minimum effective DNJ dose; take at the start of a carbohydrate meal |
| Single-dose leaf powder (glucose blunting) | 0.8–1.2 g DNJ-enriched powder (≈12–18 mg DNJ) before a sugar load | Higher single doses raise GI side-effects (bloating, flatulence, loose stool) |
| Mulberry fruit (*Sang Shen*) | 10–15 g dried fruit, decoction or as food | Nourishing/moistening tonic; not a glucose-lowering dose |
| Traditional leaf decoction (*Sang Ye*) | 5–10 g dried leaf | Classical "disperse wind-heat / cool the eyes" use |

Take leaf preparations **with the meal**, not apart from it — the mechanism is intraluminal enzyme inhibition, so timing is the whole point.

### Scientific Correlation

The glycaemic action of mulberry leaf is one of the better-supported traditional-remedy claims, resting on real human randomised trials.

According to PubMed: Kimura et al. (2007, *J Agric Food Chem*) showed in healthy volunteers that a single dose of DNJ-enriched mulberry powder (equivalent to 12 and 18 mg DNJ) significantly suppressed the postprandial rise in blood glucose and insulin after a sucrose load ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf062680g)). Thaipitakwong et al. (2020, *Complementary Therapies in Medicine*) ran a randomised dose-finding and 12-week efficacy/safety study in obese people with borderline diabetes, identifying **12 mg DNJ** as the minimum effective dose for blunting postprandial hyperglycaemia and reporting modest reductions in fasting plasma glucose (−3.86 mg/dL) and HbA1c (−0.11%), with gastrointestinal symptoms (bloating, flatulence, loose stools) as the common adverse effects ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctim.2019.102292)). A 2025 randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial using continuous glucose monitoring (Shinkawa et al., *Nutrients*) found a mulberry-leaf-containing tea significantly lowered postprandial glucose variability and the 1-hour glucose AUC over two weeks, without increased hypoglycaemia on CGM ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu17142308)).

**Evidence grade: Good for postprandial glucose blunting (multiple human RCTs, consistent mechanism via DNJ α-glucosidase inhibition). Effects on longer-term HbA1c are real but modest. Fruit-as-tonic claims remain traditional.**

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** *Sang Ye* (leaf) — cool, sweet and bitter; Lung and Liver channels; disperses wind-heat, clears the eyes, cools the blood. *Sang Shen* (fruit) — sweet, cool(-neutral); Heart, Liver, Kidney; nourishes yin and blood, generates fluids, moistens dryness.
- **Ayurvedic (fruit):** *madhura* (sweet) rasa, *guru/snigdha* (heavy, unctuous) guna, *sheeta* (cooling) virya, *madhura* vipaka — pacifies Pitta and Vata, relieves thirst and dryness.
- **Unani (by analogy):** the fruit cold-and-moist, quenching and nourishing; consistent with its "cools heat and thirst" use.

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ Hypoglycaemia risk with antidiabetic drugs.** Because mulberry leaf actively lowers postprandial (and modestly fasting) glucose, combining it with insulin, sulfonylureas, meglitinides, acarbose or other glucose-lowering agents can produce **additive hypoglycaemia**. Anyone on antidiabetic medication should use it only with clinician oversight and glucose monitoring, and doses may need adjustment. Watch for shakiness, sweating, confusion.
- **Gastrointestinal effects:** bloating, flatulence and loose stools are dose-related (the α-glucosidase-inhibition mechanism delivers undigested carbohydrate to the colon) — start low.
