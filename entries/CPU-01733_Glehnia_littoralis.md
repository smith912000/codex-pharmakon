# Glehnia littoralis — Sha Shen, Radix Glehniae Littoralis (Shennong Bencao Jing)

## *Glehnia littoralis, as the pharmaceutical Latin "Radix Glehniae Littoralis" that Yang 1998 prints for the entry Sha Shen* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Shennong text (SBJ); this entry rests on one text.** No other text read for this entry names this species. The entry is in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`). The Latin name is the translator's pharmaceutical Latin and was matched to the binomial by stem in the extraction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, row 101: "pharmaceutical Latin 'Radix Glehniae Littoralis' matched by stem to on-disk binomial 'Glehnia littoralis'"). A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk, and of the two Tibetan texts, for the genus name *Glehnia* found only Yang's entry, its footnote and the extraction row.

**Identity doubt.** The Codex's own list of Shennong names (`_sources/shennong.csv`, line 138) reads "Adenophora Root (沙參 Shā Shēn)", which gives the same Chinese name as *Adenophora*, a different genus, and not *Glehnia*. Yang's English text names the drug "Radix Glehniae Littoralis" in the entry and in footnote 143 (lines 2374 and 2379, "Glehnia"). This entry follows Yang's printed Latin, which is also what the extraction and the spine row use. It does not say that the classic's Sha Shen was *Glehnia* rather than *Adenophora*, and the list's characters are not used in the title for that reason. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *Sha Shen, PDF pages 62 and 63.* The entry opens on `[[p62]]` (line 2349), at line 2358, after Fang Feng, and closes on `[[p63]]` (line 2388). The text reads, with the page break between "Protracted" and "taking":

"Sha Shen (Radix Glehniae Littoralis)143 is bitter and slightly cold. It is nontoxic, mainly treating blood accumulation and fright qi. It eliminates cold and heat, supplements the center, and boosts the lung qi. Protracted taking brings benefit to people.144 Its other name is Zhi Mu (Gratitude to Mother). It grows in rivers and valleys."

(Lines 2358 to 2361 and 2390 to 2392. The OCR prints "takin~" at line 2390.) The superscripts 143 and 144 are Yang's footnote numbers. Footnote 143 (lines 2374 to 2386) is the translator's commentary and not the classic's text; it begins "Ginseng and Glehnia are both able to supplement the qi or, more specifically, the lung qi. Glehnia, however, is also able to supplement yin" and then quotes Xu Da-chun, and it is not used here as the Shennong text's claim. Footnote 144 (line 2403) says that the Chinese word *li* "is translated by Wiseman as 'to disinhibit' when used in a Chinese medical sense, but nonmedically means to benefit"; it is the translator's note on the verb of "brings benefit to people". The entry prints no toxicity statement other than "nontoxic".

*The class.* No class heading is printed above the entry on its own page: `[[p62]]` is followed by the running head "The Divine Farmer's Materia Medica Classic" (line 2350) and then Fang Feng. The entry's closing lines (2390 to 2392) sit directly under "Herbs: Middle Class", printed at line 2389 at the top of `[[p63]]`. The nearest heading above the entry's opening is "Herbs: Middle Class" at line 2315, at the top of `[[p61]]`. The preface (Book One, lines 385 to 390) says the middle-class medicinals "mainly nurture personality and correspond to humanity", that they "may or may not be toxic", and that one bases the effort "to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion" on the middle class.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Glehnia*, "Radix Glehniae" and "Sha Shen" found only I_171 (*Dendrobium nobile*), whose closing cross-reference line names "Sha Shen" among the herbs that moisten and generate fluids in a tradition other than this text; it is not an entry on this plant. No entry on *Adenophora* was found. I_1289 (*Gentiana macrophylla*, Qin Jiao) is a sibling entry from the same page range of the Shennong text and the same class. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The entry itself prints no constituent. It is a general fact that *Glehnia littoralis* is a coastal plant of the carrot family (Apiaceae) and that members of the family commonly contain coumarins, including furanocoumarins. The sources read do not say what the Shennong drug contained or which part was used beyond the name "Radix" in the translator's Latin.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Shennong entry records a bitter, slightly cold, nontoxic drug that "mainly treat[s] blood accumulation and fright qi", "eliminates cold and heat, supplements the center, and boosts the lung qi", and whose protracted taking "brings benefit to people". These are the text's claims in its own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The entry gives no preparation, no part used beyond the name "Radix" in the translator's Latin, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. "Protracted taking" is the only direction on duration and it carries no number. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: the middle class (中品), by position.* The class is assigned by the printed heading "Herbs: Middle Class". The heading stands at the top of `[[p63]]` (line 2389), directly above the entry's last three lines, and at the top of `[[p61]]` (line 2315), and not above the entry's opening on `[[p62]]`; so the grade rests on the entry's position between the two, with the continuation directly under the heading. This is a printed class and not a promise of cultivation use. In the preface (lines 385 to 390) the middle class mainly "nurture[s] personality", may or may not be toxic, and is the class on which one bases the effort to control disease, supplement vacuity and replenish exhaustion. It is not the class the preface ties to making the body light and prolonging life, which is the superior class (lines 352 to 355).

The entry's own words fit that frame and go a little further than a bare indication list: it "supplements the center" and "boosts the lung qi", and "protracted taking brings benefit to people". The last sentence is a statement about long use, and the translator's footnote reads its verb as "to benefit", not as any named effect. It says nothing of making the body light or of prolonging life. It asserts no *jing*, *shen* or *neidan* action, and none is added here.

**Practitioner's note.** A middle-class entry in the Chinese scheme: for controlling disease, weighed before use, and not the class of long-term life-nourishing drugs, although this entry does carry a "protracted taking" sentence. The entry promises no cultivation outcome beyond "benefit" and gives no dose. Its identity as *Glehnia* rather than *Adenophora* is the translator's. Nothing here supports taking this root as a cultivation practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The entry prints no warning and calls the drug "nontoxic"; the preface says the middle class "may or may not be toxic" and that one should "weigh and ponder before putting them to their appropriate use" (line 387 onward). No dose, preparation or safety information is given for this root, and the entry's identity is the translator's pharmaceutical Latin. Do not gather or take a coastal plant on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
