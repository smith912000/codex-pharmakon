### Botanical Identity
Perennial 20–90cm; feathery, finely divided aromatic leaves (the "thousand leaves"); flat-topped white to pale pink flower clusters (corymbs); pungent aromatic scent; cosmopolitan weed of meadows, roadsides, and disturbed ground in temperate regions worldwide. Among the most archaeologically ancient medicinal plants: *Achillea* pollen found in Neanderthal burial sites (Shanidar Cave, Iraq, ~60,000 BCE) — potentially the oldest evidence of intentional medicinal plant use in human prehistory.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 De Materia Medica (DMM)

Dioscorides (*Book IV, Chapter 36*):
> *"Achilleios: warming and drying. The leaves and the plant are good for fresh wounds — stops bleeding, closes wounds. Taken with wine it relieves pain in the lower bowels. Applied with oil it heals old wounds and calms inflammations."*

The mythological attribution to Achilles (who used it to staunch battlefield wounds in the Trojan War) precedes Dioscorides; the medical application is the same as the myth.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine (CM)

Ibn Sīnā on *buqayliqa*:
> *"Drying and astringent. Stops bleeding from wounds, staunches blood in the urine. Relieves toothache and headache. Useful for haemorrhoids and intestinal bleeding. The dried herb in wine benefits fevers."*

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (EP)

White-flowered wound herbs consistent with yarrow in Egyptian wound-staunching preparations.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)

Aromatic wound herbs in Babylonian texts for haemostasis and wound infection prevention.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Haemostatic (Primary Traditional Application)

**Achilleic acid + tannins + rutin**: Multiple haemostatic mechanisms:
- **Achilleic acid**: Directly promotes platelet aggregation → primary haemostasis
- **Tannins**: Protein precipitation → vasoconstriction at wound site → reduces blood flow
- **Rutin**: Strengthens capillary walls → reduces capillary fragility → prevents haemorrhage

The triple haemostatic mechanism (platelet activation + vasoconstriction + capillary strengthening) explains why yarrow has been the universal battlefield wound herb — three complementary mechanisms addressing different aspects of wound bleeding.

### 5.2 Anti-Inflammatory

**Chamazulene + apigenin + achillin**: COX-2 inhibition; 5-LOX inhibition; NF-κB modulation. The sesquiterpene lactone achillin is particularly active against cytokine-driven inflammation.

### 5.3 Antispasmodic / Digestive

**Apigenin + luteolin + camphor**: Smooth muscle relaxation → antispasmodic for gut, biliary, and uterine smooth muscle. Explains the traditional application for intestinal colic, bile duct spasm, and dysmenorrhoea.

### 5.4 Diaphoretic / Antipyretic

**Volatile EO components (camphor, cineole)**: Promote perspiration at elevated body temperature → assists fever clearance via evaporative cooling. The classical "breaks fevers through sweating" application.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Haemostasis (topical)**: Multiple in vitro and clinical case series confirm yarrow's haemostatic efficacy. Direct comparison studies confirm superiority to pressure alone for minor wound haemostasis.

**Wound healing**: Formal RCT data limited; extensive traditional use with consistent practitioner-reported outcomes. German Commission E approved for external use in mild haemostasis and wound care.

**Dysmenorrhoea**: Kashefi (2015): Yarrow capsules significantly reduced menstrual pain duration and intensity vs placebo in primary dysmenorrhoea RCT.

**GI / IBS**: Commission E approved for "loss of appetite and dyspeptic disorders" based on traditional evidence and pharmacological plausibility of the bitter-tonic and antispasmodic mechanisms.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Achilles' wound medicine**: The Iliad records Patroclus treating Eurypylus' wound with yarrow — and Achilles was himself taught wound medicine by the centaur Chiron (the mythological teacher of medicine). The attribution to Achilles is the oldest named medical recommendation for any plant in Western literature — the hero of the Trojan War as the proto-physician, using the plant that bears his name on the battlefield. Whether or not Achilles existed, the attribution encodes: this is the warrior's first-response plant for bleeding wounds. The cultural encoding in mythology preserved the clinical application more durably than any medical text could.

**Neanderthal and the oldest medicine**: The 1960 Shanidar Cave discovery (Ralph Solecki, Smithsonian) found *Achillea* pollen (among other medicinal plants) clustered around a Neanderthal burial approximately 60,000 BCE. Whether the flowers were intentionally placed or incidentally deposited by animals remains debated — but if intentional, this is the earliest evidence of any creature deliberately using medicinal plants. Yarrow was potentially the first recorded medicine in the human lineage — which is either a remarkable archaeological coincidence or an indicator that the haemostatic and wound-healing properties of yarrow were already known to Neanderthal communities.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

| Form | Dose | Application |
|------|------|-------------|
| Fresh crushed leaf (topical) | Applied directly | Acute wound haemostasis — traditional battlefield use |
| Tea (aerial parts) | 3–5g per cup | Digestive; emmenagogue; antipyretic; diaphoretic |
| Tincture (1:5) | 3–5ml 3× daily | Systemic anti-inflammatory; digestive; menstrual |
| Essential oil (diluted 2%) | Topical | Anti-inflammatory; antimicrobial; wound care |
| Poultice (fresh herb) | Applied to wound/joint | Topical anti-inflammatory; haemostatic |

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Bitter, acrid; slightly cool. Channels: Liver (肝), Stomach (胃), Lung (肺). Cools Blood, stops bleeding, resolves toxins, disperses Qi stagnation.

**Ayurvedic**: Bitter, pungent; slightly cooling. Reduces Pitta ↓↓. *Shotahara* (anti-inflammatory), *Shoṇitasthāpana* (haemostatic), *Artavajanana* (emmenagogue).

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Pregnancy**: Emmenagogue — therapeutic doses contraindicated; topical haemostatic safe
- **Asteraceae allergy**: Cross-reactivity (ragweed, chamomile) — patch test topical; avoid internal if allergy
- **Anticoagulants**: Haemostatic + mild anticoagulant paradox — lower doses haemostatic; clarify dose with coagulation team; monitor with warfarin
- **Photosensitivity**: Furanocoumarins in some populations → skin photosensitisation; avoid sun exposure after topical use on large areas
- Culinary yarrow (small amounts in salad) generally safe
