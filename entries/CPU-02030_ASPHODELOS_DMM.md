# ASPHODELOS [DMM] — Asphodelos (ἀσφόδελος), "Asphodel, King's Rod"; flower called anthericon; Roman name albucium (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 2-199)

## *Asphodelus aestivus Brot. (also written A. ramosus L.), a tall Mediterranean lily-family plant with a leafy base, a stalk and a spike of white flowers, whose tuberous root is the drug; the identification is ours, from the Greek name and the standard modern reading, and is of medium-high confidence at genus level and medium at species level, since the Dioscorides translator lists several suggested species (Asphodelus foemina, Lilium purpureum, Lilium martagone, Asphodelus albus, Asphodelus ramosus) and prints a plate labelled Asphodelus luteus* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

> **CAUTION.** Dioscorides himself says the plant is an emetic and that the seed and flowers "purge the intestines excessively". This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should take this plant on the strength of it.

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The only chapter is 2-199 in the translation on disk (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, chapter heading at line 6281, text from line 6285 to 6300 across a plate break). The name index (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`) holds the same row (ASPHODELOS, 2-199, line 6281). Other hits for the word in that file are the book's own introduction (line 411), the index pages, the "lily asphodel" suggestion under the lily chapters 1-62 and 3-116 (lines 4021 and 7571; a different plant), and a back-reference in the aloe chapter (line 6744), where aloe's seed is said to be "similar to asphodelus [2-199]". The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder; this entry replaces it.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter sits in the book's section "Herbs with a sharp quality" (the running head at lines 6080-6082 and again at line 6272), directly after arisaron (2-198). Dioscorides describes it as "a plant (known to most) with leaves similar to the great leek and a smooth stalk. On the top is a flower called anthericon. The roots are underneath — somewhat long, round, similar to suppositories, sharp to the taste, and warming in strength" (line 6287). The "known to most" is Dioscorides's own claim of familiarity; he gives no habitat in this chapter.

*Uses of the root, as printed (lines 6287-6300).* A teaspoonful of the roots taken in a drink of wine "induces the movement of urine and the menstrual flow", and cures "pains of the side, coughs, convulsions and hernia". It causes easy vomiting "if as much as a knucklebone is eaten with meat". Three teaspoonfuls given to those bitten by snakes "is effective", and a poultice of leaves, root and flowers with wine goes on the bite. The sediment of wine boiled with the root is for "filthy feeding ulcers, inflammation of the breasts, stones [kidney, urinary], tubercula [nodules], and boils or inflammatory tumours", and with polenta for new inflammation. The juice of the root with old sweet wine, myrrh and saffron, boiled together, is "an excellent medicine to rub on the eyes"; with frankincense, honey, wine and myrrh it goes into purulent ears; the juice alone poured into the opposite ear "lessens toothache". The burnt ashes of the root are rubbed on "to thicken thinned hair". Oil boiled in the hollowed roots over a fire is rubbed on ulcerated chilblains and burns, and poured into the ear it helps earache. The root is smeared on to take away "vitiligo alba [type of leprosy]" that has first been rubbed with a napkin in the sun. The seed and flowers in wine are "an extraordinary remedy against the strikes of millipedes, centipedes and scorpions", but "they purge the intestines excessively".

*Season of cutting.* "It bears flowers at the time of harvest but white asphodelus must be cut down around the vernal equinox before the seed increases" (line 6300). The word "white" is Dioscorides's; he does not say what other kind he means.

*Reported, not endorsed.* "They say that the root (taken in a drink) makes men have no appetite for pursuit of sexual pleasure [anaphrodisiac]." He then adds that Crateuas the herbalist "says the same", and that Crateuas gave one teaspoonful of the root in wine for the pains of gout. Dioscorides closes with the alternative names: "anthericum" and, among the Romans, "albucium".

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, asphodel roots are starchy tubers of a plant in the lily family (Asphodelaceae, the family that also holds aloe), and plants of this family often carry anthraquinone-type compounds, which are associated with a laxative action. This agrees in kind, though not as proof, with Dioscorides's remark that the seed and flowers purge the bowels. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** A long list of external and internal uses: urine and menses, side pain, cough, convulsion, hernia, snakebite and stings, ulcers, swellings, ear and eye complaints, hair, skin patches.
- **What the text does not say.** It gives no safe limit beyond the measures quoted below, no duration of use, and no identification of the "white" plant it contrasts with.

The Codex does not translate Dioscorides's disease names into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend this plant. The one tradition on the tag line records a household plant "known to most"; that is a fact about the ancient pharmacy, not a guide to present use.

### Preparation & Dosing

Preparations given by Dioscorides at 2-199 (lines 6287-6300): the root taken in wine; the root eaten with meat as an emetic; a poultice of leaf, root and flower with wine; wine sediment boiled with the root; the root juice with old sweet wine, myrrh and saffron for the eyes; the juice with frankincense, honey, wine and myrrh for the ears; burnt root ash for the hair; oil boiled in the hollowed roots; the root smeared on the skin; seed and flowers in wine.

Measures printed (translator's units): "a teaspoonful" of the roots in wine for urine and menses; "as much as a knucklebone" eaten with meat for vomiting; "as much as three teaspoonfuls" for snakebite; "one teaspoonful" in wine for gout, on the authority of Crateuas. These are the translator's renderings of ancient measures, and the original Greek units are not shown in the text on disk. No dose is given for the other preparations. **Do not use any measure from this entry.** [USER: verify against the Greek before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Asphodel is a Mediterranean perennial with a deep tuberous root and tall flower spikes, grown or found wild on dry ground, and its root is starchy. Members of its family are known to contain anthraquinone-type compounds with laxative action, and a root that causes vomiting and purging is consistent with an irritant plant. The claims of snakebite, scorpion sting, hernia, convulsions and leprosy have no modern equivalent and are not translated into one. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM (Dioscorides).** The root is "sharp to the taste, and warming in strength" (line 6287). That is the only quality printed. No other quality (moist, dry, cold) is given for it in the chapter.
- The chapter's place among the "herbs with a sharp quality" agrees with the "sharp to the taste" description.
- No Galenic temperament scheme is applied; that is later than Dioscorides and is not imported here.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is attested for this plant in the chapter. What the chapter offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: a plant "known to most" described by leaf, stalk, flower and root; a sensory assay (the root is "sharp to the taste"); and a season of gathering ("cut down around the vernal equinox before the seed increases"). The anaphrodisiac remark is reported with "they say" and a named authority, Crateuas, which is recording and not endorsing, and it assigns no vital or spiritual status.

**Practitioner's note.** A sharp, warming, emetic root that the ancient pharmacy used for many outward and inward complaints. A cultivator takes nothing from it except the habit of noting how Dioscorides identifies a plant, when he says to cut it, and what he says it does to the bowels. It is not a cultivation substance and should not be taken.

### Contraindications

- **Emetic and purgative.** Dioscorides says the root causes vomiting and the seed and flowers purge the intestines "excessively". Do not ingest.
- **Pregnancy and menstrual flow.** The root is said to move the menses (line 6287). Given that, avoid in pregnancy. [USER: verify]
- **Lactation, children, the weak.** No statement in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Snakebite, convulsions, hernia, leprosy-like skin patches.** These are medical conditions; use proper care and not this plant.
- **Identity.** The translator lists several suggested species; the species is our identification. [USER: verify]
- **Plant of the same name.** "Lily asphodel" in the lily chapters (1-62, 3-116) is a different plant; do not mix the two.
