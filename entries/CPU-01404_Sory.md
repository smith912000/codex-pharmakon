# Sory — Soru (as printed in the chapter heading, "SORU"); "sory" in the chapter text, Dioscorides 5-119
## *A black-looking, holey, "fat", astringent, poisonous mineral of the copper-vitriol group, nauseating and more poisonous than melanteria; the translator gives no identification ("UNKNOWN")* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-119, heading "SORU" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 10441; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 892, "DMM, SORU, 5-119 (line 10441), UNKNOWN, mineral"; list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 845, spelt "Soru", mineral, flagged as an uncertain identification; the book index gives "SORU 803", line 12640, and "sory 781, 803", line 17643). The whole chapter was read; the text spells the name "sory". It reads (line 10443, continuing across the page break to the next page):

"Some are deceived, supposing sory to be melanteria [above], for it is a different thing though not unlike. Sory is more poisonous and causes nausea. It is found in Egypt and in other places such as Africa, Spain and Cyprus. The Egyptian is considered the best — that which looks blackest when broken, has many holes, is somewhat fat, and also astringent and poisonous to taste or smell, overturning the stomach. That which does not glisten when broken (like misy) is thought to be another kind and weak. It has the same properties as those mentioned above and is burnt like them. It heals toothaches (put into their cavities) and strengthens loose teeth. Given as a suppository with wine it helps sciatica, and smeared on with water it extinguishes varos [smallpox pustules]. It is mixed [with other ingredients] for dying hair black. As a general rule, for these and almost all others, those which are not burnt are considered to be stronger than the burnt; except for salt, wine sediment, saltpetre [potassium nitrate], chalk, and other similar things which are weak raw, but are more effective burnt."

The chapter thus gives: a warning about a confusion (sory is mistaken for melanteria, 5-118, but is "a different thing"); a comparison (more poisonous, causes nausea); places (Egypt, Africa, Spain, Cyprus); a rule for the best (the Egyptian; blackest when broken, many holes, somewhat fat); a sensory assay (astringent and poisonous to taste or smell, "overturning the stomach"); a weak kind (the one that does not glisten when broken, "like misy"); the statement that it has "the same properties as those mentioned above" and is "burnt like them"; and four uses (toothache and loose teeth, sciatica, smallpox pustules, hair dye).

**Chapters that mention it.**
- 5-84 (*Kadmeia*, cadmia): in describing a hill over Solis from which a burnt stone called *purites* is dug, Dioscorides says "in the same mound are found (as it were) some veins of chalcitis, misy, sory, melanteria, coeruleus, chrysocolla, calcanthus and diphryges" (line 10223). This places sory with the copper-bearing materials of the book, as a vein-mineral occurring in the same mound.
- 5-118 (*Melantheria*, line 10438), the chapter just before: "Melanteria grows together in the manner of salt at the mouths of mines out of which brass is dug ... The best is a sulphurous colour [yellow], smooth, even and clear, and on touching water it quickly turns black. It is sharp like misy [above]." (line 10440). Sory is set against it as its more poisonous look-alike.
- 5-117 (*Misu*, misy, line 10435), the chapter the weak kind of sory is compared to ("like misy"): "Cyprian misy must be chosen — looking like gold, hard, and if broken a golden colour, glistening like a star." (line 10437). So the sory that does not glisten when broken is contrasted with the glistening of misy.

**Which chapters "the same properties as those mentioned above" points to.** The chapter does not list them. The chapters immediately above it are 5-115 chalcitis (line 10426), 5-116 psoricum (line 10429), 5-117 misy and 5-118 melanteria. Chalcitis is printed there as "astringent, warming and scab forming" (line 10428). Misy "has the same strength as chalcitis" (line 10437). Melanteria is "sharp like misy" (line 10440). Whether the phrase means all four or only the nearest is not stated. [USER: verify]

**Identity.** The translator gives no identification ("UNKNOWN") and the Codex list marks the identification uncertain. The chapter's clues (a black, holey, somewhat fat, astringent, poisonous, stomach-turning mass found in Egypt, Africa, Spain and Cyprus, burnt like chalcitis and misy, and found in the same mound as the copper-bearing materials of 5-84) do not name a mineral. Which material Dioscorides meant is not settled by the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None can be stated from the chapter. The material is unidentified, so no composition is asserted. The chapter ties it by occurrence and by property to chalcitis, misy and melanteria, the materials the book groups with copper working, and calls it poisonous and nausea-causing. In general terms, mineral salts and sulphates of copper and iron are irritants and emetics when swallowed. That is a statement about such materials in general, not a reading of the chapter, and no composition is claimed for sory.

---

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records sory as a poisonous mineral used in the mouth for tooth complaints, as a suppository for sciatica, as an external application for smallpox pustules and as an ingredient in hair dye. He himself describes its taste and smell as "poisonous" and its action as "overturning the stomach". The material is unidentified and likely to be a toxic metal salt (an inference, not a statement of the chapter). The Codex supplies no modern protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations (line 10443): it is "burnt like them" (no method is given in this chapter; 5-116 psoricum, line 10429 ff., gives a burning procedure for that material, and 5-117 says misy "is burnt the same way" as chalcitis; which procedure sory follows is not stated); put into the cavities of aching teeth; given as a suppository with wine for sciatica; smeared on with water for smallpox pustules; and mixed with other ingredients for dyeing the hair black. The closing rule says unburnt material of "these and almost all others" is held stronger than burnt, with exceptions (salt, wine sediment, saltpetre, chalk and similar things, which are more effective burnt); the text does not say which of these applies to sory beyond "these". No amount, proportion or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM).** The chapter prints these qualities of sory itself: "astringent and poisonous to taste or smell", "more poisonous" than melanteria, and nauseating ("overturning the stomach"). It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality for sory. It says sory "has the same properties as those mentioned above", and the nearest chapters print "astringent, warming and scab forming" for chalcitis (line 10428) and "sharp" for melanteria (line 10440); which of these is meant is not stated (see Origin & Tradition), so they are not carried across here.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. What the herbal offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, sensory assay, adulteration detection), and this chapter is a compact example of it: it names the places (Egypt, Africa, Spain, Cyprus), the best kind (Egyptian, blackest when broken, holey, somewhat fat), a weak kind marked by not glistening when broken, a sensory test by taste or smell, and above all a substitution to guard against, since sory is mistaken for melanteria and is the more poisonous of the two. It gives no season of gathering. The chapter assigns it no cultivation status, only therapeutic uses.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to take from it for cultivation beyond the discipline the chapter shows: know a material's place and appearance, test it by the plainest means, and know its more dangerous look-alike. The text itself calls sory poisonous and stomach-turning, and it is not a recommended practice. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Poisonous by the text's own account:** the chapter calls sory "poisonous to taste or smell" and "more poisonous" than melanteria, and says it "overturn[s] the stomach". Do not taste, smell closely, swallow or insert it.
- **Possible toxic metal salt:** the material is unidentified but is grouped by Dioscorides with copper-bearing minerals; do not apply it to the mouth, rectum, broken skin or eyes.
- **Look-alike:** the text warns that sory is mistaken for melanteria, and that a kind which does not glisten when broken is weak; neither can be told apart safely by an untrained person today.
- **Unidentified material:** the identification is uncertain; nothing should be collected or used on the strength of the name.
- **Hair dye:** the chapter's black hair dye is a historical use; hair dyes containing metal salts are not endorsed.
