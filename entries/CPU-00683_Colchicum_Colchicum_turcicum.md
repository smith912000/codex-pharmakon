# Turkish Autumn Crocus — Autumn Crocus (Colchicum group), Meadow Saffron kin, Turkish Colchicum

## ⚠️ *Colchicum turcicum* Janka | Family: Colchicaceae

> **⚠️⚠️ POISON — DEADLY. This plant contains COLCHICINE, a potent antimitotic alkaloid with a NARROW THERAPEUTIC INDEX and NO SPECIFIC ANTIDOTE. Ingestion of plant material has caused fatal multi-organ failure. It is not a home remedy, not a food, and not a safe herb. This entry is documentary. Do not prepare, dose, or self-administer. All legitimate use is via pharmaceutical colchicine under medical supervision.** ⚠️⚠️

### Origin & Tradition

**Genus & species note:** *Colchicum turcicum* Janka is a Balkan–Anatolian member of the autumn-crocus genus. The historical materia medica does not name this micro-species; classical and medieval sources describe the *Colchicum* group (chiefly *Colchicum autumnale*, "meadow saffron / autumn crocus") and its colchicine-bearing relatives collectively. All statements of tradition below are therefore at the level of the genus, of which *C. turcicum* is a genuine, colchicine-containing member. The genus name itself derives from Colchis, the region on the Black Sea coast famed in Greek tradition for poisons.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** *De Materia Medica* explicitly describes *kolchikon* (ephemeron / autumn crocus) as a **poisonous** bulb — Dioscorides warns that it resembles edible bulbs but kills by "choking," and records remedies (milk) against poisoning by it. This is one of the clearest ancient toxicological warnings in the Greek corpus: the plant was recognised as lethal, not therapeutic, in classical antiquity. Its later reputation as a poison of accidental ingestion persisted directly from this description.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM):** Medieval Arabic and Persian medicine knew the autumn-crocus corm (*surinjan* — a name that also covers related colchicaceous drugs) as a potent, hazardous drug used cautiously, externally and internally, for joint pain and gout — an early, empirical grasp of the very indication (gout) for which colchicine is used today. Unani texts flag its toxicity and the need for correction/processing, reflecting genuine awareness of its danger.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Colchicum is the canonical *pharmakon* in the original Greek double sense — remedy and poison in one substance, separated only by dose. Its symbolic layer is not one of nourishment or tonification but of the sharp, cutting agent that halts a runaway process at the cost of grave risk. In the Kenetica frame it is a **boundary marker, not a tool**: it defines the edge where a plant's power becomes lethal. Its real pharmacology — arrest of cell division by binding tubulin and preventing microtubule assembly — is the molecular expression of "the substance that stops motion." That same mechanism that halts the inflammatory cascade in an acute gout flare also halts the division of gut and bone-marrow cells, which is precisely why it kills in overdose.

### Active Components

The defining constituent is **colchicine**, a tricyclic alkaloid that binds β-tubulin and blocks microtubule polymerisation, arresting mitosis in metaphase and disrupting all microtubule-dependent cellular processes (mitotic spindle, intracellular transport, neutrophil motility, and inflammasome trafficking). The corms and seeds also contain related alkaloids including **demecolcine (colchamine)** and other colchicinoids. There is **no meaningful therapeutic margin in crude plant material** — colchicine content varies with plant part, season, and specimen, making any home preparation an uncontrolled poisoning risk. Pharmaceutical colchicine is a purified, precisely dosed drug; the plant is not.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**None as a training agent.** Colchicum is included in the Codex as a hazard-reference and as the botanical source of a real, narrow-index pharmaceutical. Its legitimate medical uses — all requiring a physician and pharmaceutical colchicine, never plant material — are: acute and prophylactic **gout**, **familial Mediterranean fever (FMF)**, and **recurrent pericarditis**, with active research into cardiovascular inflammation and other NLRP3-inflammasome-driven conditions. In the Kenetica system it functions as the exemplar of "the poison that is only a medicine under exact control" — the case that justifies the whole toxicology discipline. It is deployed in the curriculum as a warning, not a protocol.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Unani/Galenic:** Cold and dry; classed as a strong, hazardous, "sharp" drug (*sam*, poison) requiring correction; used cautiously for joint disease.
- **Greek (Dioscoridean):** Explicitly a poison (*deleterion*) — not assigned to the ordinary tonic/warming categories but to the toxic bulbs.
- **Ayurvedic/TCM:** No safe canonical materia-medica place; related *Gloriosa superba* (also colchicine-bearing, Colchicaceae) is treated in Ayurveda as a potent, poisonous *upavisha* drug requiring rigorous purification — the same toxicological logic applies.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian (EP).** No secure Egyptian attestation of the autumn-crocus corm is established in the sources retrieved. The Ebers material is indication-organised, and substance identity within it is a modern hypothesis rather than a fixed equation — so even where a bulb-drug might be conjectured, there is no basis for reading a *heka*-bearing intervention or any framing of the plant's action on the *ka*, *ib* or vital constitution. No operative recitation and no deity attribution can be claimed without a source, and none is. **(c) absent.**

**Assyrian (AT).** Under the standing blocking warning in force for every Mesopotamian substance identification, deity/demon attribution and tablet reference, no cuneiform identification of the *Colchicum* corm is asserted here, and no classification of it by causal agency (divine hand, ghost, oath) is claimed. There is accordingly nothing to place on a cultivation ladder. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** This is the tradition's strongest engagement with the plant, but it runs in exactly the opposite direction from cultivation. *De Materia Medica* (Book IV) describes *kolchikon* / *ephemeron*, the autumn crocus, as a poisonous bulb that resembles an edible one and kills by choking, and records milk as a remedy against poisoning by it — one of the clearest toxicological warnings in the Greek corpus. Dioscorides carries no subtle-body doctrine that could assign the plant a vital status in any case; what he offers a cultivator here is his discipline of attention at its most literal and life-saving — the identification of a lethal corm against the edible bulbs it mimics, a matter of provenance and sensory assay rather than of vitality. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status; the attestation is a warning, not a status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The autumn-crocus corm (*surinjan*, a name covering related colchicaceous drugs) was known as a potent, hazardous drug — classed as *sam* (poison) requiring correction and processing — used cautiously for joint pain and gout. The conservation of innate heat and radical moisture, the corpus's central longevity idea, is a frame of regimen and belongs nowhere near this sharp evacuant poison; no cultivation status is assigned to it, only a dangerous therapeutic one hedged by explicit awareness of its toxicity. **(b) framed:** attested therapeutically and toxicologically, no cultivation status sourced.

**Ayurveda (SS).** *Colchicum turcicum* itself has no canonical place in the Ayurvedic materia medica; the *upavisha* (potent-poison) standing and the demand for rigorous *shodhana* purification are attested for the related Colchicaceae plant *Gloriosa superba* (*langali*/*kalihari*) — a different species, not this one. For the autumn crocus proper, no *rasayana* or *vajikarana* assignment and no *ojas*-related action are recorded, and none is imported here from its poisonous cousin. **(c) absent** for this substance; the purification logic that exists belongs to a related plant, not to it.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this plant — that is the whole and honest answer. No tradition on this tag line assigns *Colchicum* a cultivation status; the tradition that engages it most directly, the Greek, records it precisely as a poison to be identified and avoided. Its only legitimate use is purified pharmaceutical colchicine under a physician, never plant material, and its place in the curriculum is as the boundary marker where a plant's power becomes lethal. The correct cultivation practice is total abstention.

### Contraindications

**⚠️ THIS IS THE MOST HEAVILY FLAGGED CLASS OF CONTRAINDICATION IN THE CODEX. READ IN FULL.**

- **Lethal in overdose with NO ANTIDOTE.** Colchicine poisoning progresses through a GI phase (severe nausea, vomiting, profuse diarrhoea, abdominal pain — often within hours), then a life-threatening multi-organ phase: **bone-marrow suppression (pancytopenia), cardiovascular collapse, respiratory failure, hepatic and renal failure, rhabdomyolysis, and death**. Management is supportive only; there is no reliably available specific antidote.
- **Never ingest any part of the plant.** Corms, seeds, flowers, and leaves are all toxic. The corm is sometimes mistaken for wild garlic/ramsons or an edible bulb — this misidentification has caused deaths.
- **Absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy** — colchicine is antimitotic and crosses the placenta; and in **breastfeeding** without specialist guidance.
- **Contraindicated / requires major dose reduction in renal impairment and hepatic impairment** — colchicine is cleared renally and hepatically, and impairment sharply raises toxicity risk.
- **Dangerous drug interactions:** colchicine is a substrate of **CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein**. Co-administration with **strong CYP3A4 inhibitors** (e.g. clarithromycin, ketoconazole, certain antivirals) or **P-gp inhibitors** (e.g. cyclosporine) can cause fatal colchicine accumulation. Additive myotoxicity with **statins/fibrates** (rhabdomyolysis risk).
- **Keep away from children and animals.** Even small ingestions can be fatal.
- If poisoning is suspected, this is a **medical emergency** — contact emergency services / poison control immediately.

This plant is documented in the Codex for identification, hazard-awareness, and its role as the source of a controlled drug. It has no place as a self-administered remedy.
