# Deer blood — Stag's Blood (Bryan's Ebers) / sha.wahi.khrag, "Deer blood" (Clark 1995, Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *Blood of the deer; Bryan prints "Stag's Blood" and Clark glosses the Tibetan deer sha.wa as* Cervus elaphus *(red deer)* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP, FMT

### Origin & Tradition

Deer antler, the best-known deer product in the Codex, has its own entry (`I_154_Lu_Rong_Cervus_nippon.md`); this entry is the blood only.

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The English text on disk is C. P. Bryan's 1930 *The Papyrus Ebers*, a translation with summaries; the Egyptian words are not printed. The list row is `_sources/ebers.csv` line 87, "Stag's blood", *Cervus elaphus*, source field "Ebers; Wikipedia/Ebers Papyrus", and the row marks the identification uncertain; the second source was not relied on. The text locus is the eye section, under the heading "To prevent the hair growing into the eye after it has been pulled out" (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` lines 2879-2894): "Incense-ground-in-Lizard's-dung, Cow's Blood, Ass's Blood, Pig's Blood, Dog's Blood, Stag's Blood, Collyrium, Incense: Crush, rub into one in the different kinds of Blood, and place on the part whence the Hair has been pulled out so that it may not grow again." The stag's blood is thus one of five bloods in a single compound, and no remedy using it alone was found. The same compound is the Ebers locus for the pig's blood (`III_23_Pig_blood.md`); the dog is treated in `III_33_Dog.md`. Bryan's introduction lists the stag among the animals drawn on for remedies (line 1398) and blood among the products taken from them (line 1415). Bryan reports "Words to be spoken" over verdigris in the same eye section (about line 2839) but none with this compound, and since he summarises, that is no proof the papyrus gives none. No deity is attributed.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The Explanatory Tantra ch.20 lists deer blood under "Blood", the fourth of thirteen kinds of animal medicine. Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 1172): "sha.wahi.khrag: Deer blood: is anthelmintic, halts bleeding from the womb [and prevents hangovers.]" The brackets are Clark's marker for interpolations from the *Blue Aquamarine* and *Spreading Tree* commentaries (his note, line 82), so the hangover use comes from the commentary. Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 3539): "Deer blood cures ein disorders and stops menstrual bleeding." The two translations render the first action differently (Clark "anthelmintic", Men-Tsee-Khang *ein* disorders), and I did not check the Tibetan. The deer is identified by Clark as "sha.wa: Tibetan Deer: Cervus elaphus: [has three names]" (line 1154). Its neighbours show a pattern: deer fat is "anthelmintic and protects against poisoning" in Clark (line 1180) and "cures ein disorders and protects from poisoning" in Men-Tsee-Khang (line 3546), so deer blood and deer fat share the first action in both translations. Deer meat is a game meat in the dietary lines: "game meat (e.g., deer)" is among the foods for bile disorders (Clark line 175), and the deer heads the herbivorous wild animals (Men-Tsee-Khang line 2907). The spine's list row is `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 357, "Deer blood", marked identification uncertain.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM) is not on this entry's tag line but has deer blood.** *De Materia Medica* 2-97 *PERI HAIMATON* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 5363, the edition's heading "Blood — CAUTION"): "Blood from a he-goat, she-goat, deer or hare (fried in a pan and eaten) stop dysentery and discharges of the abdominal cavity. Taken in a drink with wine it is an antidote against poison." No DMM paragraph is written here because the tag line does not carry it. ### Active Components

Blood is a fluid tissue of cells (red cells, white cells, platelets) in plasma. In general, red cells carry haemoglobin, so blood is a source of iron and protein; plasma carries albumin, globulins, clotting factors, lipids, hormones and electrolytes. The texts name no active component. No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources record deer blood as one of five bloods in an Egyptian compound for a plucked eyelash, and, in the Tibetan text, as a medicine against parasites or *ein* disorders and uterine bleeding. The Codex has no protocol for either and offers none: raw animal blood is an infection risk and wild deer carry specific diseases (below); the sources give no dose; the Egyptian use is at the eyelid; uterine bleeding is a medical matter; and the Tibetan hangover use is the commentators' addition. Deer meat as an ordinary game food is outside this entry's sources beyond the dietary lines cited.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation given is the Egyptian one, as Bryan renders it: the five bloods with collyrium and incense (the latter ground in lizard's dung), "rub into one in the different kinds of Blood", placed on the part where the hair had been pulled out. The Greek line cited above fries the blood in a pan and eats it. The Tibetan texts give the action only, with no method in the lines read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Raw blood from wild or farmed deer can carry bacteria (*Brucella*, *Leptospira*, *Salmonella*), parasites and viruses, and cervids are the host of chronic wasting disease, a prion disease of deer and elk reported in North America, Scandinavia and South Korea; there is no treatment and no safe-level test for people to rely on. Cooked blood is a source of haem iron. No effect of deer blood on parasites, uterine bleeding, poisoning or the regrowth of plucked eyelashes is established. The Tibetan *ein* disorders are a disease concept of that tradition and are not translated into a biomedical condition here. No clinical studies of the traditional uses were retrieved for this entry. ### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* The only locus for the stag's blood is an eyelash remedy, one of five bloods ground with incense and collyrium. It records what was done for a complaint and does not say what the blood does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); Bryan reports no recitation with this compound, and since he summarises, that is not proof the papyrus gives none. No deity is attributed. Because Ebers identifications are modern hypotheses, "Stag's Blood" is Bryan's English rendering, and the spine row (`_sources/ebers.csv` line 87) rests on a list citing Wikipedia as well as the papyrus. **Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Deer blood is one of the blood medicines in the Explanatory Tantra's animal list, given for parasites or *ein* disorders and for uterine bleeding (commentary adds hangover). The texts read place deer blood in the same list as deer fat and the deer's horn (cross-reference above), but assign no cultivation role to it. No essence-extraction (*bcud len*) use is attested in the lines read, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition; none is asserted. Whatever the antler entry records for the horn (Clark line 1154 says the horns of the deer have an action similar to rhino horn) is not carried over to the blood.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. In Ebers the stag's blood is one of five bloods in an eyelash compound, and in the Tibetan text it is a drug for a complaint. The antler entry is where the Codex's deer material for cultivators sits, and what it says is not transferable to the blood. This entry gives no reason to take deer blood.

### Contraindications

- **Infection and prion disease.** Raw deer blood and offal can carry bacterial, parasitic and viral infections, and chronic wasting disease occurs in deer and elk in several countries; never drink raw blood or apply it to the eye or broken skin. [USER: verify specifics]
- **Uterine bleeding.** Heavy or unexplained bleeding needs a clinician.
- **Eye.** The Egyptian use is at the eyelid margin; an unsterile product there risks infection of the eye.
- **Legal and conservation.** Many deer species are game-regulated, and trade in deer parts may be restricted by hunting and wildlife law; check local rules.
- **Dose unknown.** No dose is given in the sources read.
