# Butterbur / Petasites

## *Petasites hybridus* (L.) G.Gaertn., B.Mey. & Scherb. | Family: Asteraceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Warm–Dry, resolvent/discutient (inferred from classical wound-and-swelling use; not a codified humoral entry in the surviving texts)
**Training Phase:** Foundation — Signature Study (with an Advanced caution: this is an *extract-engineering* entry, not a whole-herb entry)

---

### Origin & Tradition

Butterbur is a stout, rhizomatous perennial of wet ground — riverbanks, ditches, damp meadows — across Europe and northern/western Asia. It flowers before it leafs: pinkish spikes push up in early spring, and only afterward come the enormous rhubarb-like leaves, among the largest of any European herb, historically used to wrap butter in warm weather (hence the English name). That giant leaf is also the source of the genus name: Greek *petasos*, the broad-brimmed felt hat of travellers and of Hermes. The plant is, quite literally, filed under its own signature — the hat-leaf.

**DMM (Dioscorides / Greek herbal stream):** The Greek herbal tradition records a *petasites* — a broad-leaved marsh plant — applied externally to foul and spreading ulcers and to wounds. The attestation is for the **crude leaf as a topical resolvent**, not for anything resembling the modern oral extract. Note a genuine caution: the identity of Dioscorides' *petasites* with our *P. hybridus* is probable from the leaf description but not certain across the manuscript tradition. **[USER: verify]** against a critical edition of *De Materia Medica* before asserting a hard identification — the marsh-butterbur/coltsfoot (*Tussilago*) cluster is easy to conflate.

**CM (Avicenna / Canon of Medicine, Unani):** The broad-leaved butterbur enters the later materia medica as a warming, drying, discutient herb — used to disperse swellings and cold, boggy accumulations, consistent with its damp-ground habitat being "corrected" by a warm-dry remedy. Treat the specific Canon citation as **[USER: verify]**: the Arabic-Latin nomenclature for the *petasites/tussilago* group is unstable, and I have not confirmed a chapter-and-line attribution this run.

The doctrinal reading is coherent: a plant that throws up the widest shade-leaf in the flora, rooted in standing damp, read as a remedy that *spreads, covers, and disperses* — resolving stagnation the way its leaf spreads over the ground. Kenetica keeps that signature. What Kenetica refuses to do is let the signature vouch for the pharmacology, because the modern evidence belongs to a substance the ancients never handled.

### Active Components

- **Sesquiterpenes — petasin, isopetasin, neopetasin** (eremophilane-type esters). These are the accepted active fraction of the leaf extract, with documented leukotriene- and STAT-pathway-modulating activity. "Standardised to petasins" is the marker used in the allergy extract Ze339.
- **Pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs)** — the crux of this entry. Butterbur naturally contains unsaturated PAs (senecionine-type; the genus also carries petasitenine/neopetasitenine in some species). Unsaturated PAs are **hepatotoxic and genotoxic/carcinogenic**. They are the reason the raw plant, tea, or home tincture is **not** a safe medicine.
- Volatile oil, mucilage, tannins, flavonoids (minor).

The whole point of the pharmaceutical story is the **separation** of the first group from the second: modern preparations are PA-*depleted* CO₂ extracts (e.g. Petadolex/Petaforce root extract; Ze339 leaf extract), manufactured specifically to strip the alkaloids while retaining the petasins.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Butterbur is positioned in the practitioner's path as the **canonical lesson in "the plant is not the drug."** Two honest, human-trial-supported uses exist, and both belong to *engineered, PA-free standardised extracts* — not to anything a cultivator can prepare from the plant in front of them:

1. **Migraine prophylaxis** (root extract, e.g. Petadolex 75 mg twice daily).
2. **Allergic rhinitis** (leaf extract Ze339, standardised to petasins).

Honest deflation, stated plainly: the crude plant and any home preparation (tea, tincture, poultice taken internally) carry a real liver-cancer and liver-failure hazard and have **no** validated therapeutic dose. The classical DMM/CM uses were *topical and external* on a plant whose systemic toxicity was invisible to pre-modern practice. The triumph here is industrial chemistry, not herbcraft. A student who "grows their own butterbur for migraines" has learned the wrong lesson from this page.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Form | Traditional / historical handling | Modern evidence-based use | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh leaf, topical | Bruised leaf applied to spreading ulcers/wounds (DMM) | — | External only in the classical record |
| Tea / home tincture (internal) | (Folk) | **Do not use** | Unquantified pyrrolizidine alkaloid exposure — hepatotoxic/carcinogenic |
| PA-depleted root extract (Petadolex-type) | n/a — modern | Migraine RCTs used **75 mg twice daily** (150 mg/day); 50 mg twice daily was **not** significantly better than placebo | Only PA-certified extracts; liver monitoring advised |
| PA-depleted leaf extract (Ze339) | n/a — modern | Allergic-rhinitis trials standardised to **~8 mg petasins**, dosed 2–3× daily for 1–2 weeks | Dose-dependent effect shown |

**Safety caveat:** there is no legitimate "cultivator's dose" of the raw herb. Any internal preparation not certified PA-free is outside safe practice. Standardised-dose figures above are drawn from the cited trials, not extrapolated.

### Scientific Correlation

**Migraine prophylaxis — GRADE: moderate (positive), extract-specific.**
- Lipton 2004 (Neurology, n=245, three-arm RCT): PA-depleted root extract **75 mg bid reduced attack frequency by 48% vs 26% placebo (p=0.0012)**; responder rate 68% vs 49%. The **50 mg bid dose was not significantly better than placebo** — dose matters.
- Diener 2004 (independent reanalysis of the earlier Petadolex RCT, n=60): responder rate 45% verum vs 15% placebo; the authors explicitly flag major shortcomings in the *original* protocol/analysis.
- Agosti 2006 systematic review: only "moderate evidence," at a higher-than-label dose, and calls for long-term safety confirmation before routine recommendation.
- Guideline note: butterbur was once endorsed for migraine prevention in a major neurology guideline (AAN, 2012) and that endorsement was subsequently **withdrawn over hepatotoxicity concerns**. I could not retrieve the primary guideline-withdrawal document via PubMed this run — treat the 2012→2015 reversal as **[USER: verify]** against the American Academy of Neurology's own record before printing it as fact.

**Allergic rhinitis — GRADE: moderate (positive), extract-specific (Ze339).**
- Schapowal 2004 (Arch Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg, n=186, RCT): Ze339 superior to placebo for intermittent allergic rhinitis with a **dose-response** relationship; adverse-event profile indistinguishable from placebo.
- Thomet 2002 (Int Immunopharmacol): Ze339 reduced nasal histamine and leukotriene levels in allergic-rhinitis patients — mechanistic support.
- Steiert 2017 (BioFactors): petasin/Ze339 anti-inflammatory action via inhibition of STAT signalling in human nasal epithelial cells — preclinical mechanism.

**Pyrrolizidine-alkaloid hazard — GRADE: well-established (toxicological).**
- Yanagi 2021 (J Toxicol Sci): PBPK modelling of the *Petasites* PA neopetasitenine and its carcinogenic deacetylated metabolite petasitenine; concludes that daily dietary consumption of the PA-producing plant could keep dangerous plasma levels of the carcinogen continuously present. (This is a *P. japonicus*/congener study — see caution below — but it is the mechanistic spine of the "raw plant is unsafe" position.)

**Congener caution:** the Yanagi 2021 toxicokinetic data are on ***Petasites japonicus*** (Japanese fuki), a related species, not on *P. hybridus*. It is cited here as **congener/genus-level toxicology** demonstrating the class hazard of *Petasites* pyrrolizidine alkaloids — not as a species-specific measurement in *P. hybridus*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Read as **warm and dry, resolvent/discutient** — a corrector of cold, damp, boggy stagnation, mirroring its wet habitat and broad dispersing leaf. This reading is inferred from the external wound/swelling use and the plant's signature; it is not a securely codified Hot/Cold degree in the surviving DMM or CM text, so it is offered as interpretation, not attribution.

### Contraindications

- **Raw plant, tea, and any non-PA-certified preparation: do not ingest.** Unsaturated pyrrolizidine alkaloids are hepatotoxic and carcinogenic; hepatic veno-occlusive disease and liver cancer are the class risks.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding: avoid entirely** (PA transfer and genotoxicity risk); no safe dose established.
- **Pre-existing liver disease: avoid**, even PA-free extracts, without medical supervision and liver-function monitoring.
- **Asteraceae/Compositae allergy** (ragweed, chrysanthemum, marigold, daisy): risk of cross-reactive hypersensitivity.
- Even PA-depleted extracts have been associated with rare hepatic injury reports; monitor liver enzymes on prolonged use and use only pharmaceutical-grade, PA-certified products.
- Not a self-prepared herb: the safe forms cannot be produced by a home cultivator.
