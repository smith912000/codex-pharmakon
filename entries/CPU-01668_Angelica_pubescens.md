# Angelica pubescens — Du Huo 獨活 (root), printed "Du Buo" (Shennong Bencao Jing)

## *Angelica pubescens, as the pharmaceutical Latin "Radix Angelicae Pubescentis" that Yang 1998 prints for the entry whose name the text file gives as "Du Buo"; the entry's first other name is "Qiang Huo", which Yang's own note on another entry uses for a different plant* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Shennong text (SBJ).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The entry is in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`). The Latin name is the translator's pharmaceutical Latin and was matched to the binomial by stem in the extraction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, row 64). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The text file prints the name as "Du Buo". The Codex's own list of Shennong names (`_sources/shennong.csv`, line 39) prints "Angelica pubescens Root (獨活 Dú Huó)", so the Codex reads "Buo" as an OCR form of "Huo". The characters 獨活 come from that list and not from Yang's English text. [USER: verify]

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *Du Buo (Du Huo), PDF page 46.* The page marker `[[p46]]` is at line 1774 and the entry runs from line 1779 to line 1786. The heading "Herbs: Superior Class" is printed at line 1775, the top of the same page, and the next heading, "Herbs: Superior Class" again, is at line 1856 on `[[p48]]`. The entry therefore stands under the superior-class heading, four lines below it on the same page with no page break between, and with the last lines of the preceding entry (Xi Xin, lines 1776 to 1778) in between. The headings in this text are repeated at the top of pages, and no heading is printed for this entry alone. The text reads (lines 1779 to 1786, with the OCR "takin~" read as "taking"):

"Du Buo (Radix Angelicae Pubescentis) is bitter and balanced. It is nontoxic, treating mainly assaulting wind cold and incised wounds. It relieves pain, running piglet, epilepsy, and tetany, and, in females, mounting conglomeration. Protracted taking may make the body light and slow aging. Its other name is Qiang Huo (Qiang Activator).100 Yet another name is Qiang Qing (Qiang Green-blue). It is also called Hu Qiang Shi Zhe (Protecting the Qiang Envoy). It grows in rivers and valleys."

Yang's note 100 (line 1793) is attached to "Qiang Huo" and reads in full: "The Qiang were a tribe of nomads living in northwest China in olden times." It is the translator's note. The entry has no other note.

*A name that points to another plant.* The other name "Qiang Huo" is not unique to this entry in Yang's own text. In the translator's note 145, on Mi Wu (lines 2405 to 2418), Yang writes "Qiang Huo (Radix Et Rhizoma Notopterygii)" at lines 2411 to 2412, in a list of channel ushers for headache. That is the pharmaceutical Latin of *Notopterygium*, which is the subject of I_313. It is a modern note on a different entry and not a statement of the classic. The Codex keeps the two apart and does not decide whether the classic's "Qiang Huo" was one plant or the other. [USER: verify]

*The class heading.* Yang's table of contents prints "Herbs: Superior Class" (line 711). The preface (lines 352 to 355 and 383 to 384) says: "There are 120 superior class medicinals which are used as sovereigns. They mainly nourish life and correspond to heaven. They are nontoxic and taking them in large amounts and for a long time will not harm people. If one intends to make one's body light, boost the qi, prevent aging, and prolong life, one should base [one's efforts] on the superior class." Yang's commentary (lines 356 to 357) says they "are able to nurture life and therefore bestow longevity". The preface says "prevent aging", where the Du Huo entry says "slow aging", and the Codex does not treat the two as the same phrase.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Angelica pubescens*, *Du Huo* and *Qiang Huo* found no entry on this plant. I_002 and I_003 (*Achyranthes bidentata*) name *Du Huo* (*Angelica pubescens*) as an ingredient of the formula *Du Huo Ji Sheng Wan* in their prose. I_029 (Bay laurel) uses *Du Huo* as a traditional analogy. I_313 (*Notopterygium incisum*) carries the tags SBJ and FMT and names *Du Huo* as the lower-body pair of *Qiang Huo*, in a post-classical account which that entry itself flags. I_010 and I_011 are on *Angelica sinensis* (Dang Gui, a separate Shennong entry at line 2229). None is an entry on this plant. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

General composition, stated without numbers: *Angelica pubescens* is a plant of the carrot family (Apiaceae), and the roots of plants of this family are generally known to contain coumarins, including furanocoumarins. This is a general statement about the family. The composition of the root as the Shennong text meant it was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Shennong text lists the entry's actions in its own terms (assaulting wind cold, incised wounds, relieving pain, running piglet, epilepsy, tetany, mounting conglomeration in females, and, with protracted taking, making the body light and slowing aging). These are not modern diagnoses, and they are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The entry gives no preparation. It says "Protracted taking may make the body light and slow aging", without a quantity, frequency or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Shennong terms (assaulting wind cold, running piglet, tetany, mounting conglomeration) are not modern diagnoses or measured outcomes, so no modern finding can be set beside them here. It is a general, well-established fact that furanocoumarins can make the skin more sensitive to sunlight, but no source read for this entry says that of this plant.

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: the superior class (上品), by the page-top heading.* The class is assigned by the printed heading "Herbs: Superior Class" (line 1775, `[[p46]]`), which is a repeated page-top heading and not a heading printed for this entry alone; the entry stands four lines below it on the same page, and the next such heading is at line 1856. In the preface (lines 352 to 355, 383 to 384) the superior class "mainly nourish[es] life", is nontoxic, and is the class on which one bases the effort "to make one's body light, boost the qi, prevent aging, and prolong life". The entry itself says: "It is nontoxic" and "Protracted taking may make the body light and slow aging."

The phrase "make the body light" is *qing shen* 輕身, which the cultivation spec says has no pharmacological equivalent and must not be translated into one. Yang's own note on that phrase (lines 373 to 378, in a stretch where the OCR order is scrambled) ties such phrases to Daoist writing on elixirs; it is the translator's commentary and not the classic's. The entry asserts no *jing*, *shen* or *neidan* action, and none is added here. The status is the status of the entry, on the translator's identification.

**Practitioner's note.** A superior-class entry in the Chinese scheme, whose class the preface calls nontoxic and for long taking. The entry itself carries the long-taking sentence, with no preparation, no quantity and no duration. The grade is the classic's; the plant's identity rests on a translator's Latin name and one of its other names is used elsewhere in the same book for another plant, and the Codex has no dose and no clinical record to set beside it. Nothing here supports taking a root on the strength of this entry. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The Shennong entry says "It is nontoxic" and prints no warning. The preface calls the superior class "nontoxic" and says that taking its members "in large amounts and for a long time will not harm people". Those are statements of the text about the class and the entry and not safety findings. The identity rests on a translator's Latin name, and the name Qiang Huo is used for another plant in the translator's own note, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Plants of the carrot family that contain furanocoumarins can sensitise skin to sunlight, a general fact about the family and not a finding about this plant. Epilepsy, tetany, wounds and pain need medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
