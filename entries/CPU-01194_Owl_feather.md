# Owl feather — hug.pa, as printed in Clark's translation of the rGyud-bzhi
## *The feather of the owl, a nocturnal bird of prey; named in the Tibetan materia medica of animal origin* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan Tradition (FMT).** The text is Barry Clark's 1995 translation of the rGyud-bzhi, file `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`. The locus is the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (heading at line 792), section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147). Clark's opening line for the section (1148) says "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body." The owl falls under "Hair" (line 1192), which in this list includes feathers. The line (1195) reads: "hug.pa: Owl: [has fifteen names]. Its feather cures oedema." The same line continues with the fruit bat ("Its [tail] feathers expel womb disorders"), the burrhel sheep and the billy-goat's pubic hair.

The names.csv row (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, cited as "Clark 1995 (line 1195)") and `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 371 ("Owl feather", animal, id_uncertain "yes") point to this same line. No species or binomial is printed for the owl: Clark gives only "Owl". In his preface (line 83) he says that where the identity of an animal product is in doubt the Tibetan name is given with a "probable English identification". The owl species is therefore unspecified. The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3558) has the parallel sentence: "Owl feather cures ascites." The two translations render the indication differently (oedema; ascites) and I do not reconcile them. [USER: verify] That edition's list of animal groups puts the owl among the birds that "survive by their hunting skills" (line 2914). Clark's meat classification (line 672) likewise lists the owl in section "(f) Birds of prey", with the vulture, red vulture, harrier hawk, crow and sparrowhawk. That classification concerns the flesh as food and is not applied to the feather.

### Active Components

A feather is built of keratin, a structural protein; it carries no known medicinal compound. As a general fact, a feather from a wild bird can carry mites, lice, fungal spores and bacteria.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The Tibetan text says owl feather "cures oedema". The Codex does not recommend using it for that or anything else, and gives no protocol. Oedema (swelling from fluid) can signal heart, kidney, liver or vein disease and needs diagnosis. Owls are protected in many countries, and feathers may not lawfully be collected or traded (see Contraindications). The line is kept as a record. Clark notes (line 57): "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts."

### Preparation & Dosing

The line gives no preparation (burnt, ground, worn, swallowed) and no dose. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Tibetan: the line prints an action only ("cures oedema"), with no taste, power or post-digestive taste. None is given for the feather in the sources retrieved. No other tradition is tagged.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* The owl feather appears once in the animal-origin materia medica, in the "Hair" group (line 1195), with a therapeutic action only. No cultivation status, no essence-extraction (*bcud len*) role and no rejuvenation role is printed for it in the lines read. The adjacent peacock-feather line mentions *nagas* (line 1193); the owl line mentions no spirit and no protective use, and none is supplied here.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. A cultivator does not use owl feather. The text records it against oedema, and the Codex keeps the record without carrying it into practice.

### Contraindications

Owls are legally protected in many jurisdictions, and possessing or trading their feathers can be an offence; check the law of the jurisdiction before holding any owl material. [USER: verify] Wild feathers can carry parasites and microbes. Oedema needs medical assessment, since it can mark serious organ disease. This entry gives no medical advice.
