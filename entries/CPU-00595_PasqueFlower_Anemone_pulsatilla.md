# Pasque Flower / Meadow Anemone

## *Pulsatilla vulgaris* Mill. (syn. *Anemone pulsatilla* L.) | Family: Ranunculaceae

**Tradition Tags:** EF
**Tags (previous line, retained for reference):** DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Cool, dry, dispersing; sedative-relaxant with a corrosive fresh aspect that the drying process removes

**Training Phase:** Restoration / Nervine Settling — never Acute Intake

---

### Origin & Tradition

The Pasque Flower opens on chalk grassland and dry limestone banks in the short window around Easter — hence *Pasque*, from the Old French *pasque*, Easter. It is one of the few plants in this Codex whose common name is a calendar entry. The nodding, violet, silk-haired bell appears before the leaves fully expand; the seed heads that follow are long-plumed and windblown, giving the genus name *Pulsatilla* — "that which is beaten" or "shaken," from the wind's constant working of the flower on its stalk. It is a plant of exposure: it grows where the soil is thin, the drainage sharp, and the wind unbroken.

The tradition around it is unusually consistent on one point, which is what makes this entry worth writing carefully. Nearly every attested use insists on the **dried** herb. That insistence is not fastidiousness or ritual — it is chemically load-bearing, and it is the spine of this entry.

**Western Herbal / Dispensatory Tradition (DMM)** — Known in nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century Anglo-American materia medica as *Pulsatilla*, with the dried aerial herb collected at flowering as the officinal part. The classical indication cluster is threefold and quite specific: (1) **eye complaints** — described as catarrhal or "rheumatic" ophthalmia, styes, and inflamed lids, given internally rather than as a wash; (2) **nervous and depressive states**, particularly those the texts characterised as tearful, changeable, and worse in warm close rooms; and (3) **menstrual and reproductive complaints** — delayed, scanty, or painful menses, and spasmodic dysmenorrhoea. Dispensatory-era doses were small and the texts are near-unanimous that the fresh plant is unusable as an internal medicine because it is acrid and blistering. That negative instruction is more reliably attested than any of the positive indications.

**Homeopathic Tradition (DMM, overlapping)** — *Pulsatilla* is one of the most heavily prescribed polychrests in the homeopathic materia medica, and its "constitutional picture" — mild, weepy, thirstless, craving open air, symptoms shifting location — is among the most elaborated in that literature. This is a genuine historical attestation and the reason the plant survived into modern commerce at all. It is not evidence of pharmacological effect: homeopathic preparations of *Pulsatilla* in common use are diluted past the point where the plant's chemistry is present in any meaningful quantity, so whatever the homeopathic tradition records, it cannot be recording the actions of ranunculin, anemonin, or the saponins. The two literatures must be read as separate claims about separate things, and this Codex treats the *material* dried-herb tradition and the *homeopathic* tradition as distinct lines that happen to share a plant name.

**Chinese Medicine (CM) — attribution caution.** *(⚠️ code-gloss correction 2026-08-10: in this project **CM denotes Avicenna / Unani**, not "Chinese Medicine" — the label below is legacy drift from an old scheme in which CM meant "Chinese Materia Medica". The Chinese content here is genuine; whether the **CM tag itself** belongs on this entry is under audit and has NOT been ruled.)* The Chinese drug *Bai Tou Weng* (白头翁) is *Pulsatilla chinensis* (Bunge) Regel — a different species in the same genus, used as the root, for damp-heat dysentery and intestinal complaints. It is a *congener, not this plant*. Most of the modern pharmacological literature indexed under "Pulsatilla" — the anemoside saponin work in particular — is *P. chinensis* root, and importing it into a *P. vulgaris* entry would be a category error. It is flagged wherever it appears below. Whether *P. vulgaris* itself carries an independent Chinese attestation is doubtful — **[USER: verify]**.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

The doctrine attached to Pasque Flower reads, at first, as pure signature-thinking: a flower that bows its head, weeps silk hairs, and trembles in the wind, prescribed for the patient who is tearful, yielding, and changeable. The seed-plume disperses on the least breath; the medicine is given for states that will not hold still. This is the classic sympathetic logic of the doctrine of signatures and it should be deflated as *explanation* — the plant does not calm weeping because it looks like weeping.

But underneath the signature sits one doctrinal instruction that is not decorative at all, and that maps onto real chemistry with a precision almost nothing else in this Volume matches. **The tradition forbids the fresh plant and requires the dried.** Fresh *Pulsatilla* tissue, on crushing, converts its stored glycoside **ranunculin** — via a released β-glucosidase — into **protoanemonin**, a volatile, highly reactive vesicant lactone that blisters skin and mucosa. On drying, protoanemonin dimerises to **anemonin**, which is stable and non-vesicant. The old instruction "use only the dried herb, gathered and dried carefully" is therefore not a preservation convenience. It is a *chemical conversion step* — an unwitting synthesis, encoded as a rule. The tradition detoxified its own drug and wrote the procedure down without knowing what it had done.

That is the real doctrine here, and it survives contact with the chemistry. The signature does not.

### Active Components

According to PubMed, the ranunculin → protoanemonin → anemonin cascade is the defining chemistry of the Ranunculaceae and is well characterised. A 2025 review in *Current Medicinal Chemistry* (Sirak, Aragaw & Tadesse) sets it out directly: ranunculin is a glucoside serving as a chemotaxonomic marker in Ranunculaceae; when the plants are damaged, β-glucosidase triggers hydrolysis of ranunculin into protoanemonin; protoanemonin then undergoes cyclodimerisation to form anemonin. The review notes that the instability of ranunculin and the rapid dimerisation of protoanemonin make both unsuitable for biological assay, and that anemonin is the molecule actually usable in bioassays — where it shows anti-inflammatory, anti-infective and antioxidant activity ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.2174/0109298673294080240221115830)).

Read that again as a cultivator and preparer: the toxic species is the *intermediate*. It exists only in the window between tissue damage and completed drying. Every rule the tradition attaches to this plant is a rule about that window.

- **Ranunculin** — the stored, inert glucoside precursor. Present in intact living tissue. Harmless until the cell wall is broken.
- **⚠️ PROTOANEMONIN** — the vesicant. Formed enzymatically on crushing, bruising, or chewing of fresh tissue. Unstable, volatile, intensely reactive. This is the entire toxicity of the plant. **FLAGGED.**
- **Anemonin** — the dimer. The end-point of drying. Stable, non-vesicant, and the only member of the cascade with a coherent pharmacology.
- **Triterpenoid saponins** — present in the genus. The named anemosides (A3 and relatives) are characterised from ***P. chinensis*** root, **not** from *P. vulgaris*, and no claim about them is made for this plant here.
- Minor flavonoids, tannins. Data on *P. vulgaris* specifically is sparse and mostly old.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Pasque Flower sits in this system as a **settling agent for the over-mobile state** — the practitioner whose attention will not stay put, whose affect swings within a single session, who is worse in heat and closed rooms and better walking outdoors in cool air. That symptom-picture is lifted directly from the dispensatory and homeopathic literatures, and it is offered here as a *selection heuristic*, not as a validated pharmacological indication. It tells you when the old texts would have reached for this plant. It does not tell you the plant works.

The honest position: the dried herb's plausible active is anemonin, and anemonin's demonstrated activity is anti-inflammatory, not nervine. There is no retrieved evidence connecting *P. vulgaris* to sedation, mood, menstrual function, or ophthalmia in any modern controlled setting. The classical indication cluster is **unvalidated**.

Its real place in a Kenetica curriculum is closer to **pedagogical than therapeutic**. This is the plant that teaches processing. A student who understands why Pasque Flower must be dried understands, in one clean example, that traditional preparation instructions can encode chemistry — and that ignoring them is not conservatism-shedding but poisoning. Volume I contains no better single demonstration. (See the Jack-in-the-Pulpit entry for the same lesson at greater scale and greater risk.)

**Cultivator's note.** *P. vulgaris* is a plant of thin, sharply drained, alkaline soil in full sun — chalk, limestone rubble, gravel. It resents disturbance and rich ground, and it will rot in wet winter clay. Sow fresh seed with the plume attached; germination is erratic. It is slow to establish and long-lived once settled. Wild populations in Britain are scarce and legally protected in places — **do not wild-collect**. Grow it. Harvest the aerial herb at flowering, cut cleanly rather than crushed, and dry it fast in shade with good airflow. Fast, complete drying is the whole job: a half-dried, humid, bruised harvest is where protoanemonin lingers. **Wear gloves cutting fresh material.**

### Scientific Correlation

Graded honestly. The nulls are the majority of this section.

**The processing doctrine — STRONGLY SUPPORTED (Grade A).** This is the one claim that lands cleanly. The ranunculin → protoanemonin → anemonin pathway, the enzymatic trigger on tissue damage, and the dimerisation of the vesicant to the stable dimer are established chemistry, set out in the 2025 *Current Medicinal Chemistry* review ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.2174/0109298673294080240221115830)). The tradition's insistence on dried material is chemically necessary and chemically correct.

**Anti-inflammatory activity of anemonin — SUPPORTED at the molecule level, NOT at the species level (Grade B for the molecule; Grade D as a *P. vulgaris* claim).** According to PubMed, anemonin shows real anti-inflammatory activity in animal and cell models. In a DSS-induced murine ulcerative colitis model, anemonin improved weight loss, colon length and disease activity index, suppressed IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-6, and appeared to act via PKC-θ ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13020-022-00599-3)). In LPS-challenged piglets, dietary anemonin at 100 mg/kg diet increased villus height and transepithelial electrical resistance, raised tight-junction claudin-1, occludin and ZO-1 expression, and reduced TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8 and IL-1β mRNA ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1753425916648223)). These are respectable findings — but they are findings about *anemonin*, a compound distributed across Ranunculaceae and Gramineae, given by injection or in feed at defined doses. **None of this is a study of *Pulsatilla vulgaris*, and none of it validates a cup of Pasque Flower tea.**

**Congener contamination — flagged, not imported.** The most active current *Pulsatilla* research concerns anemoside A3 from ***P. chinensis*** saponins: A3 polarises macrophages toward M1 via TLR4/NF-κB/MAPK and suppresses breast tumour growth and angiogenesis ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.taap.2021.115755)), and inhibits M2-like polarisation to restrain triple-negative breast cancer metastasis, apparently via STAT3 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28041611)). Interesting work. **Wrong species.** It is named here so that no later reader mistakes it for *P. vulgaris* data, which is exactly the error the literature invites.

**Eye complaints — NO EVIDENCE (Grade F).** No retrieved study addresses *Pulsatilla* in ophthalmia, styes, or catarrhal eye conditions. The classical indication is unsupported.

**Nervine / mood / "changeable temperament" — NO EVIDENCE (Grade F).** No retrieved study. The symptom-picture is a historical artefact of the homeopathic and dispensatory literatures. It has descriptive charm and no demonstrated pharmacology.

**Menstrual and spasmodic complaints — NO EVIDENCE (Grade F).** No retrieved study of *P. vulgaris* in dysmenorrhoea or menstrual irregularity.

**Homeopathic *Pulsatilla* — mechanistically empty.** At standard homeopathic potencies no plant chemistry remains. Whatever clinical observations that literature records, they cannot be attributed to the compounds described in this entry. Deflated.

**Net:** one A-grade doctrine (dry it, and here is why), one B-grade molecule that this species has never been shown to deliver usefully, and a full column of Fs against every classical indication. The plant's genuine contribution to this Codex is the processing lesson, not the pharmacy.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Cool and dry. Dispersing rather than tonifying — the old readings place it as a relaxant to spasm and a settler of the shifting, tearful, warm-room-worsened state. Affinity: nervous system, reproductive tract, mucous membranes, eyes (per classical claim; unvalidated). In its fresh state the energetics invert entirely — the plant is hot, corrosive, and destructive to the tissues it touches. Drying is what makes it cool. This is one of the few cases where the traditional energetic assignment is *created by the processing* rather than inherent to the plant, which is worth sitting with.

### Contraindications

⚠️ **TOXIC FRESH — PROTOANEMONIN VESICANT.**

- **Never handle, chew, taste, juice, or tincture fresh *Pulsatilla* material.** Crushing intact tissue liberates protoanemonin, which blisters skin and mucosa on contact and produces severe gastrointestinal injury — burning of mouth and throat, vomiting, colic, bloody diarrhoea — on ingestion. Renal irritation is reported for protoanemonin-bearing Ranunculaceae generally.
- **Wear gloves** for harvest and handling of fresh material. Contact dermatitis and blistering from fresh Ranunculaceae sap is well described as a class effect.
- **Incompletely dried or humid-stored herb is not safe herb.** Partial drying leaves the vesicant intermediate present. Dry fast, dry fully, store dry.
- **Pregnancy — contraindicated.** The classical use as a menstrual stimulant is reason enough to exclude it; there is no safety data.
- **Lactation — contraindicated.** No data.
- **Children — contraindicated.** No data; narrow margin.
- **Ranunculaceae sensitivity** — cross-reaction with buttercups, clematis, and related genera should be assumed.
- **Do not wild-collect.** Populations are scarce and protected in parts of the range.
- The historical low doses given above are **[USER: verify]** and are recorded for scholarship, not prescription. This plant has no modern pharmacopoeial dose standard because no modern body has judged it worth setting one.
