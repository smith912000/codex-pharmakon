### Botanical Identity
Perennial dioecious herb 1–2.5m; heavily armed with stinging trichomes containing formic acid, histamine, serotonin, and acetylcholine; native to Europe, Asia, North Africa, North America; found worldwide in disturbed nitrogen-rich soils (indicator species for rich soil and former human habitation). Three pharmacologically distinct drug materials from one plant: **leaf** (anti-inflammatory; anti-allergic — flavonoids, formic acid compounds); **root** (BPH — polysaccharides, lectins affecting SHBG binding); **seed** (adaptogenic — lipids, sterols). Most foods and supplements use leaf; most BPH clinical trials use root.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 De Materia Medica (DMM)

Dioscorides (*Book IV, Chapter 93*):
> *"Akalēphē: warming and drying. The seeds taken in wine provoke lust. The juice of the leaves mixed with myrrh heals wounds and excoriations of the genitals. Applied as poultice it disperses inflammatory tumours. The boiled leaves eaten stop bleeding and diarrhoea. The root rubbed on joints relieves gout."*

The urtication (deliberate stinging) application for rheumatism is described in later classical texts: applying live nettle to arthritic joints was practiced in Roman and Greek medicine.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine (CM)

Ibn Sīnā on *qurrāṣ*:
> *"Hot and dry in the third degree. Promotes urine, dissolves kidney stones, provokes menstruation and sexual desire (the seeds especially). The roots rubbed on the scalp promote hair growth. The leaves cooked with salt and oil are nutritious and warm the body. Relieves pleuritis and chronic cough."*

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (EP)

Nettle-type preparations for hair growth, skin conditions, and inflammatory diseases.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)

Stinging plant preparations in Babylonian texts for skin and inflammatory applications.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Anti-Inflammatory (Leaf)

**Quercetin + caffeic acid**: Inhibit NF-κB activation → reduce TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 production. COX-1 and COX-2 inhibition (not selective — both pathways). MMP (matrix metalloproteinase) inhibition → protects joint cartilage. The Randall 1999 study established that nettle leaf extract inhibits multiple pro-inflammatory mediators simultaneously.

### 5.2 Anti-Allergic (Leaf)

**Quercetin**: Inhibits mast cell degranulation → reduces histamine release → reduces allergic rhinitis symptoms. Specific mechanism: quercetin stabilises mast cell membranes at physiologically relevant concentrations (IC50 ~7µM). Remberg et al. (2004): nettle leaf extract inhibits several key enzymes of inflammatory arachidonic acid cascade + histamine release simultaneously.

**Urtication mechanism**: The sting itself (histamine + formic acid + serotonin + acetylcholine in trichomes) induces localised mast cell degranulation → paradoxically reduces inflammatory mediator availability at arthritic joints (counter-stimulation mechanism). Ancient urtication therapy has a pharmacological basis.

### 5.3 BPH (Root)

**Polysaccharides + UDA lectin + β-sitosterol**: Three synergistic mechanisms:
1. Polysaccharides inhibit SHBG binding to prostatic receptors → reduce androgenic stimulation
2. UDA lectin inhibits prostate cell growth (anti-proliferative via growth factor receptor interaction)
3. β-Sitosterol inhibits 5α-reductase → reduces DHT production → reduces prostatic volume

The triple-mechanism root application explains why nettle root is among the most well-evidenced botanical treatments for BPH.

### 5.4 Nutritive / Haematopoietic

High bioavailable iron + vitamin C (enhances iron absorption) + folate + chlorophyll (structurally similar to haemoglobin). Used as a food (blanched greens) for iron-deficiency anaemia historically.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Allergic rhinitis**: Mittman (1990, *Planta Medica*): Freeze-dried nettle leaf (300mg 2× daily) — 58% of patients preferred it to placebo for allergic rhinitis (subjective). Limited by design but historically significant.

**Arthritis (urtication)**: Randall et al. (1999): Urtication with live nettle for knee osteoarthritis — significantly reduced pain scores vs dead nettle (placebo sting). The sting itself is pharmacologically active.

**BPH**: Koch (1995): Nettle root (459 extract) vs placebo — significant improvement in urinary flow, IPSS scores. Sokeland (2000): Nettle root + saw palmetto combination vs finasteride — equivalent efficacy for BPH (urine flow, symptom score) at 12 months.

**Glucose**: Farzami (2003): Aqueous nettle leaf extract reduces blood glucose in T2DM patients — significant but mechanism incompletely established (insulin secretagogue + sensitiser activity).

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The plant that stings and heals**: Every tradition named nettle for its sting — Greek *akalēphē*, Latin *urtica*, Arabic *qurrāṣ*, English "nettle" from "needle" — yet the sting was pharmacologically instrumental. Urtication (deliberate application of live nettle to arthritic joints) was Roman and medieval medical practice; the counter-irritant mechanism is now established. A plant that heals through its most apparently harmful quality is a paradigm case for why traditional empiricism sometimes reached the right treatment for the wrong reason.

**The poor man's food**: Before nettle was primarily medicine, it was food — a free, abundant, iron-rich green available to the poorest communities in Europe for millennia. Its high nutritional density (protein, iron, calcium, vitamins) made it a survival food in famines and early spring before cultivated greens were available. The medical tradition built on a nutritional foundation: communities eating nettle as food observed its health effects and formalised them as medicine. The pharmacological became inseparable from the nutritional.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

| Form | Dose | Application |
|------|------|-------------|
| Leaf tea (dried) | 4–8g per cup | Anti-inflammatory; allergy; nutritive; diuretic |
| Freeze-dried leaf capsule | 300mg 3× daily | Allergic rhinitis; hay fever |
| Root extract (459 extract) | 300–600mg/day | BPH; urological |
| Root + saw palmetto | Per combination product | BPH combination (evidence-based) |
| Food (blanched fresh nettle) | As vegetable | Nutritive; iron-building; spring tonic |
| Urtication (live nettle) | Apply to joints | Arthritis counter-stimulation (specialist use) |

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Acrid; warm (leaf). Channels: Lung (肺), Spleen (脾), Liver (肝). Dispels wind-damp, reduces oedema, promotes Blood.

**Ayurvedic**: Astringent, slightly bitter; neutral. *Mutrala* (diuretic), *Śoṇitasthāpana* (haemostatic), *Krimighna*, *Vājīkaraṇa* (seeds — aphrodisiac).

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Pregnancy**: Uterotonic (high doses of seed particularly) — caution; culinary leaf amounts safer
- **Antihypertensives**: Additive diuretic/BP lowering — monitor
- **Anticoagulants**: Vitamin K in leaf may antagonise warfarin — monitor INR
- **Diuretics**: Additive fluid/electrolyte loss — monitor
- Fresh uncooked nettle: sting hazard (blanch or dry before consumption)
- Root and leaf have distinct pharmacological profiles — specify form for clinical application
