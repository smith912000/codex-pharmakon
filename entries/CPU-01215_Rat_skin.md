# Rat skin — "Rat and Mouse skin" (Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20); "Rat skin" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *The skin of the rat, which Clark's translation pairs with mouse skin; neither Tibetan text prints a Latin name or a Tibetan name for it in the passages read* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is the Tibetan *Four Tantras*. In Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; "Clark" below) the animal medicines close chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Skin is the eighth of them (heading at line 1183). That section reads in full: "Snake skin: cures leucoderma, glang.shu (a skin disease) [and is beneficial against bhutaspirits]. Rhino hide and Ox hide: cure smallpox. Rat and Mouse skin: drains all types of pus accumulations" (lines 1184-1185). The statement about rat skin stands outside Clark's square brackets, which mark interpolations from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries (line 82), so it is the root-text claim as Clark renders it. Clark prints no Tibetan name before this entry, so the Tibetan term for the rat's skin is not available from the passages read. The spine's other locus is the list row `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 369 ("Rat skin", class animal, identity flagged uncertain), which gives only the name.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`; "MTK" below) has the same section (lines 3554-3555): "Snake skin cures vitiligo and langshu. The hide of the rhinoceros and the ox cure small pox. Rat skin drains out pus." It names the rat alone, where Clark names rat and mouse, and says "drains out pus" where Clark says "drains all types of pus accumulations". Both agree on the action. The same two texts also say that "the droppings of birds and rats drain pus" (MTK line 3568; Clark line 1199, "Bird and Rat droppings: both drain pus [accumulations]"), so the pus action is attached to the rat in two forms, skin and droppings. The Codex has those droppings as a separate material, and they are not treated here.

### Active Components

Skin is mainly collagen and other connective-tissue proteins, with keratin in the outer layer and in the fur, and some fat. The Tibetan texts name no constituent and no mechanism for the action on pus, and this entry supplies none.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources say one thing: rat skin drains pus. They do not say whether the skin is applied outside the body or taken, whether it is raw, dried or burnt, or in what form. A claim for "drawing" pus is a claim about a wound or abscess, and the modern answer to a pus-filled wound is clinical drainage and antibiotics where indicated, not an animal skin. Rats are also a documented source of infection (see Contraindications). Clark adds that "nowadays only very few animal products are used" (line 57). The Codex's application layer has nothing to add, and no protocol is offered.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT).** Clark prints no taste, potency or post-digestive taste for rat skin, and neither does MTK. Clark's preface says that detailed texts such as the *Crystal Orb* record for each substance "which of the six tastes, eight natural potencies, three post-digestive 'tastes,' seventeen secondary qualities and individual uses each possesses" (line 57); those texts were not read. The one attribute the sources give is the action, draining pus. No TCM, Unani or Ayurvedic classification applies, because the entry is not tagged with those traditions.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Rat skin is attested in the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica as one of the skins in the animal-medicines list, with a single wound-and-pus action (Clark lines 1183-1185; MTK lines 3554-3555). Its neighbours in the list are snake skin, for skin disease and (in the bracketed commentary) harm from spirits, and the rhino and ox hides, for smallpox. The tradition has a formal division of rejuvenation, "essence-extraction" (Clark line 128, "the division of essence-extraction (rejuvenation, etc.)"; also line 136). In the animal section (lines 1147-1202) no animal substance is assigned to it, and rat skin is not named in the compounding lists of chapter 21. Clark's own statement of what is rejuvenating is about plants (line 69). In this tradition medicine is one branch of treatment and the root of disease lies in the three poisons; no passage read ties rat skin to that frame. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in the Tibetan sources, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports a cultivation use, and a cultivator has no reason to seek this material. It is a wound remedy in a materia medica that its own modern translator describes as largely out of use for animal products. The honest entry is a record.

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** wild and commensal rats carry pathogens (among them the bacteria behind plague, leptospirosis and salmonellosis, and hantaviruses). Handling rodent skins, droppings or carcasses is a real infection risk; wounds are the worst place to apply an untreated animal material.
- **Wounds and abscesses:** a wound that is draining pus needs medical assessment. Delay in order to use an animal skin is the main harm.
- **Legal and ethical status:** commensal rats are unprotected pests in most places. The sources do not say how the skin was obtained or prepared.
