# I-356 — Brassica rapa · Turnip / Pak Choi / Napa Cabbage

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Brassica rapa* L.  
**Family:** Brassicaceae  
**Common names:** Turnip (EN, root vegetable); pak choi / bok choy (EN, leaf vegetable); napa cabbage / Chinese cabbage (EN, heading leaf); rapini / broccoli rabe (EN, flowering tops); canola / oilseed rape (EN, oil crop); Rübe / Pak Choi (DE); navet (FR); nabo (ES); shalgam [شلجم] (AR/FA); wu jing / bai cai [白菜] / xiao bai cai [小白菜] (TCM)

**Major subspecies (selected):**

| Subspecies | Common form | Primary use |
|-----------|------------|------------|
| *ssp. rapa* | Turnip (globe root) | Root vegetable; traditional European medicine |
| *ssp. chinensis* | Pak choi / bok choy | Leaf vegetable; Southeast Asia, China |
| *ssp. pekinensis* | Napa cabbage / Chinese cabbage | Fermentation (kimchi); East Asia |
| *ssp. nipposinica* | Mizuna / kyona | Japanese salad green |
| *ssp. rapifera* | Purple-top turnip | Root; Eastern European folk medicine |
| *ssp. oleifera* | Canola / oilseed rape | Industrial oil; erucic acid variant |
| *ssp. parachinensis* | Choy sum / flowering pak choi | Flowers + stem eaten; Cantonese |
| *ssp. rapa* (turnip tops) | Cima di rapa / rapini | Southern Italian cuisine; bitter glucosinolate greens |

**Parts used:** Root (turnip varieties); leaves, stems, and flowers (leaf varieties); seeds (oil extraction; condiment); sprouts (glucosinolate-concentrated)  
**Habitat & distribution:** Annual or biennial; temperate climate crop; multiple independent domestications across Europe (turnip) and East Asia (leaf forms); one of the oldest cultivated brassicas; now globally distributed; cool-season preferring

## 3. Convergence Analysis

*Brassica rapa* presents a taxonomic and pharmacological paradox: a single species with dramatically divergent cultivated forms spanning root vegetables (turnip), East Asian leaf vegetables (pak choi, napa), Italian bitter greens (rapini), and an industrial oil crop (canola) — each with distinct pharmacological profiles reflecting profoundly different phytochemical concentrations between above-ground and below-ground tissues.

The core cross-traditional consensus focuses on **digestive-hepatic support** (all traditions) and **respiratory/catarrhal clearing** (TCM and European traditions especially). The TCM leaf forms (bai cai, xiao bai cai) are classified as cooling, liver-clearing foods particularly appropriate in "liver heat" constitutional patterns — used in high volumes as daily dietary medicine rather than acute therapeutic doses.

The European turnip holds historical significance disproportionate to its current status: in pre-potato Northern Europe, turnip was the primary winter root staple, making turnip-based bitters and digestive preparations central to winter health maintenance across Germanic, Scandinavian, and British folk medicine. The potato's 18th-century displacement of turnip as primary root vegetable corresponds to the near-disappearance of European turnip medicine in popular culture despite continuous pharmacological relevance.

The Indian and Arabic *shalgam* (turnip) appears prominently in Unani-Tibb as an aphrodisiac and digestive — Avicenna's Canon describing turnip as "increasing semen and exciting sexual desire" reflects classical humoral theory of warming root vegetables stimulating vital fire.

## 4. Phytochemical Composition

**Glucosinolate profile (highly variable by subspecies):**

| Compound | Primary subspecies | Notes |
|----------|------------------|-------|
| Gluconapin (3-butenyl glucosinolate) | Turnip root (highest); rapini | → 3-butenyl ITC via myrosinase |
| Progoitrin (2-hydroxy-3-butenyl glucosinolate) | Turnip; oilseed rape | → goitrin (thyroid peroxidase inhibitor) — significance at high supplemental doses |
| Glucobrassicanapin (4-pentenyl) | Turnip tops | → 4-pentenyl ITC |
| Sinigrin (2-propenyl) | Lower concentration than black mustard/radish | → AITC |
| Gluconasturtiin | Pak choi, napa | → PEITC |
| Glucobrassicin | All subspecies | → Indole-3-carbinol → DIM |

**Leaf-specific composition (pak choi, napa):**

| Nutrient | Content per 100g raw | Significance |
|---------|---------------------|-------------|
| Calcium | 105 mg (pak choi) | HIGH bioavailability: ~40% vs 5% spinach (no oxalate interference) |
| Vitamin K1 | 45–70 μg | Warfarin monitoring |
| Vitamin C | 45–70 mg | High density per calorie |
| Folate | 66–80 μg | Clinically significant |
| Beta-carotene | 1.2–2.8 mg | Pro-vitamin A |
| Potassium | 250–300 mg | Antihypertensive |

**Oil composition (canola, *ssp. oleifera* low-erucic acid varieties):**
- Oleic acid (MUFA): 55–65%
- Linoleic acid (omega-6): 18–23%
- Alpha-linolenic acid (omega-3): 8–12% (omega-6:omega-3 ≈ 2:1 — best ratio of common cooking oils)
- Erucic acid: <2% (breeding reduced from 40–50% in original rapeseed)

**Historical note on erucic acid:** Original high-erucic-acid rapeseed oil was associated with cardiac lipidosis in rodent feeding studies; Canadian and Swedish breeders developed low-erucic variants in the 1970s. "Canola" = **Can**adian **o**il **l**ow **a**cid; the name was created to distinguish safe from historically problematic varieties.

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Glucosinolate-ITC cascade:**
Same enzymatic principle as broccoli and radish — myrosinase hydrolysis releases ITCs upon cell disruption. Gluconapin → 3-butenyl ITC → NRF2/Keap1 activation → phase II enzyme induction. Progoitrin → goitrin (neither ITC nor glucosinolate) — goitrin specifically inhibits thyroid peroxidase (TPO), reducing T3/T4 synthesis; significant primarily at high therapeutic or supplemental doses, not culinary consumption.

**Calcium bioavailability advantage:**
Pak choi and napa cabbage provide calcium with ~40% absorption efficiency versus ~5% from spinach (oxalate-bound calcium poorly absorbed). This makes Brassica leaf vegetables nutritionally superior calcium sources for individuals avoiding dairy, particularly in East Asian populations for whom these vegetables form daily dietary staples. Low oxalate content is the decisive factor.

**Indole-3-carbinol / DIM pathway:**
Glucobrassicin → I3C → DIM (3,3'-diindolylmethane) in gastric acid. DIM: aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) ligand → CYP1A1/1A2 induction; estrogen 2-hydroxylation → 2-OHE1/2-OHE2 (less estrogenic) over 16α-OHE1 (more proliferative); selective estrogen receptor modulation (ERα antagonism, ERβ agonism) at higher doses.

**Liver cooling (TCM framework):**
TCM classification of bai cai as a "liver-cooling" food maps to: (1) bitter ITCs stimulating hepatic phase II detoxification; (2) folate supporting hepatic methylation capacity; (3) high water/potassium content supporting renal clearance; (4) alkalizing mineral profile.

**Rapini/cima di rapa bitter stimulation:**
The flowering tops (*ssp. rapifera* turnip tops and rapini) concentrate glucosinolates 3–5× versus leaves → intense bitterness → strong choleretic and digestive stimulation.

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**Level A (adjacent — population/nutritional epidemiology):**
High crucifer vegetable intake (of which pak choi and napa are major contributors in East Asian populations) consistently associates with reduced colorectal and breast cancer incidence in large prospective cohort studies (Bharat 2004, Higdon 2007 Pharmacological Research); the specific contribution of Brassica rapa forms is not isolated from total crucifer intake.

**Level B:**
- Pak choi calcium bioavailability: metabolic balance studies (Heaney 1993) demonstrate 40% fractional absorption vs 5% spinach; consistent across studies; this distinction directly informs clinical dietary counseling for dairy-free calcium adequacy
- Canola oil cardiovascular (omega-6:3 ratio): multiple RCTs support low omega-6:omega-3 dietary ratio cardiovascular benefit; PREDIMED + Mediterranean diet trials (Level A for the overall dietary pattern)

**Level C:**
- Turnip root digestive: European folk use documented in multiple national pharmacopoeia-adjacent texts; no controlled trial
- Rapini/bitter greens choleretic: clinical observation, case series; no RCT
- I3C/DIM and hormonal cancers: numerous Phase II trials with mixed results; DIM supplementation studies Level B quality for specific outcomes

**Level D:**
- NRF2 activation by Brassica rapa glucosinolates: cell line and rodent studies (multiple; consistent with Brassicaceae class evidence)
- Gluconapin anticarcinogenic: in vitro studies in colon and breast cancer lines

## 7. Phenomenological Record

**Turnip as the pre-potato winter staple:**
Before the potato's 18th-century European adoption, turnip was the primary winter root vegetable across Northern and Central Europe. The entire winter survival food system — turnip soup, turnip beer, stored turnips in root cellars, turnip-based animal fodder — structured rural European life from roughly 500 CE to 1800 CE. Medicinally, turnip featured in every major European herbal during this period: Fuchs, Matthioli, Culpeper, and Bauhin all document it. Its pharmacological displacement by the potato parallels a nutritional regression: turnip provides 2.0 g dietary fiber/100g and significant glucosinolates; potato provides starch energy with fewer phytochemicals. Post-potato European folk medicine largely forgot turnip medicine as a distinct practice.

**Kimchi and Brassica rapa pekinensis:**
Napa cabbage (*ssp. pekinensis*) is the primary substrate for kimchi, the Korean fermented vegetable preparation that has undergone more rigorous scientific investigation than almost any traditional fermented food. Kimchi fermentation by *Lactobacillus kimchii* and other lactic acid bacteria transforms Brassica glucosinolates, producing glucosinolate hydrolysis products with distinct bioavailability profiles from fresh cabbage. The cultural-pharmacological integration is total: kimchi is simultaneously a staple food, a probiotic vehicle, a glucosinolate delivery mechanism, and a cultural identity marker consumed across all socioeconomic levels multiple times daily in Korea.

**Canola oil and the politics of food safety:**
The 1970s development of canola oil (low-erucic-acid rapeseed) was one of the most consequential plant breeding achievements in modern food history. Original rapeseed (40–50% erucic acid) produced cardiac lipidosis in rodent feeding studies; Canadian (Manitoba) and Swedish breeders achieved <2% erucic acid by 1974. The "canola" name was coined in 1978 specifically to dissociate the new crop from rapeseed's reputation — an early example of plant breeding for safety rather than yield affecting naming and marketing of a commodity oil crop now consumed globally.

**Rapini (cima di rapa) in southern Italian cuisine:**
*Cima di rapa* (turnip tops) — the bitter, glucosinolate-concentrated flowering tops — anchors the cucina povera of Puglia, Calabria, and Campania. "Orecchiette con le cime di rapa" is considered the signature dish of Puglia; the bitterness from ITCs, historically managed with repeated blanching (reducing glucosinolate content) or blending with olive oil and garlic, represents daily dietary glucosinolate consumption embedded in regional cultural identity rather than conscious therapeutic practice.

## 8. Practical Application

**Therapeutic dose ranges:**

| Form | Dose | Indication |
|------|------|-----------|
| Pak choi (raw or lightly steamed) | 200–400 g/day | Dairy-free calcium adequacy; folate; anti-inflammatory glucosinolates |
| Napa cabbage (fresh) | 150–300 g/day | Microbiome support; cancer chemopreventive dietary baseline |
| Turnip root (raw grated or cooked) | 100–200 g/day | Digestive bitters; winter liver support |
| Rapini (blanched briefly) | 100–200 g/day | Choleretic; intense glucosinolate dose |
| DIM supplement (from glucobrassicin extraction) | 100–300 mg/day | Hormonal balance; estrogen metabolism (supplement form) |
| Canola oil (culinary) | 15–30 mL/day | Best dietary omega-6:3 ratio among common cooking oils |

**Preparation principles:**
Lightly steaming (2–3 minutes) pak choi preserves >80% glucosinolates and >60% vitamin C while improving palatability. Blanching reduces bitterness (removes ITCs via water extraction) but reduces therapeutic content — traditionally appropriate for culinary palatability. Raw consumption maximizes myrosinase-ITC activation but most people will not consume therapeutic quantities raw.

**Contraindications:**
- **Hypothyroid / on thyroid medication:** Progoitrin → goitrin → TPO inhibition; limit high-dose supplemental forms; culinary amounts safe for most patients; separate levothyroxine by ≥2 hours; monitor TSH if consuming large quantities of raw turnip daily
- **Warfarin:** Consistent monitoring with consistent intake; vitamin K content (45–70 μg/100g) significant at high volumes

**Drug interactions:**
- Levothyroxine: timing separation (≥2 hours) recommended; monitor TSH with high intakes
- Warfarin: consistent intake preferred over variable; adjust INR monitoring accordingly
- DIM supplementation specifically: CYP1A2 induction → may reduce caffeine, olanzapine, clozapine blood levels; monitor clinically

**Safety profile:**
All leaf forms: GRAS; nutritionally excellent; no upper limit concerns at culinary doses. Turnip root: safe across all populations. High-dose DIM supplementation: mild GI effects; theoretical hormonal effects at high doses; avoid in pregnancy. Canola oil: generally safe; avoid high-heat frying (omega-3 oxidation at high temperatures).

**Calcium adequacy note:**
For dairy-free diets, pak choi is clinically recommended as the most bioavailable plant-based calcium source available; 300 g/day provides ~315 mg calcium with ~40% absorption efficiency (≈126 mg net absorbed), comparable to ~220 mL whole milk.

**Energetic tier:** ◉ Qi (digestive/hepatic regulation); secondary ◈ Jing (nutritive mineral density, especially calcium)  
**Optimal form:** Fresh pak choi/napa cabbage for daily nutritive-chemopreventive dose; turnip root (raw grated) for digestive bitters; rapini for concentrated choleretic application; DIM extract for targeted hormonal metabolism
