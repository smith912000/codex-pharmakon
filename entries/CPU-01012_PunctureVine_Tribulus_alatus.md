# Winged Puncture Vine — Gokshura (relative), Ci Ji Li (relative), Caltrop

## *Tribulus alatus* Delile | Family: Zygophyllaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM, EP, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Warm, drying, dispersing (Galenic/Unani); moving, diuretic
**Training Phase:** Circulation / Waterworks (urinary flushing; disputed vitality claim)

> **Note — species and relatives.** *Tribulus alatus* is the winged-fruited puncture vine of North Africa and the Near East, closely allied to the far better-studied *Tribulus terrestris* L. Most modern pharmacological and clinical data are for *T. terrestris*; where evidence below is from *T. terrestris* it is labelled as such and applied to *T. alatus* only by botanical/chemical proximity, not by direct study. The Ayurvedic **Gokshura** is *T. terrestris*; the Chinese **Ci Ji Li / Bai Ji Li** (白蒺藜) is also *T. terrestris*.

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian.** The spiny caltrop-fruited *Tribulus* is native across Mesopotamia and appears in the Near-Eastern repertory of plants for urinary complaint and "stone" — a plausible reading given the plant's persistent later reputation as a diuretic and lithagogue. Specific cuneiform binomial attribution is uncertain and should be hedged. [USER: verify the Assyrian plant-name attribution before printing any native name.]

**Ebers Papyrus / Egyptian.** *Tribulus* species grow throughout Egypt and the caltrop fruit is consistent with the Egyptian tradition of plant diuretics and urinary remedies. Precise Ebers identification is debated among Egyptologists — treat as tradition-general, not a verbatim prescription. [USER: verify Ebers attribution.]

**Dioscorides (De Materia Medica).** Dioscorides describes *tribolos* (τρίβολος), the land-caltrop, directing it (in decoction) as a cooling astringent for the mouth and, taken internally, against "stone" and urinary gravel — an early explicit statement of the urinary/lithiasis use that persists across every later tradition.

**Sushruta / Ayurveda — Gokshura.** *T. terrestris* is **Gokshura**, one of the principal *mutrala* (diuretic) and *ashmarihara* (stone-dissolving) dravyas, a staple of *Dashamula* and of urinary and rejuvenative (*rasayana*/*vajikarana*) formulae. Ayurveda holds it as both a genuine urinary remedy *and* a *vajikarana* (aphrodisiac/virilising) tonic — the two claims that the modern evidence base treats very differently.

**Avicenna / Unani.** Carried as a warm, dispersing diuretic *mudirr al-bawl* for gravel, dysuria and bladder complaint, and — echoing the Ayurvedic reading — as a tonic to sexual function.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Two doctrinal strands, one well-founded and one over-sold. The **urinary/lithiasis** strand — "moving, drying, flushes gravel from the bladder" — is plausibly grounded in the plant's diuretic saponins and flavonoids and in a signature-of-form that is almost too neat: a spiny *stone*-like fruit prescribed for urinary *stone*. The **aphrodisiac/virilising** strand maps onto the **steroidal saponins, principally protodioscin**, whose furostanol skeleton is structurally steroid-adjacent and which marketing translates directly into "raises testosterone." Here doctrine and molecular appearance conspire to over-promise: a steroid-*shaped* saponin is read as a steroid-*making* agent. The honest chemistry (below) is that protodioscin's furostanol structure does *not* reliably translate into raised endogenous testosterone in humans — the symbolic "steroid signature" outruns the pharmacology. The doctrine of the caltrop is thus a study in where "signature" thinking succeeds (urinary) and where it fails (testosterone).

### Active Components

- **Steroidal saponins — protodioscin** (furostanol; the principal marker), dioscin, and related spirostanol/furostanol glycosides.
- **Flavonoids** — quercetin, kaempferol, rutin glycosides (antioxidant, contributing to diuretic/vascular effects).
- **Alkaloids** — harman/norharman β-carbolines (implicated in livestock neurotoxicity, "staggers").
- **Steroidal sapogenins** — diosgenin (aglycone; industrially relevant, not evidence of in-vivo steroidogenesis).
- **Tannins, sterols, potassium salts** (the potassium load contributes to the mild diuretic reputation).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Filed under **Circulation / Waterworks**: a traditional urinary-tract flushing and mild diuretic herb for gravel, dysuria and irritable bladder, and (in some populations) a mild pro-libido tonic — *explicitly not* filed as a testosterone booster. The aphrodisiac use is retained as a hedged, population-dependent effect on sexual desire, decoupled from any hormonal claim. Pairs with corn silk, couch grass and gokshura-containing urinary formulae.

### Scientific Correlation

*Based on articles retrieved from PubMed (all clinical data below are for *Tribulus terrestris*):*

- **Testosterone claim — largely NOT supported.** Santos et al.'s randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in men with erectile dysfunction (800 mg/day, 30 days) found *no* difference between *T. terrestris* and placebo in either erectile-function score or serum total testosterone — improvement occurred in both arms (a time/placebo effect), with no between-group difference ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.acuro.2013.09.014)). This is the core deflation: the marquee "testosterone booster" claim fails in controlled men.
- **Libido — some positive signal, mainly in women.** In premenopausal women with hypoactive sexual desire disorder, Vale et al. found *T. terrestris* improved multiple Female Sexual Function Index domains versus placebo, with a modest rise in free/bioavailable testosterone ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/09513590.2017.1409711)); de Souza et al. reported similar improvements in desire and function in postmenopausal women at 750 mg/day ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/GME.0000000000000766)). Honest reading: a real but population-specific effect on *sexual desire/function* (best evidenced in women), which is not the same as reliably "raising testosterone" in men.
- **Protodioscin chemistry.** Kuvendziev et al. characterise protodioscin as the aphrodisiac-associated saponin proposed to enhance testosterone-to-DHT conversion and libido — but this is a mechanistic/extraction study, not a clinical demonstration of raised human testosterone ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ultsonch.2024.107141)). The proposed mechanism remains hypothesis, not confirmed human endocrinology.

**Evidence grade:** *Urinary/diuretic/lithiasis use* — traditionally consistent, biologically plausible, modestly supported (better than the hormonal claim). *Libido/sexual function* — low-to-moderate, population-dependent (women > men). *"Raises testosterone in men"* — **not supported by controlled trials; treat as marketing myth.** *Muscle/athletic performance* — not supported. The gap between the supplement-industry claim and the controlled evidence is the entry's central lesson.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** *Ci Ji Li / Bai Ji Li* (白蒺藜, *T. terrestris* fruit) — acrid, bitter, warm/neutral; enters Liver; **calms Liver, subdues rising Liver yang, dispels wind, brightens the eyes, moves Liver qi** (classically used for headache, dizziness, red eyes, and Liver-qi stagnation — note the classical Chinese use is *not* primarily aphrodisiac).
- **Ayurveda (Gokshura, *T. terrestris*):** *rasa*: sweet (madhura); *guna*: heavy, unctuous; *virya*: cooling (shita); *vipaka*: sweet; actions *mutrala* (diuretic), *ashmarihara* (litholytic), *vajikarana* (aphrodisiac/tonic), *rasayana*.
- **Galenic / Unani:** warm and drying, dispersing; *mudirr al-bawl* (diuretic) and tonic to sexual function.

*(Note the divergence: Chinese doctrine treats the caltrop primarily as a Liver-calming/eye-brightening herb, while Ayurvedic/Unani doctrine emphasises the urinary and aphrodisiac roles — the traditions do not agree on its core action.)*

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian (EP).** *Tribulus* species grow throughout Egypt and the caltrop sits within the Egyptian tradition of plant diuretics and urinary remedies, but the precise Ebers identification is debated and the entry marks it for verification; the Ebers corpus is indication-organised, recording what was done for a urinary complaint, not what a substance does to a person's *ka*, *ib* or vital constitution. No operative *heka* recitation and no deity attribution is sourced for the caltrop. Cultivation status: **(c) absent** — the attestation is genus-level and therapeutic, and species identity here is a modern hypothesis.

**Assyrian (AT).** The spiny caltrop is native across Mesopotamia and plausibly sits in the Near-Eastern repertory for urinary complaint and "stone," but the specific cuneiform attribution is uncertain and the standing blocking warning for Assyrian substance identifications, deity attributions and tablet references is in force. Where illness is sorted by causal agency (divine hand, ghost, oath), no such role is sourced for this plant; the reading is therapeutic at most. No cultivation status is claimed. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *Tribolos* (τρίβολος), the land-caltrop, is given a therapeutic account — a cooling astringent decoction for the mouth, and taken internally against stone and urinary gravel — and Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system into which a cultivation status could be set. What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention: recognising the caltrop by its spined fruit, preparing the decoction correctly, and judging the plant by provenance and season. That is a practice of assay, not a vital-body doctrine. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The caltrop is carried as a warm, drying, dispersing diuretic (*mudirr al-bawl*) for gravel, dysuria and bladder complaint and — echoing the Ayurvedic reading — as a tonic to sexual function. The strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus, the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture (a finite endowment whose depletion is ageing, so longevity is conservation rather than acquisition), is a frame of the whole regimen and not a status the tradition assigns to this diuretic; the sexual-tonic strand is the over-sold one the entry deflates — a therapeutic tonic claim, not a sourced cultivation status — and no gladdening cardiac (*mufarrih*) role for the *ruh* is claimed. **(b) framed.**

**Ayurveda (SS).** Ayurveda's *Gokshura* — classically *Tribulus terrestris*, not the winged *T. alatus* of this entry — is a principal *mutrala* (diuretic) and *ashmarihara* (stone-dissolving) dravya and carries a *vajikarana* (generative/aphrodisiac) and *rasayana* standing, a genuine formal cultivation status among the Sushrutan divisions. But that status is attested for *T. terrestris*; *T. alatus* enters only by botanical and chemical proximity, and no *rasayana* or *vajikarana* assignment is attested for the winged species itself in the sources retrieved. For this substance the standing is therefore therapeutic (urinary) by genus-proximity, with the rejuvenative status belonging to its relative. **(b) framed** for *T. alatus* — the *vajikarana/rasayana* grade attaches to *Gokshura* (*T. terrestris*), not to this species.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator works the winged caltrop as a urinary-tract flushing and mild diuretic herb for gravel, dysuria and irritable bladder — the one strand that is plausibly grounded — and, cautiously and population-dependently, as a mild pro-libido tonic decoupled from any hormonal claim. It is explicitly not a testosterone builder: the marquee virilising claim fails in controlled men. The formal Ayurvedic rejuvenative (*vajikarana/rasayana*) status belongs to *Gokshura* (*T. terrestris*), not to *T. alatus*; a cultivator using the winged species should treat it as a urinary herb held by genus-proximity, not as a vitality tonic in its own right.

### Contraindications

- **The testosterone myth — flag explicitly.** Do not market or prescribe *Tribulus* as a testosterone booster or anabolic aid; controlled human evidence does not support raised testosterone in men. Athletes should note supplement products carry contamination/adulteration risk.
- **Spiny fruit hazard.** The hardened, spined caltrop fruit is a mechanical injury hazard (skin, foot, mouth) and to livestock — handle dried fruit with care; not to be taken whole.
- **Hepato/neurotoxicity reports.** Case reports link high-dose *T. terrestris* supplement use to hepatotoxicity and neurotoxicity; discontinue with any signs of liver injury. [USER: verify whether the codex wishes to cite a specific human case report — none retrieved this run; stated as a general safety flag, not a cited datum.]
- **Livestock phototoxicity (geeldikkop).** *Tribulus terrestris* is a documented cause of hepatogenous photosensitisation ("geeldikkop"/"yellow thick head") in grazing sheep via steroidal-saponin-induced hepatic crystal formation — a genuine veterinary hazard and a caution against high saponin loads.
- **Pregnancy/lactation:** avoid — hormonal/uterine activity in animal models and insufficient human safety data.
- **Diuretic interaction:** caution with other diuretics/potassium-affecting drugs; caution in renal impairment.
