# Armenian bole — طين أرمني (Avicenna, *Canon* Book II)

## *A red clay tending to dusky grey, known as ṭīn armanī, Armenian bole; the identification is by name and description, confidence high at the level of 'a red bolus clay', and no source of the earth is stated; it is a different substance from the spine's Armenian stone (CPU-01282, blue copper carbonate)* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Avicenna, Canon (CM).** The entry is *ṭīn armanī* (*Canon* Book II, Arabic text line 13594, to line 13604). Description: هو طين أحمر إلى الغبرة معروف يستعمله الصائغون في صبغ الذهب والالاني (13595-13596): "it is a known clay, red going towards dusky-grey; goldsmiths use it in colouring gold and ..." (the last word, *al-ālānī*, is unclear in this text and is left untranslated; [USER: verify]).

Temperament: قريب الطبع: بارد في الأولى يابس في الثانية, "near [in nature]: cold in the first degree, dry in the second" (13596-13597). Property: يحبس الدم لأن تجفيفه في الغاية, "it holds back blood, because its drying is at the extreme" (13597). Swellings: it helps against plagues (*ṭawāʿīn*), drunk and applied, and prevents the spread of putrefaction in the members (13597-13598). Wounds: "wonderful in the matter of wounds" (13598). Head: it prevents catarrh and helps thrush (13599). Chest: good for spitting of blood, helps consumption (*sill*) by drying the ulcer of the lung, and "is a remedy for tightness of breath from descents" (13599-13601). Excretion: good for ulcers of the bowels, diarrhoea and uterine bleeding (13601). Fevers: it helps consumptive and pestilential fevers especially (13602); "a people were saved from a great pestilence because they were accustomed to drink it in thin wine" (وقد سلم قوم من وباء عظيم لاعتيادهم شربه في شراب رقيق, 13602-13603); and if it is given in pestilential fever there must be wine "to escort it to the heart" (ليبذرقه إلى القلب), the wine mixed with rose water (13603-13604). No source author is named.

**Not the spine's Armenian stone.** The spine row Armenian stone (CPU-01282, entry II_22) is the blue copper mineral that the Canon and Dioscorides treat elsewhere; this clay is a separate substance.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established fact: clays adsorb water and are astringent on skin and mucosa. Eating clay can interfere with the absorption of other medicines and may carry grit or contaminants. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

In Avicenna's Unani terms this cold and dry clay (cold in the first degree, dry in the second) acts on the blood and on bleeding sites. It is said to hold back blood, and it is given for spitting of blood, for uterine bleeding, and for ulcers and diarrhoea of the bowels. For the chest it is named for the lung: it dries a consumptive ulcer of the lung (sill) and is given against tightness of breath. In the head it is said to prevent catarrh and to help thrush, which maps to the nasal and oral mucosa. For wounds it is called wonderful, and for swellings and plague (tawaʿin) it is drunk and applied, to stop putrefaction spreading in the members (limbs). The fever section names consumptive and pestilential fevers and says the clay should be given with wine to escort it to the heart. Modern anatomy places its claimed sites in the blood and vessels, uterus, bowel, lung, nasal and oral mucosa, skin and wound tissue, limbs and heart.

### Contraindications

- **Contamination and grit.** Natural clays may carry heavy metals or microbes. Do not eat clay on the strength of this entry.
