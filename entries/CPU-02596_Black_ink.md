# Black ink — Melan (μέλαν, Dioscorides)

## *Writing ink made from soot, gum and other additions, used by Dioscorides as an applied medicine for burns; the identification is the chapter's own description, so confidence in "soot-and-gum ink" is high, and it is a manufactured preparation and not a single natural mineral* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **CAUTION.** One of the recipes below includes chalcanthum (blue vitriol or copper sulphate, as the translator glosses it), which is toxic. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation.

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 8 November 2026 as an unverified placeholder; this entry replaces it. It is distinct from the soot row (CPU-01081) and from the glass-maker's soot of 5-182, which is a separate chapter.

**Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter is 5-183, "MELAN", glossed "Black Ink" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 10775 to 10777). It stands at the very end of the fifth book, in the group headed "Metallic Stones", immediately after 5-182 (asbole, "Soot from glass-making"). It reads: "The ink with which we write is prepared from the soot gathered from torches, and to each ounce of gum is mixed three ounces of soot. It is also made from the soot of rosin, and the previously mentioned soot pictoria. Mix one pound of soot pictoria, half a pound of gum, half an ounce of bulls' glue, and half an ounce of chalcanthum [blue vitriol or copper sulphate]. It is good included in antiseptic medicines; and for burns it is rubbed on thickly with water and left alone until it forms new skins, for it falls off of its own accord when the sores have healed."

The chapter is followed in the text by Dioscorides' closing address to Areius and the words "AN END OF THE FIFTH AND LAST BOOK OF MEDICINAL MATERIALS" (lines 10778 to 10779). The ink is thus the final substance in the work.

The neighbouring soot chapter 5-182 says of glassmakers' soot (fuligio pictoria): "It is putrifying and sharp, and with a waxy ointment of rosaceum [1-53] it heals fractures." The ink recipe refers back to it ("the previously mentioned soot pictoria").

### Active Components

The source names no constituent. In general terms, soot is mostly amorphous carbon (lamp-black), gum is a plant polysaccharide used as a binder, and glue is an animal protein binder; the translator glosses chalcanthum as copper sulphate, a soluble copper salt that is an irritant and toxic if swallowed. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Inclusion in "antiseptic medicines", and for burns: rubbed on thickly with water and left alone until new skin forms and the dressing falls off of its own accord.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no frequency, and no instruction beyond leaving the application undisturbed.

The Codex does not translate "antiseptic" into modern wound care, gives no protocol, and does not suggest putting ink or a copper salt on a burn.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives the manufacture and not a dosing: gum and soot at one to three by weight from torch soot (three ounces of soot to each ounce of gum); or one pound of soot pictoria, half a pound of gum, half an ounce of bulls' glue, and half an ounce of chalcanthum (line 10775 onward). For burns it is "rubbed on thickly with water". These are recipe proportions for making ink, not doses for a patient. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Carbon black and gum arabic are the classic constituents of ancient carbon inks. Copper sulphate is an antimicrobial and astringent salt that is also caustic and toxic; it is not used on burns in modern care. Burns need clean, cool-water first aid and medical assessment, and unsterile pigment on a burn risks infection and permanent staining (tattooing) of the wound. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** The chapter prints no quality of the kind a later tradition would call warm, cold, moist or dry, and no taste. The neighbouring chapter's "putrifying and sharp" is said of the soot, not of the ink, and is not carried across.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. No cultivation status is given to the ink. What the chapter shows is a discipline of attention in the specification's sense: the sources of the soot are listed, the proportions of binder are recorded, and the substitution of one soot for another is noted ("It is also made from the soot of rosin"). The closing placement of the ink as the last item of the work is a fact about the text and implies nothing.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing for a cultivator to do with it as a medicine. If one makes ink, it is a craft and the chapter is a recipe. Do not apply it to the body.

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

Little to map: the chapter names one site, the burn. Rubbed on thickly with water and left alone, the preparation is said to form new skin over the burn and to fall off once the sores have healed, so the claimed tissue is the damaged skin of the wound surface, where new epidermis forms. The chapter also includes the ink among antiseptic medicines, which is a class of action and names no organ or tissue. The text prints no quality of the warm, cold, moist or dry kind, no taste and no temperament. No dose, frequency or vehicle beyond water is printed. The mapped claim is the surface of a burn and its healing, and nothing deeper in the body is named.

### Contraindications

- **Burns.** Do not use. Cool the burn with running water and seek care.
- **Copper salt.** The recipe containing chalcanthum is toxic if swallowed and irritating to skin and eyes.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Contamination.** Soot pigment and glue are not sterile.
- **Identity.** The ink is a made product with several recipes; there is no single substance to name.
