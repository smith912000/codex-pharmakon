# Rabdosia rugosa — Shhimthikle, zhim thig le (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary by column position); "Rabdosia"; printed "Rabdosia ragosa"

## *Rabdosia rugosa, as the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints it (OCR "Rabdosia ragosa", line 5758, as the second of two scientific names on that line) in its first "S" run, which column order sets against the name Shhimthikle (zhim thig le) and the English "Rabdosia"; Clark 1995 prints no Rabdosia in the entry for the same Tibetan name* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi (Four Tantras), read in two modern translations, Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011. The binomial comes from the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary's column order alone, and Clark's entry for the same Tibetan name prints five other Latin names, so the botanical identity is open.** Both texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk found no *Rabdosia* or *Isodon*. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP, confidence "medium") and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. **Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 354) the row is keyed by the scientific name "Rabdosia ragosa", with the locus "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 5758)" and the note "Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". The key is not the first name of its line: line 5758 reads "Pinus sp. Rabdosia ragosa", and *Rabdosia* is the second. The OCR reads "u" as "a", so the form is read as *rugosa*, and the spine merges the printed "ragosa" into it. The Tibetan name comes from the column order below and not from the CSV.

**The glossary, by column position.** `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the first "S" run. The phonetic names (lines 5685 to 5719) are fifteen when each is counted separately: "Sa dre", "Saegoe", "Sae kar", "Sazika", "Sedu", "Sengdeng", "Sergyi metok", "Sergyi phuebu", "Ser kue", "Sewai metok", "Seyab", then "Shelta Shhimthikle Shhu khen" together on line 5718, and "Shingkun". The transliterations (lines 5687 to 5725) include "zhim thig le" (line 5723). The scientific names (lines 5727 to 5765) are fifteen: Syzygium and *Rosa macrophylla* (5727), *Oxytropis kansuensis* (5732), *Ustilago nuda*, *Punica granatum* and *Acacia catechu* (5736 to 5737), *Herpetospermum pedunculosum* (5743), *Luffa aegyptiaca* (5745), *Lethariella* (5749), *Rosa branonii* (5753), *Chaenomeles speciosa* (5757), then on line 5758 "Pinus sp. Rabdosia ragosa", then *Symplocus paniculata* (5764) and *Ferula asafetida* (5765). The English names (5729 to 5768) are "Black plum", "Rose", "-", "Corn ergot", "Pomegranite", "Catechu tree", "Golden flower", "Sponge gourd", "Golden dodder", "Musk rose", "Tibetan quince", "-", "Rabdosia", "Sapphire berry" and "Asafoetida". Counted in order, "Shhimthikle" is the thirteenth phonetic name, *Rabdosia ragosa* the thirteenth scientific name and "Rabdosia" the thirteenth English name. The count is checked at the twelfth place and from both ends: "Shelta" (twelfth) stands against *Pinus sp.* as Clark's *shel.ta* (line 926) is "Pine Resin: Pinus griffithii"; "Sengdeng" (sixth) against *Acacia catechu* as Clark's *seng.ldeng* (line 985); "Saegoe" (second) against *Rosa macrophylla* as Clark's *se.rgod* (line 1042); and "Shingkun" (fifteenth) against *Ferula asafetida*. The same run is counted in I_1251 (*Chaenomeles speciosa*), I_1324 (*Oxytropis kansuensis*) and I_1348 (*Rosa roxburghii*). The glossary does not print the pairing on one row. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents line 225), page 208.* Line 3442: "Shhimthikle removes lingthok." The index lists "Shhimthikle 208" (line 7491) and "lingthok 208" (line 7420). "Lingthok" is a kept Tibetan term (note at line 686). The Glossary on Tibetan Terms lists "Lingthok (ling tog)" third in a run of four terms at line 4919, and the definitions printed in order at lines 4924 to 4927 give, third, "An eye disorder accompanied with appearance of pimple-like growth in the eyes" (line 4926). That pairing is by position, as in I_1295. [USER: verify] The text gives no Latin name, no description and no taste for the drug.

*Clark 1995, chapter 20, "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, line 1054: "zhim.thig.le: Geranium dahuricum: Plectranthus sp. (black type): Geranium pratense: Euphrasia reqellii Wettstein.: Morrubium incisum: [has fifteen names. It has a square stem with many joints, square petioles and a leaf similar to that of nettle, but without sting. ... All three main types] dispel cataract [as well as other eye diseases and are anthelmintic]." Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the five Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the clause "dispel cataract"; the description, the tastes and the further uses are the commentary layer. Clark's name index lists *zhim.thig.le* (line 1743). The two translations agree in kind, since both give an eye drug (Clark "dispel cataract", Men-Tsee-Khang "removes lingthok"); the Codex does not treat "cataract" and "lingthok" as one term. The two texts are read as one drug on the spelling of the name and that agreement; neither says so. [USER: verify]

**Identity: the glossary against Clark.** Clark's list is *Geranium dahuricum*, *Plectranthus* sp., *Geranium pratense*, *Euphrasia* and *Marrubium incisum*. The glossary prints *Rabdosia* with the English "Rabdosia". As a general botanical fact *Rabdosia* is a genus of the mint family (Lamiaceae), as are *Plectranthus* and *Marrubium*, and *Geranium* and *Euphrasia* belong to other families. That the glossary's genus and Clark's *Plectranthus* sp. may be near each other is a general observation and not a statement of the texts, and the Codex does not choose among the names. The row stands for the Tibetan drug and not for one species. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Rabdosia*, *Isodon*, *Plectranthus*, Shhimthikle and *zhim.thig.le* found no entry on this binomial. Entries on the same Tibetan name and the same passages (Clark line 1054; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3442) are **I_1295** (*Geranium dahuricum*, tag FMT), **I_1296** (*Geranium pratense*, tag FMT) and **I_1315** (*Marrubium incisum*, tag FMT); each is keyed to a different Latin name of Clark's list, and none prints *Rabdosia*. I did not find a spine row for *Plectranthus* or *Euphrasia regelii*. **I_808** (Eyebright, *Euphrasia officinalis*) and **I_597** (White Horehound, *Marrubium vulgare*) are entries on European and other-tradition plants and not on this drug. I_1251, I_1324 and I_1348 quote the same glossary run. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. As a general fact, *Rabdosia* (also placed in *Isodon*) is a genus of the mint family known for diterpenoid compounds in some of its species. That is a statement about the genus and not a finding of the Tibetan texts, which do not say which plant is meant. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record one use of the name: an eye drug, which "removes lingthok" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3442) and whose entry in Clark says "dispel cataract" (line 1054, outside the brackets). The commentary layer adds other eye diseases and an anthelmintic use. That is the tradition's own record and not a Codex protocol. The identity is open, so the Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Both translations name the drug in the materia medica as an eye drug (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3442; Clark line 1054). That is a therapeutic place and not a status in rejuvenation or in *bcud len* (essence extraction). The rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23: Clark lines 1264 to 1276; Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3904 to 3945) does not name the drug. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. **Practitioner's note.** A cultivator has no practice to take from this entry. The one thing the texts say is that an eye drug of this name exists; the plant is not fixed, and the glossary's genus may not be the plant Clark's physicians meant. Nothing here supports taking or gathering a mint-family herb on the strength of the entry.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for the drug. The identity is uncertain, so no plant should be gathered or taken on the strength of this entry, and nothing should be put in the eye on it. No conservation information and no safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
