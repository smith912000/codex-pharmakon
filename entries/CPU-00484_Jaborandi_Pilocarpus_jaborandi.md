# Jaborandi / Jaborandi

## *Pilocarpus jaborandi* Holmes | Family: Rutaceae

**Tradition Tags:** SA
**Energetic Classification:** South American ethnobotanical framing — a "hot/opening" secretory stimulant in folk practice (a plant that "opens the pores and the mouth"); the humoral hot/cold axis is not native to Tupí-Guaraní systems, so this reading is *inferred* by analogy and marked as such.
**Training Phase:** Foundation — Signature Study

### Origin & Tradition

Jaborandi is a shrubby understory tree of the Rutaceae (the citrus and rue family), native to the moist forests and *caatinga* margins of northeastern Brazil — the states of Maranhão, Piauí, Ceará and Pernambuco. The name descends from Tupí-Guaraní roots commonly glossed as "slobber-mouth plant" or "plant that makes you drool," a folk-etymological reading that is *plausible but not securely documented* — recorded because the leaf's most conspicuous effect is a sudden flood of saliva. Northeastern Brazilian peoples chewed or infused the leaves as a sudorific (sweating agent) and sialagogue (saliva-inducing agent), and applied it in feverish and dropsical conditions where drawing fluid out through sweat and spit was the therapeutic aim.

The plant entered European medicine late and decisively. In the 1870s dried jaborandi leaf reached France and Britain, and the Brazilian physician Symphronio Coutinho is generally credited with demonstrating its dramatic salivating and sweating action to European clinicians. Within a few years the German chemist Hardy and, independently, the British pharmacist Gerrard isolated the principal alkaloid, **pilocarpine**, and the leaf and its alkaloid were rapidly absorbed into the *British Pharmacopoeia*, the *United States Pharmacopeia* and continental pharmacopoeias as *Folia Jaborandi* and *Pilocarpinae* salts. Jaborandi is therefore one of the clearest cases in this Codex of a South American folk secretory remedy being purified into a still-current pharmaceutical drug.

A commercial-source distinction matters for the cultivator and the buyer. Although *Pilocarpus jaborandi* Holmes is the classical "true jaborandi," most pilocarpine now in world trade is extracted from the **congener *Pilocarpus microphyllus* Stapf ex Wardleworth** — *maranhão jaborandi* — which yields a higher and more consistent alkaloid fraction and dominates the Brazilian export industry (largely from Maranhão and Piauí, controlled by the extraction firm long associated with the trade). Any "jaborandi" pilocarpine you meet in a pharmacy is most likely of *P. microphyllus* origin; the two species are named separately here and their leaf chemistry should not be treated as identical.

### Active Components

- **Pilocarpine** — the defining imidazole alkaloid, a direct-acting **muscarinic (parasympathomimetic) cholinergic agonist**. This is the molecule of interest.
- **Congener alkaloids** — isopilocarpine, pilocarpidine and related imidazole bases, present in smaller and variable amounts.
- **Volatile oil and tannins** in the leaf, contributing to the crude drug's older topical use.

Alkaloid content in the leaf is neither fixed nor high, and is strongly modulated by plant stress and growth conditions. In *P. jaborandi* seedlings, salicylic acid and methyl-jasmonate elicitors induced roughly a four-fold rise in leaf pilocarpine, while salt stress, wounding, hypoxia and nitrogen or potassium deprivation *lowered* it (Avancini et al., 2003) — a caution for the cultivator that "wild-harvested jaborandi" is a chemically variable material, not a standardised dose.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica jaborandi is filed as the archetypal **secretory opener** — the plant whose signature is an outpouring of the "watery" secretions the parasympathetic nervous system governs: saliva, sweat, tears, bronchial and gastric fluid. Pilocarpine stimulates muscarinic receptors on exocrine glands and on smooth muscle, producing salivation, diaphoresis, lacrimation, miosis (pupil constriction) and increased glandular and gut motility.

Two of these effects are genuine, current clinical uses of the *purified alkaloid* (not the crude leaf):

1. **Xerostomia (dry mouth).** Oral pilocarpine is an approved sialagogue for dry mouth following head-and-neck radiotherapy and in Sjögren syndrome — it works by driving residual functioning salivary tissue to secrete.
2. **Ophthalmic miotic.** As eye drops, pilocarpine constricts the pupil and lowers intraocular pressure, historically a mainstay in glaucoma and still used in specific indications (e.g. acute angle-closure, and more recently presbyopia drops).

The Kenetica reading keeps the folk logic and the pharmacology aligned: the Brazilian "sweating and slobbering herb" and the modern anti-dry-mouth drug are the *same cholinergic action*, read once as a fever therapy and once as a glandular rescue. What the Codex refuses is any leap from "opens secretions" to systemic "detoxification" or "cleansing" claims — there is no evidence the crude leaf detoxifies anything.

### Preparation & Dosing

The crude leaf is **not** a self-dosing herb. Pilocarpine is a prescription drug with a narrow therapeutic window; the table records forms for reference, not as a recipe.

| Form | Context | Notes on dosing |
|---|---|---|
| Dried leaf infusion (*Folia Jaborandi*) | Historical/folk sudorific | Alkaloid content highly variable (elicitor- and stress-dependent); no reliable standardisation — historical use only, not recommended |
| Oral pilocarpine HCl tablets (e.g. Salagen) | Xerostomia, Sjögren | In trials, **5 mg four times daily (20 mg/day)** produced significant symptom benefit; 2.5 mg doses were weaker (Vivino et al., 1999) — physician-directed only |
| Concomitant oral pilocarpine during radiotherapy | Prevention of radiation xerostomia | 5 mg three times daily used in trial protocols (Haddad & Karimi, 2002) — physician-directed only |
| Ophthalmic pilocarpine drops | Miosis, raised IOP, presbyopia | Specialist ophthalmic prescribing only |

Safety caveats: start low, titrate under supervision; the dose-limiting adverse effect is cholinergic excess (see Contraindications). Sweating is the most common adverse effect even at therapeutic oral doses (Vivino et al., 1999).

### Scientific Correlation

- **Pilocarpine is a muscarinic cholinergic agonist and sialagogue** — *Established (mechanism + pharmacopoeial drug).*
- **Oral pilocarpine relieves dry mouth in Sjögren syndrome** — *Established (multiple RCTs);* note a 2024 meta-analysis found benefit on salivary and some ocular-surface measures but **no** significant improvement on Schirmer's tear test or tear break-up time — i.e. partial, saliva-weighted efficacy, deflating any claim of uniform relief.
- **Oral pilocarpine reduces radiation-induced xerostomia** — *Established (RCT-level), effect modest and subjective-scale weighted.*
- **Crude jaborandi leaf as a general "detoxifier/fever cure"** — *Traditional / NULL* — no controlled evidence; the defensible action is cholinergic secretion, not detoxification.
- **Leaf pilocarpine yield is manipulable by elicitors and stress** — *Preclinical (plant physiology).*

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Within northeastern Brazilian folk practice jaborandi is a "drawing/opening" remedy — pulling heat and fluid outward through sweat and saliva, used against fevers and stagnation. Mapped onto the Codex's cross-tradition grid it sits as a **hot, opening, moistening-by-discharge** agent (moisture is *expelled*, not conserved). This is an analogical placement: the plant's home tradition did not use a Galenic humoral vocabulary, and the label is inferred, not indigenous.

### Contraindications

Pilocarpine's therapeutic action *is* controlled cholinergic excess; overdose or susceptibility tips into a **cholinergic crisis**. Real safety flags:

- **Cholinergic toxicity** — sweating, hypersalivation, lacrimation, nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramping, diarrhoea, urinary urgency, flushing, headache, and in excess bradycardia, hypotension and bronchospasm. The pharmacological antidote to muscarinic toxicity is **atropine**.
- **Uncontrolled asthma / COPD** — bronchospasm and increased bronchial secretion can precipitate respiratory compromise; avoid.
- **Symptomatic bradycardia and significant cardiovascular disease** — cholinergic slowing of the heart and hypotension are hazardous; use only under specialist supervision.
- **Acute iritis / anterior-chamber inflammation and certain glaucoma contexts** — miotic action is contraindicated in some ophthalmic states.
- **Peptic ulcer, bowel or bladder obstruction** — increased gastric acid, gut and bladder motility can worsen these.
- **Not for self-medication with crude leaf** — variable, unstandardised alkaloid content makes home dosing genuinely dangerous. Pregnancy/lactation: use only if clearly indicated and physician-directed.
- **CNS/heat caution** — profuse sweating can cause dehydration; ensure fluid intake.
