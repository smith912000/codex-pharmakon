### Origin & Tradition

*Strychnos nux-vomica*, the strychnine tree of India and Southeast Asia, yields a flat, grey-green, button-shaped, silky-haired seed that is among the most notorious of all plant poisons. Its name — *nux vomica*, "vomiting nut" — and its English folk name "Quaker buttons" attach to a seed whose principal alkaloid, **strychnine**, is one of the defining convulsant poisons of the pharmacological and forensic literature. The seed and its alkaloids run as a thread through three quite different traditions: the convulsant poison and (briefly) tonic-bitter of European and colonial pharmacy; the processed channel-opening drug **Ma Qian Zi (马钱子)** of Chinese Medicine; and the purified, painstakingly processed **Kupeelu/Vishamushti** of Ayurveda. In every tradition the same principle governs its use: **rigorous processing and minute dosing**, with an unforgiving margin between therapeutic and lethal.

Strychnine's mechanism is precisely understood and is the reason the seed is so dangerous. Strychnine is a **competitive antagonist at the inhibitory glycine receptor** of the spinal cord and brainstem. By blocking glycine's inhibitory braking on motor neurons, it removes the normal damping of reflex arcs: ordinary sensory stimuli — a sound, a touch, a light — provoke uncontrolled, generalised muscular contraction. The clinical and forensic hallmark is violent, symmetrical convulsion with the body arched backward on rigidly contracted extensor muscles (**opisthotonos**) and a fixed grimace (**risus sardonicus**), the victim fully conscious between spasms. Death is from respiratory failure and exhaustion during the seizures. **Brucine**, the seed's second major alkaloid, is a close congener with similar but generally less potent action.

In **Chinese Medicine** the processed seed, **Ma Qian Zi**, is classed among herbs that **invigorate blood and unblock the channels/collaterals** and relieve pain and swelling — used traditionally in minute, carefully processed doses for painful obstruction (bi syndrome), trauma, numbness, and certain forms of paralysis or muscle weakness, on the rationale that its powerful action on the motor/neuromuscular system "opens the channels." The raw seed is a frank poison; only the **processed** seed (traditionally treated by methods such as sand-frying or oil-frying after soaking, to reduce alkaloid content) is used, and even then in tightly limited dose. In **Ayurveda**, the purified seed (after extensive *shodhana* in cow's milk or other media) appears in classical formulations as a nervine and tonic-bitter, again in minute, processed quantities. European and colonial pharmacy used strychnine-containing preparations as bitter "tonics," appetite stimulants and respiratory/cardiac stimulants well into the twentieth century — a use long abandoned as far too dangerous, though strychnine persisted as a rodenticide.

This entry stands in the Codex alongside Aconite (I_004) as a paramount example of a botanical whose lethal toxicity and traditional therapeutic use are separated only by processing and microdosing — and as a substance that must never be self-administered.

### Active Components

- **Strychnine** — the principal toxic indole alkaloid. A **competitive antagonist at the inhibitory glycine receptor** in the spinal cord and brainstem; removes inhibitory tone on motor neurons, producing hyperreflexia and generalised convulsions (opisthotonos, risus sardonicus). Among the most potent convulsant poisons known.
- **Brucine** — the second major indole alkaloid; a strychnine congener (dimethoxy derivative) with qualitatively similar glycine-antagonist/convulsant action, generally regarded as less potent than strychnine; also studied for analgesic and anti-inflammatory activity in research settings.

### Preparation & Dosing

**No therapeutic home dose is given. The margin between the traditional therapeutic dose and a convulsant/lethal dose is extremely narrow.**

- **Raw seed — POISON.** The raw seed is a frank convulsant poison and is never ingested in any unsupervised context.
- **Processed Ma Qian Zi (Chinese Medicine).** The seed is detoxified by traditional processing (soaking, then sand-frying or oil-frying) to reduce alkaloid content, and is used only in **minute, tightly limited daily doses** — on the order of a fraction of a gram per day — within practitioner-prescribed formulae. ### Contraindications

- **Raw/unprocessed seed — absolute prohibition.** Never ingested in any unsupervised context; lethal dose is small.
- **Self-administration — absolute.** Even processed Ma Qian Zi is for qualified-practitioner use only, in minute prescribed dose.
- **Seizure disorders / hyperexcitable states — absolute.** A convulsant; contraindicated in epilepsy and any condition lowering seizure threshold.
- **Cardiac and respiratory disease — absolute.** Convulsions cause respiratory failure and cardiovascular crisis.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding — absolute.** Toxic; contraindicated.
- **Liver/kidney impairment.** Reduced clearance increases toxicity risk.
- **Heat patterns / yin deficiency (CM):** even processed, used with extreme caution; its toxicity dominates the safety picture regardless of pattern.
- **Drug interactions:** any agent altering seizure threshold, neuromuscular function, or alkaloid metabolism increases risk. [USER: verify specific interaction list.]
- **General:** any suspected ingestion is a life-threatening emergency requiring immediate intensive care — no field antidote.
