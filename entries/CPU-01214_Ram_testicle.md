# Ram testicle — *thug.hbras* (Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20); "Ram testicles" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *The testicle of the ram (male sheep); the texts print no Latin name for the animal* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is the Tibetan *Four Tantras*. In Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; "Clark" below) chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra ends its materia medica with "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), which opens: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). The ram's testicle is filed under the third of these, "Meat" (heading at line 1161), in a run of organ entries (otter and marmot liver, goat liver, wolf stomach, tongues). Line 1170 reads: "thug.hbras: Ram testicle: is aphrodisiac. Dog testicle: is ecbolic." Both statements stand outside Clark's square brackets, which mark interpolations from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries (line 82), so they are the root-text claims as Clark renders them. The Tibetan name is printed first, as Clark says he does for every substance (line 83). The list row `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 355 ("Ram testicles", class animal, identity flagged uncertain) is the spine's other locus and adds nothing beyond the name.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`; "MTK" below) has the same pair of claims in its own wording (line 3536): "Ram testicles are an aphrodisiac and dog testicle help deliver placenta." MTK's Glossary on Animals gives the Tibetan for the ram as *Lug thuk* (line 7119) and prints no Latin name for it. The two translations agree on the substance and the action. Neither names a preparation, a form or a quantity.

### Active Components

The testis is the organ that makes sperm and, in the male, most of the body's sex steroid (testosterone); as tissue it is also protein, fat, cholesterol and water. The Tibetan texts name no constituent and no mechanism for the "aphrodisiac" action, and this entry does not claim that the traditional action is explained by any hormone. Whether an eaten tissue delivers an active hormone dose is a separate question the sources do not touch.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources say one thing about this material: it is an aphrodisiac, listed beside other animal parts credited with the same or a related action in the same section (the flesh of the sparrow, the Asian sparrow and a gecko "increases semen" and, in the bracketed commentary, "is aphrodisiac, tonic and anodyne", line 1170; the male snow frog's urine is, again in brackets, "a highly recommended aphrodisiac", line 1169). The dietary chapter adds that otter flesh is an aphrodisiac (line 680) and mutton "acts as a tonic, develops the bodily constituents" (line 674). The last two are other materials and say nothing about the testicle. There is no sourced reason to use organ tissue from slaughtered livestock as a supplement or a protocol, and the Codex offers none. Clark also records that "nowadays only very few animal products are used" (line 57).

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither passage gives a preparation (raw, cooked, dried, powdered) or a dose. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] The compounding chapter (Clark ch.21, from line 1204) lists medicines by the disease they cure; the ram testicle is not named in the lists read.

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The only general facts offered are anatomical (above): the testis makes testosterone and sperm. They are not evidence that eating the organ affects human sexual function.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT).** For the ram testicle itself Clark prints no taste, potency or post-digestive taste. For meats in general the dietary chapter (ch.16, lines 672-673) says all the meats in its groups are "sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste"; the flesh of dry-land animals has "cool, light and coarse powers", that of aquatic animals "oily, heavy and warm" powers. MTK states the same in its own words (lines 2923-2931). Those rules are for the flesh of whole animals, and the testicle is listed in the animal-medicines section and not in the dietary groups, so no quality is assigned here. The one attribute the sources give is its action, aphrodisiac. Semen is one of the seven bodily constituents in the same text (line 145), which is context for the word and not a statement about this organ.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The ram testicle is attested in the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica as a meat-class animal medicine with a single action, aphrodisiac. The tradition has a formal division of rejuvenation, "essence-extraction" (Clark line 128, "the division of essence-extraction (rejuvenation, etc.)"; also line 136), and a separate division on inducing fertility (line 128). In the animal section (lines 1147-1202) no animal substance is assigned to either, and the ram's testicle is not named in the compounding lists of chapter 21. The status is therefore therapeutic: an aphrodisiac, as that tradition's translators render the word, and not a rejuvenation material. Clark's own statement of what is rejuvenating is about plants (line 69: sundew, angelica, *Polygonatum*, salep orchid). In this tradition medicine is one branch of treatment and the root of disease lies in the three poisons; no passage read ties this organ to that frame. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in the Tibetan sources, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports a cultivation use. The text records an aphrodisiac action, nothing about long-term nourishment or restraint, and the tradition's own translator says animal medicines have largely dropped out of use. For a cultivator the honest entry is a record, and the practical answer is to leave it as one.

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** raw or undercooked reproductive-organ tissue from livestock can carry brucellosis and other zoonoses; the tissue of any slaughtered animal must be fresh, cooked and from inspected stock.
- **Hormonal and reproductive conditions:** the organ is a hormone-producing tissue. Nothing in the texts addresses its effect, so it should not be used by anyone with a hormone-sensitive condition, in pregnancy, or while trying to conceive without medical advice. [USER: verify]
- **Legal and ethical status:** domestic sheep tissue is unrestricted as food in most places, but it is an animal by-product; the sources do not say how the Tibetan physician obtained it.
