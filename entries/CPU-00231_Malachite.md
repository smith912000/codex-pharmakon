# Malachite — Qing Lang Gan (青瑯玕) "Malachitum" (Yang 1998) / "Malachite" (project Ebers list); Bryan 1930 prints "Verdigris", "Copper-verdigris"
## *Basic copper carbonate, Cu2CO3(OH)2, the green copper mineral; Yang 1998 prints "Malachitum" for Qing Lang Gan with a footnote that it "may possibly be turquoise", and Bryan's Ebers does not print the word malachite at all, so the Egyptian identification is the list's and not the text's* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP, SBJ

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The project's list `_sources/ebers.csv` has one row, line 49: "Malachite", formula Cu2CO3(OH)2, class mineral, source reference "Ebers; Nunn 1996; ophthalmologytimes.com". Two of those three references are not scholarly texts that were read, and the word malachite does not occur in Bryan's English (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`; searched). What Bryan prints is "Verdigris" and "Copper-verdigris", in the Chapter V list of mineral remedies (line 1161 "Copper-verdigris"; line 1210 "Verdigris Copper-verdigris"; `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` rows 10 and 38). Verdigris is a copper corrosion product, and whether the Egyptian drug was malachite is a modern identification that the pages read do not give. Verdigris as Bryan prints it is used in the following places. Ground up with Bread, "made into three Pills, and swallowed down with Sweet Beer", in a run of remedies under Bryan's page head "Constipation, etc." (line 1685). Mixed with Myrrh, Onions, Cyperus-from-the-North, Antelope-dung, Clear Oil and Entrails-of-the-qadit-animal and "painted on with a Vulture's feather" for Bleary Eyes (line 2735). In a poultice with Onions, Collyrium and Arabian-wood powder, crushed with Ink and Water, for Bloodshot Eyes (line 2747). With Isinglass among the remedies tried for Cataract (line 2823). In "Words to be spoken over Verdigris mixed with Beetle-wax", added to Cyperus and applied to the eye, preceded by the call "Come, Verdigris! Come, Verdigris! Come, Thou Fresh One! Come, Efflux from the Eye of the god Horus!" (lines 2829-2839). And among the ingredients of a remedy for a mattery tumour in the neck (line 3858: "Wax Cow's fat ... Copper-shavings Verdigris Fresh lead-earth Sea-salt ..."). Bryan's other copper names ("Copper-rust", "Copper-vitriol", "Copper-coal", "Copper-shavings", line 1155) are different entries in his list and are not treated here as malachite. [USER: verify]

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** Qing Lang Gan is printed in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`) under the heading "Jades and Stones: Inferior Class" (PDF p.32, lines 1240, 1246-1249): "Qing Lang Gan (Malachitum) is acrid and balanced. It mainly treats itching of the body, burns, welling abscesses, sores, scabs, and dead muscles. Its other name is Shi Zhu (Stone Pearl). It is produced in plains and swamps." Yang's footnote 65 says "This medicinal may possibly be turquoise" (line 1263-1264). The identification is therefore uncertain in the translator's own hand. A neighbouring monograph, Fu Qing (Azuritum), carries a footnote "This medicinal may also be Malachitum. Its identity is not certain" (line 1217). The spine's old note, "low; id-uncertain", agrees.

### Active Components

Malachite is a green basic copper(II) carbonate, which dissolves in acids and releases copper ions. Verdigris, if that is what the Egyptian text means, is a basic copper acetate or carbonate formed by corrosion of copper. Both are copper salts: astringent and irritant on skin and eye, and toxic when swallowed. The sources read give no composition.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Both texts use the substance as an external remedy for eye or skin and wound problems (Yang: itching, burns, abscesses, sores, scabs; Bryan: eye remedies, with a spoken formula in one). The Egyptian recipe that gives it by mouth, as pills in beer for a complaint under the head "Constipation", is the sort of use that should not be repeated; copper salts are poisonous. The Codex offers no protocol, dose or preparation.

### Preparation & Dosing

EP: ground with bread into three pills and swallowed in sweet beer (line 1685); beaten with myrrh, onions, cyperus and other ingredients as an eye paint (line 2735); mixed with beetle-wax with a recitation, added to cyperus and applied to the eye (lines 2839-2840). No quantity is printed in the lines read except "three Pills". SBJ: no preparation is printed in the monograph. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Copper salts are astringent and antimicrobial in general and are also irritant and toxic to eye, skin and gut; copper poisoning by mouth can cause vomiting, haemolysis and liver and kidney damage. Azurite and malachite have been used as pigments, as eye paint ingredients and as medicines in antiquity. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent, and the attestation rests on a list row.* The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. *Heka* is attached to the verdigris at lines 2829-2840: the call "Come, Verdigris! ..." and the "Words to be spoken over Verdigris mixed with Beetle-wax" are a recitation joined to the drug, and the drug without the recitation would be half the prescription. This is a therapeutic and ritual use for an eye complaint, not a cultivation claim, and no deity attribution is made here beyond what the line prints. The identification of verdigris with malachite is modern. [USER: verify]

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: a lower-grade drug (下品).* Qing Lang Gan stands under "Jades and Stones: Inferior Class" (Yang line 1240). Yang's preface says the inferior-class medicinals "mainly treat disease", "are usually toxic and cannot be taken for a long time", and are the ones to rely on to "eliminate cold and heat and evil qi, break accumulations and gatherings, and cure disease" (lines 391-395). The monograph prints no longevity or *qing shen* line. The status is the lowest of the *san pin*: a drug for a defined complaint and a short course, not one for nourishing life. The identification (malachite, azurite or turquoise) is uncertain in Yang's own notes.

**Practitioner's note.** A lower-grade drug in the Chinese scheme and, in the Egyptian text, an eye paint and a few pills with a spell. Nothing here supports taking it for cultivation, and the Chinese grading is itself the warning: a defined complaint, a short course and then stop. The Egyptian side offers a different point, that the recitation belonged to the prescription. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Copper salts are poisonous by mouth and irritant to the eye, so neither the Egyptian pills nor the eye paint should be copied. Powdered mineral preparations are unstandardised and may carry arsenic, lead or other metals. Because the identification is uncertain, a text's malachite, verdigris, azurite and turquoise cannot be treated as the same substance. Purchase of mineral medicines is regulated in many places. Ritual recitation is recorded as ritual.
