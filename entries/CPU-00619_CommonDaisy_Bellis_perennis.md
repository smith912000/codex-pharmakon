# Common Daisy — Bruisewort / Day's Eye

## *Bellis perennis* L. | Family: Asteraceae (Compositae)

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Cool / Dry — bitter-astringent, resolving, consolidating; a surface and interstitium herb, not a depth herb

**Training Phase:** Repair & Consolidation — the herb of the aftermath, not the event

---

### Origin & Tradition

There is no plant in this codex more thoroughly overlooked and more thoroughly *present*. *Bellis perennis* grows in virtually every mown lawn in temperate Europe, is trodden on daily by people who would pay for arnica gel, and has been the poor man's version of exactly that gel for at least five centuries.

The two names carry the entire tradition between them.

**"Day's eye."** Old English *dæges ēage* → *daisy*. The name is a description of a mechanism: the capitulum **opens at dawn and closes at dusk**, and closes again in rain. The plant is a functioning clock and a functioning barometer, and every child who has ever watched one knows this. The genus name *Bellis* is usually derived from Latin *bellus*, "pretty," and *perennis* means "everlasting" — a reference to the plant's near-continuous flowering, which in mild maritime climates means some months with no gap at all.

**"Bruisewort."** This is the medicine. The English folk name is blunt and specific: this is the herb you put on a bruise. Not a fever, not a cough — a **contusion**.

**European Domestic Materia Medica (DMM)**

The attested folk use is remarkably narrow and remarkably consistent across the European record: **bruises, sprains, contusions, blows, and slow-healing wounds.** The classical English name *bruisewort* is shared with comfrey and soapwort — all three are consolidating, tissue-knitting herbs in the folk mind, and the shared name is itself an argument about what the folk tradition thought was happening.

The preparation is domestic in the truest sense: the whole fresh flowering plant is bruised and applied directly as a poultice, or macerated in oil for an ointment, or taken as an infusion of the flowers. No specialist equipment, no rare ingredient, no expense — which is precisely why it is called the **poor man's arnica**. *Arnica montana* is a mountain plant, was always scarce, and was always dearer. *Bellis* is underfoot. The two are used for the same indication, in the same way, by the same people, with the *Bellis* reached for when the arnica is not there.

A secondary and weaker DMM attestation covers **infusion of flowers for coughs and as a mild spring tonic / "blood purifier,"** and the young leaves are eaten in salads. **Contemporary / Homeopathic Materia Medica (CM)**

*Bellis perennis* has a substantial life in homeopathic practice, where it is a **major trauma remedy** — specifically indicated for deep tissue injury, post-surgical bruising, and injury to "muscles and deep tissue," positioned as arnica's successor when arnica's picture is exhausted. It is worth stating precisely what this does and does not mean for this codex.

It means the *indication* tradition is continuous and coherent: three independent strands — English folk naming, European domestic practice, and homeopathic materia medica — all point at the same target, trauma to soft tissue. That convergence is real data about what people observed.

It does **not** mean the homeopathic *doses* carry any pharmacological weight. At homeopathic dilution there is no *Bellis perennis* left in the preparation. This codex records the indication and rejects the dilution.

The published research here needs flagging carefully. One in-vitro study of a commercial **homeopathic-spagyric remedy** containing *Equisetum arvense* and *Bellis perennis* among other constituents, tested on human fetal osteoblastic (hFOB 1.19) cells at 10% down to 0.00001%, found no reduction in cell number, viability, proliferation or migration, and a tendency to induce inflammatory genes and growth factors along with osseous regeneration genes (ALP, Col1, IL-1α, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, osteocalcin, osteonectin, RUNX2, TGF, VEGFA) ([DOI: 10.1159/000535845](https://doi.org/10.1159/000535845), PubMed). ⚠️ This is a **multi-ingredient commercial product, not *Bellis perennis***. Nothing in it can be attributed to the daisy. It is cited here because it exists and will otherwise be cited badly by someone else.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

The vulnerary doctrine is: *this herb closes what has been opened, and disperses what has been forced into the wrong place.* A bruise, in the folk reading, is blood in the interstitium — blood that has left the vessel and is sitting in tissue where it does not belong. Bruisewort is supposed to move it back out and knit the disturbed tissue.

This maps onto real chemistry more cleanly than most folk doctrines, and it is worth being precise about how.

**Saponins are surfactants.** That is not a metaphor — the *Bellis* triterpene saponins are amphipathic molecules that lower surface tension and interact with membrane cholesterol. A tradition that mashes a saponin-rich plant onto a bruise is, whether it knew it or not, applying a class of compound with genuine membrane activity, mild local irritant/rubefacient potential, and — at low concentration — documented effects on cell behaviour. The "disperses stagnant blood" doctrine and the "lowers surface tension, permeabilises membranes, promotes local circulation" chemistry are pointing in a compatible direction. The doctrine earns partial credit.

**The astringency doctrine also earns partial credit.** *Bellis* is bitter and mildly astringent, and the flavonoid/tannin fraction supports the "consolidating, closing" reading in the same weak sense that all polyphenol-astringent herbs support it: protein precipitation at a raw surface does form a physical film, and that film is not nothing.

Now the **deflation**, and it matters:

The *Bellis*-as-arnica equation is a **folk substitution driven by availability, not by demonstrated equivalence.** They are both Asteraceae, both saponin- and lactone-bearing, both used for bruises — and that is where the evidence for the equation stops. Arnica's activity is standardly attributed to its sesquiterpene lactones (helenalin and derivatives); there is no basis in the retrieved evidence for asserting that *Bellis* contains helenalin, or that its saponins do what helenalin does. **The tradition substituted the cheap plant for the dear one because it was there.** That is an entirely rational thing for a poor person to do and an entirely irrational basis for a pharmacological claim.

Similarly, the "day's eye" nyctinasty is a **beautiful, real, and medicinally irrelevant** fact. Circadian leaf and flower movement is common across Fabaceae and Asteraceae and has no bearing on what the plant does in a poultice. The codex records it because it is the plant's name, not because it is a doctrine.

### Active Components

*Bellis perennis* is chemically rich and — unusually for a lawn weed — well characterised. A 2023 review consolidated **over 310 individual components, compound classes, and bioactivities with plant-organ-specific origin** ([DOI: 10.3390/molecules28237716](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28237716), PubMed). Principal classes:

- **Acylated oleanane-type triterpene saponin bisdesmosides — the "perennisaponins."** The defining chemistry. Perennisaponins A–M were previously described; perennisaponins N–T (seven further compounds) were newly isolated from a methanol extract of the flowers ([DOI: 10.1007/s11418-016-0998-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11418-016-0998-9), PubMed). This is a substantial, structurally elaborate, genuinely *Bellis*-specific saponin family — the plant is not simply "a saponin herb," it has its own named series.
- **Flavonoids** — apigenin glycosides and quercitrin, identified and quantified by HPLC ([DOI: 10.3390/molecules23102605](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules23102605), PubMed).
- **Hydroxycinnamic acids** — chlorogenic acid, same source.
- **Polyphenols generally** — daisy petals carry sufficient total phenolic content to act as a reducing agent in the aqueous green synthesis of silver nanoparticles (5–35 nm, spherical, monodisperse), with AgNP yield concordant with total phenolic content across the plants tested ([DOI: 10.3390/nano13061010](https://doi.org/10.3390/nano13061010), PubMed). An odd citation for a materia medica, but it is a clean quantitative demonstration that the petals are phenol-rich.
- **Polyacetylenes** — reported for the species in the review literature; ⚠️ **no polyacetylene-specific pharmacology for *B. perennis* was retrieved in this run.** Polyacetylenes are a well-known Asteraceae class and are relevant to the contact-dermatitis warning below, but the codex will not assert a specific *Bellis* polyacetylene activity it cannot source.
- **Essential oil** — present; ⚠️ note that a PubMed search for *Bellis perennis* essential oil returns a paper whose oil is in fact **Japanese mint**, not daisy ([DOI: 10.1080/03601234.2024.2396730](https://doi.org/10.1080/03601234.2024.2396730)). Do not cite it for this plant. Recorded here as a trap for the next researcher.

**Organ specificity matters.** The perennisaponins were isolated from **flowers**. The insulin-mimetic work used whole-plant extracts. The folk poultice uses the whole flowering plant. Do not assume these are interchangeable.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

*Bellis* sits in **Repair & Consolidation** and its Kenetic use case is almost embarrassingly literal: it is for the body after the body has been hit.

Its value to a practitioner is **topical and structural**, not psychoactive, not perceptual, not oneirogenic. Nothing in the classical record or the retrieved evidence suggests *Bellis perennis* does anything to consciousness. Any claim that it does would be an invention, and this codex does not make it.

Where it earns its place is in the unglamorous physical maintenance that sustained practice actually requires — the bruise from a fall, the strained muscle, the slow-healing graze, the deep soft-tissue soreness that accumulates in any embodied discipline. It is the herb you already have, growing in the grass you sit on. Its Kenetic significance is that a practitioner who cannot look after a bruise has no business attempting anything harder, and the tradition put the remedy for that directly underfoot.

The **cultivator's reading** is the more interesting one. *Bellis* is the test case for whether a practitioner can see a medicine that has not been made scarce or expensive. Arnica is dear, mountain-grown, romantic, and scarce; *Bellis* is free, ubiquitous, and mowed. The folk tradition used the free one. If the practice cannot recognise the free one, the practice is buying a story, not a plant.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Cultivator's note.** *Bellis perennis* is a perennial rosette-forming lawn plant, hardy to roughly USDA zone 4, self-seeding aggressively, and effectively impossible to kill by mowing — mowing selects *for* it, which is why lawns fill with it. It is wind-dispersed, and a common-garden experiment across five wind-dispersed species including *B. perennis* found that **plants in favourable hydrological conditions produce more seed and disperse it further, because they grow taller** ([DOI: 10.1002/ece3.8305](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.8305), PubMed). For the cultivator this is a direct handle: water it and it spreads; stress it and it stays put and stays small. If you want a contained patch, keep it dry.

Harvest **flowering heads in full sun and full opening** — the nyctinastic closing means a daisy picked at dusk or in rain is a different physical object, and while the codex has no evidence that this changes the chemistry, it certainly changes the yield of the part you want. Harvest across the long flowering season; *perennis* is not idle.

### Scientific Correlation

Graded honestly, and the headline finding is a gap.

**Grade: D (no human evidence) — wound healing and bruising, i.e. the entire tradition.** This is the central fact of the entry and it must not be buried. **No human clinical trial of *Bellis perennis* for wounds, bruises, contusions, or any other indication was retrieved in this run.** The 2023 comprehensive review lists wound healing among the reported activities but its explicit critical conclusion is that the field is methodologically stalled: the work "often sticks at the level of the crude extract or a fraction, but not at a single purified and tested compound," is "hampered by a missing comprehensive bioactivity workflow," and generally cannot correlate a biological effect with a specific compound ([DOI: 10.3390/molecules28237716](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28237716)). That is a review author's polite way of saying the evidence base does not resolve. The codex says it less politely: **the best-attested folk use of this plant is the least tested.** Five hundred years of bruisewort, no trial.

**Grade: B (strong, clean, in-vitro, but for an unrelated indication) — antiproliferative activity.** The perennisaponins — the newly isolated N–T plus the previously reported A–M, twenty compounds in total — showed antiproliferative activity against human digestive tract carcinoma HSC-2, HSC-4 and MKN-45 cells. **Perennisaponin O** was the standout, with IC50 of 11.2, 14.3 and 6.9 µM respectively, and its mechanism against HSC-2 was shown to involve apoptotic cell death ([DOI: 10.1007/s11418-016-0998-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11418-016-0998-9)). This is good, specific, compound-level work — exactly what the review says is usually missing. It is also **entirely in vitro, in cancer cell lines, at micromolar concentrations, and has nothing whatever to do with bruises.** Note also the uncomfortable implication: these saponins are *cytotoxic to human cells*. A vulnerary that kills cells in a dish is not a contradiction (dose and route differ enormously) but it is not the confirmation a casual reader will want to take from it.

**Grade: C (preclinical, in ovo, genuinely interesting, and completely outside the tradition) — insulin-mimetic activity.** *B. perennis* extracts were identified in a fluorescence-microscopy screen as efficient inducers of **GLUT4 translocation in the absence of insulin** in a CHO-cell system, and reduced blood glucose levels in chick embryos (in ovo), confirming activity in a living organism; HPLC identified apigenin glycosides, quercitrin and chlorogenic acid as candidate contributors ([DOI: 10.3390/molecules23102605](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules23102605)). This is the most interesting *Bellis* finding retrieved and it is a genuine surprise: **no folk tradition retrieved in this run indicates daisy for anything diabetes-adjacent.** Where the doctrine and the chemistry agree (wounds), there is no evidence; where there is evidence (glucose transport), there is no doctrine. That inversion is the honest summary of this plant.

**Grade: C− (indirect, wrong test article) — the osteoblast/regeneration work.** The hFOB 1.19 study induced osteogenic and growth-factor gene expression ([DOI: 10.1159/000535845](https://doi.org/10.1159/000535845)) but tested a **multi-herb homeopathic-spagyric commercial product**, not *Bellis*. Unattributable. Listed to prevent misattribution, not to support anything.

**Grade: B (methodological, not medical) — phenolic content.** Petal phenolics reduce silver salts to monodisperse AgNPs with yield tracking total phenolic content ([DOI: 10.3390/nano13061010](https://doi.org/10.3390/nano13061010)). Confirms the petals are phenol-rich. Says nothing about therapeutic effect.

**Net verdict.** A chemically well-characterised plant (310+ characterised components, its own named saponin series) with a coherent 500-year folk indication and **not one human trial of that indication**. The antiproliferative data is real and irrelevant to the tradition; the insulin-mimetic data is real and *unpredicted* by the tradition. This is a plant where the science and the doctrine have essentially never met. It is not a debunking — it is an absence, and the absence is the finding.

### Contraindications

- ⚠️ **Asteraceae contact dermatitis — the primary risk, and it is the risk that matters most because the primary use is topical.** The Compositae are among the most significant causes of plant contact allergy, and sensitisation is typically cross-reactive across the family — a person reactive to arnica, chamomile, feverfew, yarrow, ragweed, chrysanthemum, tansy or *Tanacetum* should be assumed reactive to *Bellis perennis* until proven otherwise. Sesquiterpene lactones are the classic culprit class and **polyacetylenes are also implicated in Asteraceae sensitisation**. This is not a theoretical hazard: the tradition's own method — crushed fresh plant, bound onto damaged skin, left in prolonged occlusive contact — is close to the optimal protocol for inducing contact sensitisation. **Patch-test on intact skin before any use on a bruise, and never on broken skin first.**
- **Saponin cytotoxicity.** Perennisaponin O and its relatives are demonstrably cytotoxic to human cell lines in the low-micromolar range ([DOI: 10.1007/s11418-016-0998-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11418-016-0998-9)). This is a caution against concentrated extracts and against internal use of anything stronger than a traditional infusion, not against a poultice.
- **Broken skin / open wounds.** The folk record includes "slow-healing wounds," but combining a saponin-rich, potentially sensitising, unsterile fresh poultice with an open wound is a poor idea and the codex does not endorse it. Bruises are intact skin. Keep it there.
- **Pregnancy and lactation.** ⚠️ **No safety data of any kind was retrieved in this run.** Some traditional sources treat *Bellis* as a uterine-active or emmenagogue-adjacent herb. [USER: verify — the codex has insufficient basis to either endorse or refute this, and defaults to avoidance.]
- **Internal use generally.** No established dose, no human safety data, no monograph. The salad-leaf use establishes that small quantities of fresh leaf are tolerated as food. It establishes nothing about a therapeutic infusion.
- **Diabetes medication — a real theoretical interaction.** *Bellis* extract induces GLUT4 translocation without insulin and lowers blood glucose in ovo ([DOI: 10.3390/molecules23102605](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules23102605)). Anyone on insulin or an oral hypoglycaemic taking daisy extract in quantity has a plausible additive-hypoglycaemia mechanism. Untested in humans, mechanistically credible, worth naming.
- **Do not treat as interchangeable with arnica.** The folk substitution was economic. It is not a pharmacological equivalence and this codex does not license it.
- **Misidentification.** *Bellis perennis* is distinctive but shares the "daisy" name with *Leucanthemum vulgare* (ox-eye daisy), *Chrysanthemum* spp., and others. Confirm the basal rosette, the solitary leafless scape, and the small (<3 cm) capitulum.
