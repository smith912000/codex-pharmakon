# Rice-paper plant pith — 通草 (Tong Cao); also Fu Zhi, Ding Weng

## *The Shennong drug 通草 *tong cao*: by Sun Xingyan's own notes (Guo Pu) a tall southern plant with a white pith, now identified as *Tetrapanax papyrifer* (Hook.) K.Koch, the rice-paper plant (medium confidence); Yang 1998 says that before the Song dynasty the name meant the woody climber now called *mu tong*, *Akebia** | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Tong cao* is named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk. It is a single-text entry. The placeholder row it replaces was named "Caulis Akebiae" from Yang's Latin; that name is kept as one of the two readings below.

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** Sun Xingyan's edition files 通草 in the middle classic (卷二 中经; `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 1909, 味 paragraph; name line 1905, with a garbled variant note). The Shennong text proper: "acrid, balanced" (味辛，平). It "mainly" "removes evil worms; clears cold and heat of the spleen and stomach; frees and opens the nine orifices, the blood vessels and the joints; makes a person not forget" (主去恶虫，除脾胃寒热，通利九窍、血脉、关节，令人不忘). "One name: Fu Zhi" 附支. "It grows in mountains and valleys" (生山谷). No toxicity statement and no 久服 clause.

*Wu Pu layer* (3rd c.; line 1911). Aliases 丁翁, 附支. Wu Pu's report from the older authorities: "Shennong, Huangdi: acrid; Leigong: bitter." Habitat Shicheng valleys; the leaves are "blue-green and trailing/spreading" (叶菁蔓延); "it stops sweat" (止汗); gathered from the first month.

*Mingyi bielu layer* (line 1913). Alias 丁翁. Habitat: Shicheng and Shanyang. The branches (枝) are gathered in the first month and dried in the shade.

*Sun's note* (案, line 1915) gathers glosses:
- *Guangya*: *fu zhi* is a plant (the glyph is lost in the e-text).
- *Zhongshan jing*: on Mount Sheng "its plants are mostly *kou tuo*." Guo Pu on *kou tuo*: "it grows in the south, about a *zhang* tall, like a lotus leaf, and the stem has a pith (瓤) that is quite white; the people of Lingling plant it and water it daily, to make it a tree."
- *Erya*: *li nan*, *huo tuo*; Guo Pu's note is the same; and *yi shang*, *huo tuo*, which Guo Pu says "is *li nan*."
- *Fanzi jiran*: a plant (glyph lost) "comes from the Sanfu."

**Two readings (relation: variety).**

**Reading A: the pith plant (Sun's notes).** Guo Pu's description (tall, large lotus-like leaves, white pith in the stem) is the basis for the identification as *Tetrapanax papyrifer*, whose pith is the source of rice paper. This is the identification used for the entry name. Guo Pu does not say that the plant is *tong cao* in so many words; the link is made by Sun's gathering of the glosses under this entry. Medium confidence.

**Reading B: the climber (Yang 1998).** Yang (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt` line 2802) renders "Tong Cao (Caulis Akebiae) is acrid and balanced... It mainly removes malign worms, eliminates cold and heat of the spleen and stomach, and disinhibits and frees the nine orifices, blood vessels, and joints. It may improve memory. Its other name is Fu Zhi (Appending Branch)." His footnote 164 (line 2818) says: "before the Song dynasty, what is now called Mu Tong was called Tong Cao and vice versa." The rest of that footnote is Yang's own commentary and quotes a Song author (Yang Shiying), not the classic; it speaks of freeing the heart orifices and promoting lactation, which the Shennong text does not say.

*Synthesis of the two readings.* The text's wording, its Wu Pu and Mingyi aliases, and its habitat do not fix the plant. If Yang's note is right, the Shennong *tong cao* is the *mu tong* of modern practice (*Akebia*) and the modern *tong cao* (the pith) is a later reassignment of the name. The two plants belong to different families and to different parts (stem or branch against pith). The Mingyi layer says the branches (枝) are gathered, which fits a stem drug and does not say pith. The sources retrieved do not decide; both are listed and the entry name follows Sun's notes.

### Varieties

**Rice-paper plant pith, *Tetrapanax papyrifer*** (SBJ; Sun's notes, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 1915, Guo Pu on *kou tuo*). The reading above that rests on Guo Pu's description of a tall plant with a white pith.

**Mu tong / Akebia stem, "Caulis Akebiae"** (SBJ; Yang 1998, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt` lines 2802 and 2818). The reading above that rests on Yang's note that the early name *tong cao* was the *mu tong* of today. Yang gives no species. This reading is not on the spine as its own row and its name is not given an entry here.

*Synthesis of the varieties.* Sun's notes separate the plants by a description (tall, white-pithed); Yang separates them by a change of name across the Song dynasty. Neither text ranks one first, and the Shennong paragraph itself does not separate them. They are listed side by side, not equated.

### Active Components

The pith of *Tetrapanax papyrifer* is mostly a light cellular tissue; the Akebia stem is a different plant material. Neither is analysed in the sources retrieved, and no constituent is asserted. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

This is a historical record of one text, not a recommended practice.

- **What the text says it is for.** Removing "evil worms," cold and heat of the spleen and stomach, and freeing and opening the nine orifices, vessels and joints.
- **What the text says it does to the person.** "Makes a person not forget" (令人不忘); Yang: "may improve memory." This is a stated effect of the drug as given in the paragraph; there is no 久服 clause.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no preparation, no toxicity statement.

Because the identity is open, nothing here supports taking either plant for any complaint. No Kenetica protocol is derived from this entry.

### Preparation & Dosing

The Shennong text gives no preparation and no dose. The Mingyi layer says the branches are gathered in the first month and dried in the shade (line 1913). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

*Tetrapanax papyrifer* pith is used to make rice paper and, in Chinese medicine, is the crude drug *tongcao*; *Akebia* stem is the crude drug *mutong*. Whether either relieves any condition named in the Shennong text has not been tested in sources retrieved for this entry. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SBJ (Sun 1799).** Acrid, balanced (味辛，平; line 1909). Wu Pu's report: Shennong and Huangdi, acrid; Leigong, bitter (line 1911).
- **SBJ (Yang 1998).** Acrid and balanced (line 2802).
- No toxicity statement and no organ assignment is given in the classic.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(b) Framed.* The entry stands in the middle classic, which Sun's edition (line 1677) defines as "ministers, mainly nourishing the nature to correspond with the human; toxic or nontoxic, to be judged as fitting," for "one who wishes to check disease and replenish the weak." The entry has no 久服 clause and no life-nourishing promise; its one effect on the person is that it "makes a person not forget." No *san pin* cultivation status beyond the middle grade is attested for this drug.

**Practitioner's note.** A middle-grade drug with an unsettled identity and no stated duration. There is nothing here for a cultivator to do with it beyond reading the record; the one effect named on the person, memory, is not a basis for taking either plant.

### Contraindications

- **Identity.** The name has been used for two different plants (see Varieties). Do not take either on the strength of the name. [USER: verify]
- **Dose and form unknown.** The text gives none.
- **Pregnancy and lactation.** No statement in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **"Worms" and digestive complaints.** These are the classic's terms; they need medical assessment.
