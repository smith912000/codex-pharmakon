# Brown bear — "snow bear (Ursus arctos)" (Clark 1995); "Tibetan brown bear" (Ursus arctos, Men-Tsee-Khang 2011); the Tibetan name is not paired in the sources on disk
## *The bear that Clark's rendering of the Explanatory Tantra calls the "snow bear (Ursus arctos)" and the Men-Tsee-Khang translation the "Tibetan brown bear", named in the Tantra's list of carnivorous wild animals; no medicinal product of it is named in the lines read* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The brown bear is named in the Explanatory Tantra (*bshad rgyud*) in the classification of meats, and nowhere else in the lines read. The entry's locus in the project name list is `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` line 410, "Ursus arctos", from the Men-Tsee-Khang "Glossary on Animals (line 7017)", with the note that the Tibetan name is not paired because the OCR splits the glossary columns. The glossary line reads "Tibetan Brown bear Ursus arctos" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 7017), directly above "Black Bear — Ursus thibetanus" (lines 7019, 7021).

*The meat classification.* Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 672) divides meats into nine sections, and section (e) reads: "Carnivorous animals include the tiger, leopard, bear (Selenarctos thibetanus), snow bear (Ursus arctos), snow leopard (Panthera pardus), wolf, lynx, fox, hill dog and weasel." Men-Tsee-Khang (line 2912) has: "The tiger, leopard, black bear, Tibetan brown bear, snow leopard, wolf, lynx, fox, dhole (Tibetan wild-dog) and monkey are the group of canivorous [sic] wild animals" (the OCR spelling is "canivorous", line 2913). So the two translations agree that the Tantra lists two bears among the carnivorous animals, one of which is the animal of the Asian black bear entry (this Codex) and the other this one. The two translations differ in the last members of the group (Clark: hill dog and weasel; Men-Tsee-Khang: dhole and monkey).

*What the Tantra says of the group.* The Tantra gives the qualities of meat by group, not by animal. All meats in the groups are "sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste" (Clark line 673; Men-Tsee-Khang line 2923). The Men-Tsee-Khang text adds (line 2931) that "the meat of birds, carnivorous animals and birds of prey that feed on raw meat has rough, light and sharp qualities, and therefore generates digestive heat, destroys tumors, increases weight and eliminates all cold disorders." Clark's parallel sentence (line 673) names only the birds of prey, which "live on raw flesh" and whose flesh "has coarse, light and sharp powers which increase the digestive heat, disperse tumors, increase flesh and eliminate all cold disorders". The two translations therefore differ on whether the carnivorous mammals share that sentence. [USER: verify]

*Bear bile.* The list of animal medicines names "dom.mkhris: Bear Bile", with actions that constrict the mouths of the channels, halt necrosis, grow new flesh and help the eyes (Clark lines 890-891), but it does not name the species. The project list row (`_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 100) assigns it to the Asian black bear, *Selenarctos thibetanus*, and the material has its own spine row (CPU-00182). Nothing in the lines read assigns bile to the brown bear, and nothing is claimed for it here. [USER: verify]

*What the texts do not say.* In the lines read, the brown bear has no medicine of its own: no flesh, fat, skin or bile is named for it, and the Tibetan name for it is not given. The only sourced facts are its presence in the carnivore group and its Latin name.

*Other texts on disk, not folded in.* The bear (*Riksha*) is named in the Sushruta Samhita in the group of cave-dwelling mammals (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` lines 534-536), without a species, and Dioscorides names bears' grease and lung in chapters 2-21, 2-40 and 2-87. These are reported to the editor under the Asian black bear entry, and not applied here, because neither text names a brown bear.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, the flesh of a large carnivorous mammal is muscle protein and fat, and it can carry parasites; the brown bear is an omnivore. Nothing links any constituent to any action in the Tantra.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts list the brown bear among the carnivorous wild animals and give its meat no medicine of its own. The Codex does not recommend the use of any part of a bear. The brown bear is protected in law in many countries, its populations in parts of Asia are small, and handling a wild bear carries real danger. The entry exists to record that the Tantra names the animal.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources retrieved give no preparation of any part of the brown bear. The group sentence describes its meat as food and says nothing of preparation. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. The brown bear, *Ursus arctos*, is a large omnivorous bear found across the northern hemisphere. Bear meat can carry the parasite that causes trichinosis if eaten undercooked. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** No quality is printed for the brown bear by name. By membership of the carnivorous group (Clark line 672; Men-Tsee-Khang line 2912), the meat is sweet in taste and post-digestive taste (Clark line 673; Men-Tsee-Khang line 2923), and in the Men-Tsee-Khang rendering it has "rough, light and sharp qualities" (line 2931); Clark's parallel sentence names birds of prey only. These apply by group membership, which is an inference from the classification and not a sentence about the bear.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* The brown bear is named only as a member of the carnivorous animals in the meat classification (Clark line 672), and neither translation read gives it a medicine or a cultivation framing. The wider frame is the tradition's own: the three humors are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration (Clark line 51), these are "the three causes" of wind, bile and phlegm disorders (line 147), and the chapter on routine behaviour calls for refraining from the ten negative actions, the first being "taking life" (line 616). Those lines say nothing about the bear. The rejuvenation passage in chapter 23 (lines 1262-1277) does not name it, and no *bcud len* use was found. The Tibetan tradition has no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice, and none is asserted here.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a name in a classification of meats, with no medicine, no preparation and no dose, and the Tantra's own first precept is to refrain from taking life. The honest use of the entry is as a record of what the text says. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Danger and law:** wild brown bears are dangerous and protected in many countries; the Codex does not recommend taking or handling any part of one.
- **Hygiene and infection:** bear meat can carry the parasite that causes trichinosis; any wild-animal meat can carry bacteria and parasites.
- **Welfare and ethics:** the Tibetan text itself lists "taking life" first among the ten negative actions (Clark line 616).
- **Identity:** Clark prints "snow bear (Ursus arctos)" and the Men-Tsee-Khang text "Tibetan brown bear"; no medicinal product is assigned to this species in the lines read.
