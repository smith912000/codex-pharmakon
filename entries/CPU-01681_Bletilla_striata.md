# Bletilla striata — Bai Ji 白及 (herb), also Can Gen "Sweet Root" and Lian Ji Cao "Entangled Weed" (Shennong Bencao Jing)

## *Bletilla striata, as the pharmaceutical Latin "Rhizoma Bletillae Striatae" that Yang 1998 prints for the entry "Bai Ji"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Shennong text (SBJ).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The entry is in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`). The Latin name is the translator's pharmaceutical Latin and was matched to the binomial by stem in the extraction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, row 181). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The characters 白及 come from the Codex's own list of Shennong names (`_sources/shennong.csv`, line 281), which prints them alone, and not from Yang's English text. [USER: verify]

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *Bai Ji (Rhizoma Bletillae Striatae), PDF page 96.* The page marker `[[p96]]` is at line 3573. The entry runs from line 3578 to line 3583. It is the first complete entry on the page, after three lines (3575 to 3577) that close an entry begun on the previous page. The entry reads:

"Bai Ji (Rhizoma Bletillae Striatae)220 is bitter and balanced. It mainly treats welling abscesses and swellings, malign sores, putrid flat abscesses, damaged yin,221 dead muscles, evil qi in the stomach, bandit wind, ghost stroke, and disablement of slackening and inability to contract. Its other name is Can Gen (Sweet Root). Another name is Lian Ji Cao (Entangled Weed). It grows in rivers and valleys."

Yang's note 220 (lines 3594 to 3599) is the translator's modern commentary and not part of the classic. It says: "Currently, Bletilla has three uses. One is to treat damaged lungs causing lung bleeding. This typically manifests as coughing or hacking up of blood. Its second indication is bone fracture. Lastly, it is prescribed to treat burns, cracking of the skin, welling and flat abscesses, scrofulas, and hemorrhoids. However, in folk prescriptions, it is used to stop nosebleeding. For this purpose, it is applied externally, and it proves quite effective." Of these uses the entry itself shares only welling and flat abscesses; lung bleeding, bone fracture, burns and nosebleed are not in the entry. Note 221 (line 3600) says: "Damaged yin means impotence in males and prolapse of the uterus in females." It is the translator's gloss of a term the entry uses.

*The class heading.* Yang's table of contents prints "Herbs: Inferior Class" (line 729). On the page of this entry no heading is printed under the page marker: the page opens (line 3574) with the running title "The Divine Farmer's Materia Medica Classic". The heading "Herbs: Inferior Class" is printed at the top of the page before (line 3534, under `[[p95]]`) and at the top of the page after (line 3610, under `[[p97]]`). The entry therefore stands under the inferior-class heading by position between two repeated page-top headings, and not directly beneath one. The preface (lines 391 to 395) says of the inferior class: "There are 125 inferior class medicinals which are used as assistants and envoys. They mainly treat disease and correspond to earth. They are usually toxic and cannot be taken for a long time." Yang's commentary (lines 362 to 365) says that inferior-class medicinals "specifically treat disease" and "are usually at least slightly toxic". The entry itself does not say "toxic" and prints no warning.

*Neighbours.* The entry that follows, Cao Hao (*Herba Artemisiae Apiacae*, line 3584), is a different drug with its own entry.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Bletilla striata* is an orchid (Orchidaceae) that grows from a tuber-like pseudobulb, and that the pseudobulbs of *Bletilla* are generally known to be rich in a mucilaginous polysaccharide. Nothing here says what the Shennong drug contained, and the Latin name is the translator's. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Shennong text records, in its own terms, a bitter and balanced drug for welling abscesses and swellings, malign sores, putrid flat abscesses, damaged yin, dead muscles, evil qi in the stomach, bandit wind, ghost stroke and disablement of slackening and inability to contract. These are the text's claims and not modern diagnoses, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The entry gives no preparation, no part of the plant, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. The translator's Latin name says "Rhizoma" (rhizome), and the Codex takes that as the translator's, not as the classic's. Note 220 says that in folk prescriptions it is "applied externally" to stop nosebleeding, which is the translator's remark on present-day practice. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SBJ:** "bitter and balanced" (line 3578). No toxicity is printed in the entry, and no meridian or other system of later Chinese medicine is printed in it. The preface describes the inferior class as usually toxic (line 393), and that is a statement about the class and not about this entry.

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: the inferior class (下品), by position between repeated page-top headings.* The class is assigned by the printed heading "Herbs: Inferior Class", at line 3534 (`[[p95]]`) and again at line 3610 (`[[p97]]`), and the entry stands between them on page 96, with no heading of its own. It is a printed class and not a promise of cultivation use. The preface (lines 391 to 395) gives the inferior class a purpose: it mainly treats disease, it is usually toxic and it "cannot be taken for a long time". The Codex reads the class as the classic's grade for a drug used against a complaint and then stopped, and not as a *yang sheng* ("nourishing life") drug.

The entry carries no sentence on protracted taking, no mention of making the body light and no mention of prolonging life. It asserts no *jing*, *shen* or *neidan* action, and none is added here.

**Practitioner's note.** An inferior-class entry in the Chinese scheme, listed for abscesses, sores and wasted tissue, with no preparation and no dose. The class warns against long use. Nothing here supports taking this plant as a cultivation practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The Shennong entry prints no warning, and its silence is not a statement of safety; the preface says of the inferior class that it is "usually toxic and cannot be taken for a long time". The identity rests on a translator's Latin name, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Abscesses, spreading sores and bleeding need medical care.
